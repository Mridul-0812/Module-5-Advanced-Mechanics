13. Orbital Velocity ($v$) & Orbital Period ($T$)

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 4 (Part 1)

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Core Mechanics

Inquiry Question 4: How does Kepler's laws of planetary motion describe the movements of natural and artificial satellites?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus (calculus-free), students must master the mathematical derivation and physical concepts of orbital motion:

  • Derive the orbital velocity equation $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$ by equating centripetal force ($F_c = \frac{m v^2}{r}$) to the universal gravitational force ($F_g = \frac{G M m}{r^2}$).
  • Derive the orbital period equation $T = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{GM}}$ by linking linear orbital velocity to circular circumference ($v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$).
  • Analyse the physical consequences of satellite mass independence (i.e., a $1\text{ kg}$ CubeSat and a $400,000\text{ kg}$ space station at the same altitude travel at the exact same orbital speed).
  • Evaluate how orbital radius $r = R + h$ dictates both orbital speed ($v \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}$) and period ($T \propto r^{3/2}$).

Part 1: The Intuitive Bridge — Falling Around the Earth

Beginner Concept: Newton's Thought Experiment (Newton's Cannon)

How does a satellite stay in orbit without engines running continuously? Why doesn't gravity pull it straight down to the ground?

Isaac Newton imagined a powerful cannon on top of a tall mountain above Earth's atmosphere:

  1. Slow Shot: The cannonball travels horizontally, but gravity pulls it down, hitting Earth a few hundred metres away.
  2. Faster Shot: It travels further before hitting the ground.
  3. Orbital Speed Shot: The cannonball is fired so fast horizontally ($\sim 7.9\text{ km/s}$) that as gravity pulls it downward, the curvature of the Earth falls away beneath it at the exact same rate! The cannonball is in continuous free-fall, yet it never hits the ground. It is in stable orbit!

Figure 1: Vector dynamics of stable circular orbit. Gravity ($F_g$) acts as the sole centripetal force ($F_c$) pulling perpendicular to tangential orbital velocity ($v$).

Part 2: Step-by-Step Core Derivations ($F_c = F_g$)

1. Derivation 1: Orbital Speed ($v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$)

For any object of mass $m$ moving in a stable circular orbit of radius $r$ around a central mass $M$:

Step-by-Step Derivation of Orbital Velocity

Step 1: State the physical condition for stable circular orbit.

The necessary centripetal force ($F_c$) required to keep the satellite turning in a circle is provided entirely by the universal gravitational attraction force ($F_g$):

$$F_c = F_g$$

Step 2: Substitute the formulas for centripetal force and gravitational force.

$$\frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{G M m}{r^2}$$

Where:

  • $m$: Mass of the orbiting satellite ($\text{kg}$).
  • $M$: Mass of the central body being orbited ($\text{kg}$).
  • $v$: Orbital velocity of the satellite ($\text{m/s}$).
  • $r$: Center-to-center orbital radius ($\text{m}$).
  • $G$: Universal Gravitational Constant ($6.674 \times 10^{-11} \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$).

Step 3: Cancel the satellite mass $m$ from both sides.

$$\frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{G M}{r^2}$$

Step 4: Multiply both sides by $r$ to isolate $v^2$.

$$v^2 = \frac{G M}{r}$$

Step 5: Take the square root of both sides.

Orbital Velocity Formula

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}$$

CRITICAL INSIGHT: Satellite Mass Independence

Notice that satellite mass $m$ canceled out completely during Step 3! This proves that orbital speed depends ONLY on the central mass $M$ and orbital radius $r$. A feather, a astronaut, and a heavy space station placed at the exact same altitude will orbit at the exact same speed!

2. Derivation 2: Orbital Period ($T = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{GM}}$)

The orbital period ($T$) is the time required for a satellite to complete one full revolution around its orbit.

Step-by-Step Derivation of Orbital Period

Step 1: Express linear circular velocity in terms of circumference ($2\pi r$) and period ($T$).

$$v = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}} = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$$

Step 2: Square both sides of this velocity equation.

$$v^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2}$$

Step 3: Equate this to our orbital speed squared expression ($v^2 = \frac{GM}{r}$).

$$\frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2} = \frac{G M}{r}$$

Step 4: Cross-multiply to isolate $T^2$.

$$T^2 G M = 4\pi^2 r^3 \implies T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G M}$$

Step 5: Take the square root to solve for orbital period $T$.

Orbital Period Formula

$$T = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G M}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{r^3}{G M}}$$

Kepler's Third Law Connection: Rearranging $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{GM}$ gives $\frac{r^3}{T^2} = \frac{GM}{4\pi^2} = \text{constant}$. This proves Kepler's 3rd Law directly from Newton's mechanics!

Part 3: Advanced Orbital Relationships & Altitude Effects

1. The Altitude Rule ($r = R + h$)

Remember that $r$ is the distance from the **center of the central body** to the satellite. If an exam gives altitude $h$ above the surface of a planet with radius $R$:

$$r = R + h \implies v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{R + h}}$$
Orbit Type Typical Altitude ($h$) Orbital Radius ($r = R_E + h$) Orbital Speed ($v$) Orbital Period ($T$)
Low Earth Orbit (LEO) $400\text{ km}$ (ISS) $\sim 6,771\text{ km}$ $\sim 7.67\text{ km/s}$ ($\mathbf{27,600\text{ km/h}}$) $\sim \mathbf{92\text{ minutes}}$
Medium Earth Orbit (MEO) $20,200\text{ km}$ (GPS) $\sim 26,571\text{ km}$ $\sim 3.87\text{ km/s}$ $\sim \mathbf{12\text{ hours}}$
Geostationary Orbit (GEO) $35,786\text{ km}$ $\sim 42,157\text{ km}$ $\sim 3.07\text{ km/s}$ $\mathbf{24\text{ hours}}$ (1 Sidereal Day)

Exam Warning: Higher Altitude Means SLOWER Speed!

A very common student mistake is assuming that satellites further away move faster to "stay up". In reality, because $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$, as orbital radius $r$ increases, orbital speed DECREASES ($v \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}$)! High altitude satellites move slower and take much longer to complete an orbit.

Four Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 (Easy): ISS Orbital Speed & Period

[Basic Orbital Velocity Calculation]

The International Space Station (ISS) orbits Earth at an altitude of $h = 400\text{ km}$. Earth's mass is $M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$ and Earth's radius is $R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$.

a) Calculate the orbital radius $r$ of the ISS.
b) Calculate the orbital speed $v$ of the ISS in $\text{m/s}$ and $\text{km/h}$.
c) Calculate the orbital period $T$ of the ISS in minutes.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Orbital Radius ($r$):

$$r = R_E + h = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m} + 400,000\text{ m} = \mathbf{6.771 \times 10^6\text{ m}}$$

Part b) Orbital Speed ($v$):

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}{6.771 \times 10^6}}$$ $$v = \sqrt{\frac{3.9857 \times 10^{14}}{6.771 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{5.8864 \times 10^7} = \mathbf{7672\text{ m/s}} \quad (\mathbf{7.67\text{ km/s}})$$

Converting to $\text{km/h}$ ($7672 \times 3.6$):

$$v = \mathbf{27,620\text{ km/h}}$$

Part c) Orbital Period ($T$):

$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2 \pi \times (6.771 \times 10^6)}{7672} = \frac{4.2544 \times 10^7}{7672} = \mathbf{5545\text{ seconds}}$$

Converting to minutes ($\frac{5545}{60}$):

$$T = \mathbf{92.4\text{ minutes}}$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Deriving Geostationary Orbit Radius ($T = 24\text{ hours}$)

[Rearranging Period Equation]

A geostationary satellite remains fixed above the same point on Earth's equator. Its orbital period matches Earth's rotation period ($T = 24.0\text{ hours} = 86,400\text{ seconds}$).

a) Calculate the required orbital radius $r$ for a geostationary satellite.
b) Calculate its altitude $h$ above Earth's surface ($R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}, M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Orbital Radius ($r$):

Starting from $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G M_E}$, solve for $r^3$:

$$r^3 = \frac{G M_E T^2}{4\pi^2}$$ $$r^3 = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})(86400)^2}{4\pi^2}$$ $$r^3 = \frac{(3.9857 \times 10^{14}) \times (7.46496 \times 10^9)}{39.4784} = \frac{2.9753 \times 10^{24}}{39.4784} = 7.5365 \times 10^{22}\text{ m}^3$$

Taking cube root ($\sqrt[3]{r^3}$):

$$r = (7.5365 \times 10^{22})^{1/3} = \mathbf{4.224 \times 10^7\text{ m}} \quad (\mathbf{42,240\text{ km}})$$

Part b) Altitude ($h$):

$$h = r - R_E = 42,240\text{ km} - 6,371\text{ km} = \mathbf{35,869\text{ km}} \approx \mathbf{3.59 \times 10^7\text{ m}}$$

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Exoplanet Star Mass Determination

[Astronomical Mass Calculation]

Astronomers observe an exoplanet orbiting a distant star at an orbital radius $r = 1.20 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}$ with an orbital period $T = 110\text{ Earth days}$ ($9.504 \times 10^6\text{ seconds}$).

a) Calculate the mass $M_*$ of the central star.
b) Express $M_*$ as a fraction/multiple of Sun's mass ($M_{\text{Sun}} = 1.989 \times 10^{30}\text{ kg}$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Central Star Mass ($M_*$):

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G M_*} \implies M_* = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G T^2}$$ $$M_* = \frac{4\pi^2 \times (1.20 \times 10^{11})^3}{(6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \times (9.504 \times 10^6)^2}$$ $$M_* = \frac{39.4784 \times (1.728 \times 10^{33})}{(6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \times (9.0326 \times 10^{13})} = \frac{6.8218 \times 10^{34}}{6.0283 \times 10^3} = \mathbf{1.13 \times 10^{31}\text{ kg}}$$

Part b) Ratio to Solar Mass:

$$\frac{M_*}{M_{\text{Sun}}} = \frac{1.1316 \times 10^{31}}{1.989 \times 10^{30}} = \mathbf{5.69 \text{ Solar Masses}}$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension - JRAHS Style): Hohmann Transfer Orbit Velocity Boost

[Dual Orbital Velocity Shift & Energy]

A satellite of mass $m = 1200\text{ kg}$ is initially in a circular LEO orbit of radius $r_1 = 6.70 \times 10^6\text{ m}$. To transfer to a higher circular orbit of radius $r_2 = 1.34 \times 10^7\text{ m}$ ($2 r_1$), it undergoes orbital maneuvers.

a) Calculate its initial orbital speed $v_1$ and final circular orbital speed $v_2$.
b) Calculate the ratio of final kinetic energy to initial kinetic energy ($K_2 / K_1$).
c) Explain why moving to a higher orbit requires firing thrusters forward to gain total energy, yet results in a *slower* final circular speed!

Full Band 6 JRAHS Solution:

Part a) Speeds $v_1$ and $v_2$:

$$v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{r_1}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.9857 \times 10^{14}}{6.70 \times 10^6}} = \mathbf{7713\text{ m/s}}$$ $$v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{2 r_1}} = \frac{v_1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{7713}{1.4142} = \mathbf{5454\text{ m/s}}$$

Part b) Kinetic Energy Ratio ($K_2 / K_1$):

$$\frac{K_2}{K_1} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} m v_2^2}{\frac{1}{2} m v_1^2} = \left(\frac{v_2}{v_1}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 = \mathbf{0.500} \quad (50\%)$$

Part c) Energy Paradox Explanation:

Firing thrusters adds work and increases the satellite's **total mechanical energy** ($E = K + U$). However, as the satellite climbs to higher radius $r_2$, it loses kinetic energy because Earth's gravity does negative work against its climb, storing energy as **gravitational potential energy** ($\Delta U > 0$). In fact, the gain in potential energy is twice as large as the loss in kinetic energy ($\vert{}\Delta U\vert{} = 2 \vert{}\Delta K\vert{}$), leaving the satellite in a higher, slower circular orbit!

Practise Question 1 (Easy): Speed Ratio for Quadrupled Orbital Radius

[3 Marks]

Satellite A orbits Earth at radius $r_A = R$. Satellite B orbits Earth at radius $r_B = 4 R$.

Calculate the ratio of Satellite B's orbital speed to Satellite A's orbital speed ($v_B / v_A$).

Hint: Use $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} \implies v \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}$.

$$v_A = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{R}} \quad \text{and} \quad v_B = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{4 R}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4}} \sqrt{\frac{G M}{R}} = \frac{1}{2} v_A$$ $$\frac{v_B}{v_A} = \mathbf{0.500} \quad (\mathbf{1:2\text{ ratio}})$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Orbital velocity satisfies $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$, showing $v \propto r^{-1/2}$. Quadrupling orbital radius ($r_B = 4 r_A$) changes speed by a factor of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{4}} = 0.5$. Thus $\frac{v_B}{v_A} = 0.500$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies relationship $v \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}$.
  • 2 Marks: Substitutes radius factor of 4 into square root denominator.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains correct speed ratio $0.500$.

Practise Question 2 (Medium): Deriving $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$ Written Response

[4 Marks]

Derive the formula for orbital speed $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$ from first principles, explaining every step and stating why satellite mass does not affect orbital velocity.

Hint: Start by equating centripetal force $F_c = \frac{m v^2}{r}$ to gravitational force $F_g = \frac{G M m}{r^2}$.

1. Equate centripetal force to gravitational force: $\frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{G M m}{r^2}$.

2. Cancel satellite mass $m$ from both sides: $\frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{G M}{r^2}$.

3. Multiply both sides by $r$: $v^2 = \frac{G M}{r}$.

4. Take square root: $v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}$.

5. Satellite mass $m$ cancels because gravitational force and inertial resistance to acceleration are both proportional to $m$. Thus all objects at radius $r$ accelerate at the same rate regardless of mass.

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"In circular orbit, gravity provides centripetal force: $F_c = F_g \implies \frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{G M m}{r^2}$. Cancelling satellite mass $m$ gives $\frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{G M}{r^2}$. Multiplying by $r$ yields $v^2 = \frac{G M}{r}$, and taking the square root gives $v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}$. Satellite mass cancels because gravitational mass equals inertial mass; hence gravitational acceleration depends solely on central mass $M$ and radius $r$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Equates $F_c = F_g$ using correct algebraic formulas.
  • 2 Marks: Cancels satellite mass $m$ correctly.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains final expression $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$.
  • 4 Marks: Explains physical reason for mass independence (gravitational mass = inertial mass).

Practise Question 3 (Hard): Orbital Period Calculation for GPS Satellite

[5 Marks]

A GPS satellite orbits Earth at an altitude of $h = 20,200\text{ km}$. Earth's mass is $M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$ and Earth's radius is $R_E = 6,371\text{ km}$.

a) Calculate the orbital period $T$ of the GPS satellite in hours.
b) How many full orbits does this satellite complete in one 24-hour day?

Hint: $r = (6371 + 20200) \times 10^3 = 2.6571 \times 10^7\text{ m}$. Use $T = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G M_E}}$.

Part a) Period Calculation ($T$):

$$r = 6,371,000 + 20,200,000 = 2.6571 \times 10^7\text{ m}$$ $$r^3 = (2.6571 \times 10^7)^3 = 1.8759 \times 10^{22}\text{ m}^3$$ $$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G M_E} = \frac{4\pi^2 \times (1.8759 \times 10^{22})}{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})} = \frac{7.4058 \times 10^{23}}{3.9857 \times 10^{14}} = 1.8581 \times 10^9\text{ s}^2$$ $$T = \sqrt{1.8581 \times 10^9} = 43,105\text{ seconds}$$

Converting to hours ($\frac{43105}{3600}$):

$$T = \mathbf{11.97\text{ hours}} \approx \mathbf{12.0\text{ hours}}$$

Part b) Orbits per day:

$$\text{Orbits} = \frac{24.0\text{ hours}}{11.97\text{ hours}} = \mathbf{2.00\text{ orbits per day}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Orbital radius is $r = R_E + h = 2.6571 \times 10^7\text{ m}$. Applying period equation $T = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G M_E}} = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 (2.6571 \times 10^7)^3}{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}} = 43,100\text{ s} = 12.0\text{ hours}$. In a 24-hour day, the satellite completes $\frac{24}{12} = 2.00\text{ orbits}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Calculates total orbital radius $r = 2.6571 \times 10^7\text{ m}$.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly substitutes into period equation $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{GM}$.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains period in seconds ($43,100\text{ s}$).
  • 4 Marks: Converts period to hours ($12.0\text{ hours}$).
  • 5 Marks: Determines satellite completes 2 orbits per day.

Practise Question 4 (JRAHS Extension): Binary Star System Orbital Mechanics

[6 Marks]

Two identical stars, each of mass $M = 3.00 \times 10^{30}\text{ kg}$, orbit around their common center of mass in circular orbits of radius $R = 2.00 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}$ (separation $2 R = 4.00 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}$).

a) Express the gravitational attraction force $F_g$ between the two stars in terms of $G, M, R$.
b) Equate $F_g$ to centripetal force to derive the orbital speed $v$ of each star.
c) Calculate the orbital period $T$ of the binary star system in years ($G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$).

Hint: Separation distance between stars is $2 R$. Gravitational force is $F_g = \frac{G M^2}{(2 R)^2} = \frac{G M^2}{4 R^2}$. Centripetal force for radius $R$ is $F_c = \frac{M v^2}{R}$.

a) Gravitational Force ($F_g$):

$$F_g = \frac{G M M}{(2 R)^2} = \mathbf{\frac{G M^2}{4 R^2}}$$

b) Deriving Orbital Speed ($v$):

Each star orbits in a circle of radius $R$ around the center of mass:

$$F_c = \frac{M v^2}{R}$$ $$\frac{M v^2}{R} = \frac{G M^2}{4 R^2} \implies v^2 = \frac{G M}{4 R} \implies \mathbf{v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{4 R}} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{G M}{R}}}$$

Numerical calculation of $v$:

$$v = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(3.00 \times 10^{30})}{2.00 \times 10^{11}}} = 0.5 \times \sqrt{1.0011 \times 10^9} = 0.5 \times 31,640 = \mathbf{15,820\text{ m/s}}$$

c) Orbital Period ($T$):

$$T = \frac{2\pi R}{v} = \frac{2 \pi \times (2.00 \times 10^{11})}{15,820} = \frac{1.2566 \times 10^{12}}{15,820} = \mathbf{7.943 \times 10^7\text{ seconds}}$$

Converting to Earth years ($\frac{7.943 \times 10^7}{3.1536 \times 10^7}$):

$$T = \mathbf{2.52\text{ Earth years}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Separation distance is $2 R$, so gravitational attraction is $F_g = \frac{G M^2}{(2 R)^2} = \frac{G M^2}{4 R^2}$. Equating to centripetal force for orbit radius $R$ gives $\frac{M v^2}{R} = \frac{G M^2}{4 R^2} \implies v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{4 R}} = 15,820\text{ m/s}$. The orbital period is $T = \frac{2\pi R}{v} = \frac{2\pi (2.00 \times 10^{11})}{15,820} = 7.94 \times 10^7\text{ s} = 2.52\text{ years}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Recognises separation distance is $2 R$.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly expresses $F_g = \frac{G M^2}{4 R^2}$.
  • 3 Marks: Derives formula $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{4R}}$.
  • 4 Marks: Calculates numerical orbital speed $v = 15,820\text{ m/s}$.
  • 5 Marks: Calculates period in seconds ($7.94 \times 10^7\text{ s}$).
  • 6 Marks: Converts period to Earth years ($2.52\text{ years}$).
