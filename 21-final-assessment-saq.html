Question 1: Vertical Rollercoaster Loop Mechanics

[5 Marks]

The diagram below shows a rollercoaster carriage about to enter a vertical circular loop of diameter $17.0\text{ m}$. The carriage is initially at rest at a height $\Delta h$ above the bottom of the loop. Air resistance and track friction are negligible.

Figure 1.1: Rollercoaster carriage entering a vertical circular loop of diameter 17.0 m.
(a) Show that the minimum speed of the carriage at the apex (top) of the loop is $9.1\text{ m s}^{-1}$ so that a passenger remains in contact with their seat. [2 Marks]
(b) Calculate the minimum initial height $\Delta h$ above the bottom of the loop required to enable the passenger to maintain contact with their seat at the top. [3 Marks]

Hint: At the top of the loop, the normal force approaches zero ($N \ge 0$). Thus, gravity alone provides the minimum centripetal force ($mg = \frac{mv^2}{r}$). For part (b), apply the Law of Conservation of Mechanical Energy: initial potential energy ($mg\Delta h$) equals the sum of kinetic energy ($\frac{1}{2}mv^2$) and potential energy ($mg \times 2r$) at the apex.

Detailed Worked Solution:

Part (a): Minimum Speed at Apex

At the apex of the vertical loop, two downward forces act on the passenger: gravity ($mg$) and the normal force from the seat ($N$). The net force provides the required centripetal force:

$$\Sigma F_c = N + mg = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

For the passenger to just maintain contact with the seat, $N = 0\text{ N}$:

$$mg = \frac{mv_{\text{min}}^2}{r} \implies v_{\text{min}} = \sqrt{gr}$$

Given loop diameter $D = 17.0\text{ m}$, the radius is $r = \frac{17.0}{2} = 8.5\text{ m}$. Using $g = 9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$:

$$v_{\text{min}} = \sqrt{9.80 \times 8.5} = \sqrt{83.3} \approx 9.1268\text{ m s}^{-1} \approx 9.1\text{ m s}^{-1}\quad\blacksquare$$

Part (b): Minimum Initial Release Height $\Delta h$

By conservation of mechanical energy between the release point (height $\Delta h$, velocity $u = 0$) and the apex of the loop (height $h_{\text{top}} = 17.0\text{ m}$, velocity $v = 9.1268\text{ m s}^{-1}$):

$$E_{\text{initial}} = E_{\text{apex}}$$ $$mg\Delta h = mg(2r) + \frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{min}}^2$$

Dividing all terms by $m$ and substituting $v_{\text{min}}^2 = gr$:

$$g\Delta h = 2gr + \frac{1}{2}gr = \frac{5}{2}gr$$ $$\Delta h = \frac{5}{2}r = 2.5 \times 8.5\text{ m} = 21.25\text{ m}$$

Thus, the minimum height required above the bottom of the loop is $\mathbf{21.3\text{ m}}$ (or $21.25\text{ m}$).

Question 2: Stroboscopic Analysis of Projectile Motion on an Alien Planet

[6 Marks]

An athletics meeting is held on a planet in a distant solar system. A shot-putter releases a shot from an initial height of $2.0\text{ m}$ above ground level. A stroboscopic plot of the position of the shot is shown below, where successive flash readings are taken $\Delta t = 0.50\text{ s}$ apart and the shot is released at time $t = 0\text{ s}$. Air resistance is negligible.

Figure 2.1: Stroboscopic position plot of the shot recorded at 0.50 s intervals.
(a) Indicate by marking an 'X' on the trajectory graph where the maximum vertical displacement occurs. [1 Mark]
(b) Calculate the uniform horizontal velocity ($v_x$) of the shot. [2 Marks]
(c) Calculate the initial vertical velocity ($u_y$) of the shot by analyzing the coordinates of two recorded positions. [3 Marks]

Hint: Due to parabolic symmetry, the peak occurs midway between the two highest equal-height points at $t = 1.0\text{ s}$ ($x=10\text{ m}$) and $t = 1.5\text{ s}$ ($x=15\text{ m}$), i.e. at $x = 12.5\text{ m}$ ($t = 1.25\text{ s}$). For part (c), set up two kinematic equations $y_1 = u_y t_1 + \frac{1}{2}a_y t_1^2$ and $y_2 = u_y t_2 + \frac{1}{2}a_y t_2^2$ to solve for $u_y$ and $a_y$ simultaneously.

Detailed Worked Solution:

Part (a): Position of Maximum Vertical Displacement

The shot reaches identical heights of $y = 8.3\text{ m}$ at $t = 1.0\text{ s}$ ($x = 10\text{ m}$) and $t = 1.5\text{ s}$ ($x = 15\text{ m}$). By parabolic trajectory symmetry, the maximum vertical height occurs precisely halfway between these points at $x = 12.5\text{ m}$ ($t = 1.25\text{ s}$) at a height of approximately $8.8\text{ m}$. Mark 'X' at coordinates $(12.5\text{ m}, 8.8\text{ m})$.

Part (b): Horizontal Velocity ($v_x$)

Since air resistance is negligible, horizontal velocity is constant:

$$v_x = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{5.0\text{ m}}{0.50\text{ s}} = 10.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

Part (c): Initial Vertical Velocity ($u_y$)

Using the vertical displacement equation $y(t) = y_0 + u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2$ where $y_0 = 2.0\text{ m}$:

1. At $t = 1.0\text{ s}$, $y = 8.3\text{ m}$:

$$8.3 = 2.0 + u_y(1.0) + \frac{1}{2}a_y(1.0)^2 \implies 6.3 = u_y + 0.5 a_y \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$

2. At $t = 2.0\text{ s}$, $y = 6.2\text{ m}$:

$$6.2 = 2.0 + u_y(2.0) + \frac{1}{2}a_y(2.0)^2 \implies 4.2 = 2 u_y + 2 a_y \implies 2.1 = u_y + a_y \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

Subtracting Equation 2 from Equation 1:

$$(6.3 - 2.1) = (u_y + 0.5 a_y) - (u_y + a_y)$$ $$4.2 = -0.5 a_y \implies a_y = -8.4\text{ m s}^{-2}$$

Substituting $a_y = -8.4\text{ m s}^{-2}$ back into Equation 2:

$$u_y = 2.1 - a_y = 2.1 - (-8.4) = 10.5\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

Thus, the initial vertical velocity is $\mathbf{10.5\text{ m s}^{-1}}$ upwards.

Question 3: Satellite Total Energy Curve & Kinetic Energy Derivation

[6 Marks]

Three identical satellites (L, M, and N), each of mass $m = 550\text{ kg}$, orbit the Earth in circular paths. The graph below displays the total mechanical energy $E_T$ against orbital radius for satellites L and N.

Figure 3.1: Total mechanical energy curve ($E_T$) for Earth satellites.
(a) Satellite M has an orbital radius exactly half that of Satellite N ($r_M = 20 \times 10^6\text{ m}$). Place an 'X' on the total energy curve representing Satellite M and state its total energy. [1 Mark]
(b) On the axes above, sketch and label the kinetic energy ($K$) curve for the three satellites. Annotate positions L, M, and N on your curve. [2 Marks]
(c) Justify, using mathematical derivations, the shape and magnitude of the kinetic energy curve you drew in part (b). [3 Marks]

Hint: Total orbital energy is $E_T = -\frac{GMm}{2r}$. Kinetic energy is $K = \frac{GMm}{2r} = -E_T$. Therefore, the kinetic energy curve is an exact positive reflection of the total energy curve across the horizontal zero-energy axis!

Detailed Worked Solution:

Part (a): Satellite M Position & Total Energy

For Satellite N at $r_N = 40 \times 10^6\text{ m}$, total energy is $E_T = -6.8 \times 10^9\text{ J}$ (or approx $-13.6 \times 10^9\text{ J}$ depending on scale). Since $E_T \propto \frac{1}{r}$, halving the radius doubles the negative total energy: $E_{T,M} = 2 \times E_{T,N} \approx -27.2 \times 10^9\text{ J}$ at $r_M = 20 \times 10^6\text{ m}$. Mark 'X' at $(20 \times 10^6\text{ m}, -27.2 \times 10^9\text{ J})$.

Part (b) & (c): Kinetic Energy Curve & Mathematical Derivation

Figure 3.2: Completed Kinetic Energy curve $K(r)$ reflected across $E=0$.

Derivation:

1. Centripetal force for a stable circular orbit is supplied by gravity:

$$\frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{GMm}{r^2} \implies mv^2 = \frac{GMm}{r}$$

2. Substitute into kinetic energy expression:

$$K = \frac{1}{2}m v^2 = \frac{GMm}{2r}$$

3. Gravitational potential energy is $U = -\frac{GMm}{r}$. Total mechanical energy is:

$$E_T = K + U = \frac{GMm}{2r} - \frac{GMm}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{2r}$$

4. Comparing $K$ and $E_T$:

$$K = -E_T = +\frac{GMm}{2r}$$

This proves that kinetic energy $K$ is positive for all $r$, inversely proportional to radius ($K \propto \frac{1}{r}$), and equal in magnitude to the total energy at every point.

Question 4: Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry

[7 Marks]

Time-of-flight mass spectrometry accelerates ionized molecules using a uniform electric field before measuring their drift time through a field-free chamber to determine molecular mass. A laser pulse ionises a single molecule at the midpoint between two parallel charged plates separated by potential difference $V = 4\text{ kV}$.

Figure 4.1: Schematic of the time-of-flight mass spectrometer.
(a) Draw on the diagram above the electric field lines between the two charged acceleration plates. [1 Mark]
(b) The sample ion carrying a single elementary positive charge ($q = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$) starts from rest midway between the plates. Show that its exit velocity $v$ at the hole is given by $v = \sqrt{\frac{6.4 \times 10^{-16}}{m}}$ where $m$ is the ion mass in kg. [3 Marks]
(c) The drift distance between the exit hole and detector is $1.5\text{ m}$. The measured time of flight across this drift distance is $t = 23\text{ }\mu\text{s}$. Calculate the mass of the molecular ion. [3 Marks]

Hint: Since the sample is located midway between plates with total potential difference $4\text{ kV}$, the potential drop experienced by the ion is $\Delta V = 2\text{ kV} = 2000\text{ V}$. Apply work-energy equivalence $W = q\Delta V = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$. In part (c), the drift region has zero electric field, so velocity is constant ($v = \frac{d}{t}$).

Detailed Worked Solution:

Part (a): Electric Field Lines

Electric field lines are uniform, parallel, equally spaced straight lines directed from the positive (left) plate to the negative (right) plate with arrows pointing right.

Part (b): Derivation of Exit Velocity

Since the sample is positioned at the midpoint between the plates, it accelerates across half the total potential difference:

$$\Delta V = \frac{4000\text{ V}}{2} = 2000\text{ V}$$

The electric work done on the charge converts entirely into kinetic energy:

$$W = q\Delta V = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$ $$(1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C})(2000\text{ V}) = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$ $$3.204 \times 10^{-16} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \implies v^2 = \frac{6.408 \times 10^{-16}}{m}$$ $$v = \sqrt{\frac{6.4 \times 10^{-16}}{m}}\quad\blacksquare$$

Part (c): Molecular Mass Calculation

In the field-free drift space, the ion travels at constant speed $v$:

$$v = \frac{d}{t} = \frac{1.5\text{ m}}{23 \times 10^{-6}\text{ s}} \approx 65217.39\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

Substitute $v$ into the derived mass-velocity relationship:

$$v^2 = \frac{6.4 \times 10^{-16}}{m} \implies m = \frac{6.4 \times 10^{-16}}{v^2}$$ $$m = \frac{6.4 \times 10^{-16}}{(65217.39)^2} = \frac{6.4 \times 10^{-16}}{4.2533 \times 10^9} \approx 1.5046 \times 10^{-25}\text{ kg}$$

Thus, the mass of the molecular ion is $\mathbf{1.50 \times 10^{-25}\text{ kg}}$ (or approx $90.6\text{ u}$).

Question 5: Comparative Mechanics of Flat vs Banked Road Curves

[7 Marks]

Two circular road bends each have an identical curvature radius of $r = 65\text{ m}$. Curve 1 is flat (unbanked) with a suggested maximum safe speed of $v_1 = 70\text{ km h}^{-1}$. Curve 2 is banked at an angle of $\theta = 12^\circ$ with a suggested maximum speed of $v_2 = 100\text{ km h}^{-1}$.

Explain, both qualitatively and quantitatively, the physics principles accounting for the difference in maximum recommended speeds for a $1000\text{ kg}$ car negotiating these two curves. Include annotated vector force diagrams for both cases.

Figure 5.1: Car models on unbanked and banked curves.

Hint: Convert speeds to $\text{m s}^{-1}$ ($70\text{ km h}^{-1} = 19.44\text{ m s}^{-1}$, $100\text{ km h}^{-1} = 27.78\text{ m s}^{-1}$). On a flat road, centripetal force is supplied entirely by static friction ($F_f = \mu N = \frac{mv^2}{r}$). On a banked road without friction, the horizontal component of the normal force supplies centripetal acceleration ($N \sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r}$, $v = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta}$). Combine banking and friction to explain the higher speed capacity.

Detailed Worked Solution:

Vector Diagrams:

Figure 5.2: Free-body diagrams for flat road vs banked road turn.

Qualitative Analysis:

1. Flat Curve: The normal force $N$ acts purely vertically to balance weight ($N = mg$). The centripetal force required for horizontal circular motion is provided solely by static friction between tyres and road ($F_c = F_f$). If $v$ exceeds the friction threshold, skidding occurs.

2. Banked Curve: The normal force $N$ is perpendicular to the inclined road, tilting inwards. Its horizontal component $N \sin\theta$ provides a centripetal force component independent of friction. This allows higher speeds before relying on tyre friction.

Quantitative Analysis:

1. Flat Road at $v_1 = 70\text{ km h}^{-1} = 19.44\text{ m s}^{-1}$:

$$F_{c,1} = \frac{m v_1^2}{r} = \frac{1000 \times (19.44)^2}{65} \approx 5815\text{ N}$$ $$\mu_{\text{req}} = \frac{F_c}{mg} = \frac{5815}{1000 \times 9.80} \approx 0.59$$

2. Banked Road Rated Speed (Zero-Friction Speed):

$$v_{\text{rated}} = \sqrt{rg \tan\theta} = \sqrt{65 \times 9.80 \times \tan(12^\circ)} = \sqrt{637 \times 0.21255} \approx 11.64\text{ m s}^{-1} \approx 41.9\text{ km h}^{-1}$$

3. Banked Road Maximum Speed at $v_2 = 100\text{ km h}^{-1} = 27.78\text{ m s}^{-1}$:

$$F_{c,2} = \frac{1000 \times (27.78)^2}{65} \approx 11873\text{ N}$$

At $100\text{ km h}^{-1}$, the horizontal normal force component $N \sin\theta = mg \tan\theta = 1000 \times 9.80 \times \tan(12^\circ) = 2083\text{ N}$ assists static friction, drastically reducing the friction coefficient required compared to a flat turn at the same high speed.

Question 6: Spacecraft Energy & Integration vs Potential Formula

[4 Marks]

The Mars Odyssey spacecraft ($m = 700\text{ kg}$) was launched from Earth. The graph below illustrates the inverse-square gravitational force acting on the spacecraft plotted against its altitude above Earth's surface.

Figure 6.1: Gravitational force vs altitude for the 700 kg Mars Odyssey craft.

To compute the minimum energy required to transfer the spacecraft from a circular orbit at altitude $h_i = 3000\text{ km}$ ($3 \times 10^6\text{ m}$) to altitude $h_f = 15000\text{ km}$ ($15 \times 10^6\text{ m}$), two physics students proposed different methods:

Student A (Graphical Trapezium Integration) Student B (Formula Substitution)
$$\text{Energy} = \text{Area under } F-h \text{ graph}$$ $$\approx \frac{1}{2}(F_i + F_f)\Delta h = \frac{1}{2}(3500 + 500) \times (15-3) \times 10^6 = 2.4 \times 10^{10}\text{ J}$$ $$\text{Energy} = \Delta U = U_f - U_i = GMm\left(\frac{1}{r_i} - \frac{1}{r_f}\right)$$ $$= 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.0 \times 10^{24} \times 700 \left(\frac{1}{15} - \frac{1}{3}\right) \times \frac{1}{10^6}$$

Assess the accuracy and physical validity of both students' methods.

Hint: Check two things: 1. Is Student A's straight-line trapezoidal area an accurate estimate for a highly non-linear curve? 2. Did Student B use radial distance from Earth's centre ($r = r_E + h$) or altitude ($h$)?

Detailed Worked Solution:

Assessment of Student A:

Student A correctly recognises that work done (energy transfer) corresponds to the area under a force-distance graph ($\Delta U = \int F \, dr$). However, approximating the non-linear inverse-square curve ($F \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$) with a linear trapezoid over a vast distance of $12000\text{ km}$ introduces significant overestimation error. Thus, Student A's method is conceptually sound but numerically inaccurate.

Assessment of Student B:

Student B correctly selects the exact integration formula $\Delta U = GMm\left(\frac{1}{r_i} - \frac{1}{r_f}\right)$. However, Student B makes a fatal error by substituting altitudes ($h_i = 3 \times 10^6\text{ m}, h_f = 15 \times 10^6\text{ m}$) directly into $r$ rather than orbital radii measured from Earth's centre ($r = r_E + h = 6.371 \times 10^6 + h$). Therefore, Student B's method is formulaically correct but physically invalid in execution.

Conclusion: Both methods contain errors. The true energy change requires evaluating $r_i = 9.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$ and $r_f = 21.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$ using Student B's equation.

Question 8: Escape Velocity Derivation Error Analysis & Pluto Ejection

[6 Marks]

By definition, escape velocity $v_{\text{esc}}$ is the minimum launch speed required for an object to escape a planet's gravitational field completely to infinity, where its final velocity and potential energy reach zero.

(a) A student presented the derivation below. Explain why the student was awarded 0 out of 3 marks. [3 Marks]

• Initial Kinetic Energy: $K = \frac{1}{2}m v_{\text{esc}}^2$
• Surface Potential Energy: $U = -\frac{GMm}{r}$
• Equating: $\frac{1}{2}m v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \left|-\frac{GMm}{r}\right| \implies \frac{1}{2}m v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{GMm}{r} \implies v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$

(b) The graph below displays gravitational potential energy per unit mass ($\frac{U}{m}$) versus radial distance from Pluto's centre. A meteorite strikes Pluto, ejecting an ice fragment vertically upwards at $1400\text{ m s}^{-1}$. Determine whether the ice fragment escapes Pluto. [3 Marks]
Figure 8.1: Gravitational potential energy per unit mass vs distance for Pluto.

Hint: For part (a), work must be based on Conservation of Mechanical Energy ($E_{\text{total}} = K + U = 0$ at $r = \infty$). Taking absolute value $|\cdot|$ hides the physical reality that potential energy is negative and work is performed against gravity. For part (b), compare initial kinetic energy per unit mass ($\frac{K}{m} = \frac{1}{2}v^2$) with surface potential energy magnitude ($|\frac{U}{m}| \approx 7.4 \times 10^5\text{ J kg}^{-1}$).

Detailed Worked Solution:

Part (a): Flaw in Student's Derivation

1. Violation of Conservation of Energy: The student inserted an ungrounded absolute value sign $|\cdot|$ rather than applying $E_i = E_f$. At infinity, total energy is zero ($K_\infty = 0, U_\infty = 0 \implies E_{\text{total}} = 0$).

2. Physical Meaning of Negative Potential: Gravitational potential energy is strictly negative relative to $U_\infty = 0$. The correct statement is $K_i + U_i = 0 \implies \frac{1}{2}m v_{\text{esc}}^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} = 0$. Taking absolute values misrepresents energy conservation as a simple balance of magnitudes without physical justification.

Part (b): Pluto Escape Calculation

1. From the graph, Pluto's surface lies at $r \approx 1.18\text{ Mm}$ where gravitational potential energy per unit mass is:

$$\frac{U_{\text{surface}}}{m} \approx -7.4 \times 10^5\text{ J kg}^{-1}$$

2. To escape Pluto completely, initial kinetic energy per unit mass must equal or exceed potential energy magnitude:

$$\frac{K}{m} = \frac{1}{2}v^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1400)^2 = 9.8 \times 10^5\text{ J kg}^{-1}$$

3. Calculate required escape speed $v_{\text{esc}}$ from surface:

$$\frac{1}{2}v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \left|\frac{U}{m}\right| = 7.4 \times 10^5 \implies v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2 \times 7.4 \times 10^5} \approx 1216.5\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

Since $v_{\text{launch}} = 1400\text{ m s}^{-1} > v_{\text{esc}} = 1217\text{ m s}^{-1}$ (and $\frac{K}{m} = 9.8 \times 10^5 > \left|\frac{U}{m}\right| = 7.4 \times 10^5$), the ice fragment will successfully escape Pluto.

Question 9: Minimum Earth Orbit Period & Kepler's Third Law Derivation

[6 Marks]

A satellite of mass $m$ travels at angular velocity $\omega$ in a circular orbit at altitude $h$ above a central body of mass $M$ and radius $R$. Earth's mass is $M_E = 6.00 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$ and radius is $R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$.

(i) Write an equation equating gravitational force to centripetal force expressed in terms of angular velocity $\omega$. [1 Mark]
(ii) Use your equation from part (i) to show that orbital period $T$ satisfies $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2(R+h)^3}{GM}$. [3 Marks]
(iii) Explain why no satellite can orbit the Earth with a period under 85 minutes, supporting your explanation with a calculation. [2 Marks]

Hint: Centripetal force in terms of angular velocity is $F_c = m r \omega^2$. For part (iii), the absolute minimum orbital period occurs at theoretical minimum altitude $h = 0\text{ m}$ (surface radius $r = R_E$).

Detailed Worked Solution:

Part (i): Force Balance Equation

$$F_g = F_c \implies \frac{GMm}{(R+h)^2} = m(R+h)\omega^2$$

Part (ii): Derivation of Kepler's Law of Periods

Cancel satellite mass $m$ from both sides:

$$\frac{GM}{(R+h)^2} = (R+h)\omega^2 \implies \omega^2 = \frac{GM}{(R+h)^3}$$

Substitute angular velocity relation $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}$:

$$\left(\frac{2\pi}{T}\right)^2 = \frac{GM}{(R+h)^3} \implies \frac{4\pi^2}{T^2} = \frac{GM}{(R+h)^3}$$

Cross-multiplying to solve for $T^2$:

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 (R+h)^3}{GM}\quad\blacksquare$$

Part (iii): Minimum Earth Orbit Period Calculation

The smallest possible orbital radius for a satellite orbiting Earth occurs at sea-level altitude ($h = 0\text{ m}$, $r = R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$). Any lower altitude lies within Earth's mantle or atmosphere.

$$T_{\text{min}}^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 (6.371 \times 10^6)^3}{(6.67 \times 10^{-11}) \times (6.00 \times 10^{24})} = \frac{4\pi^2 \times (2.586 \times 10^{20})}{4.002 \times 10^{14}} \approx 2.5516 \times 10^7\text{ s}^2$$ $$T_{\text{min}} = \sqrt{2.5516 \times 10^7} \approx 5051.3\text{ s}$$

Convert seconds to minutes:

$$T_{\text{min}} = \frac{5051.3}{60} \approx 84.2\text{ minutes} \approx 85\text{ minutes}$$

Thus, because $r \ge R_E$, $T$ cannot be less than approximately 84-85 minutes.

Question 10: Kepler's Law Verification & Jupiter Mass Determination

[6 Marks]

Question 12: Conical Pendulum Tangential Speed Analysis

[3 Marks]

A small mass is suspended from a ceiling by a light inextensible string of length $L = 1.6\text{ m}$. The mass rotates at constant speed in a horizontal circular path with the string inclined at angle $\theta = 55^\circ$ to the vertical.

Figure 12.1: Conical pendulum rotating in a horizontal circle.

Calculate the tangential orbital speed $v$ of the mass.

Hint: Resolve string tension $T$: $T \cos\theta = mg$ (vertical) and $T \sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r}$ (horizontal). Radius is $r = L \sin\theta$. Dividing equations gives $\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg}$.

Detailed Worked Solution:

Step 1: Determine Horizontal Circle Radius ($r$)

$$r = L \sin\theta = 1.6 \sin(55^\circ) \approx 1.3106\text{ m}$$

Step 2: Resolve Force Equilibrium & Centripetal Component

$$\text{Vertical: } T \cos\theta = mg$$ $$\text{Horizontal: } T \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r}$$

Dividing horizontal force by vertical force:

$$\frac{T \sin\theta}{T \cos\theta} = \frac{\left(\frac{m v^2}{r}\right)}{mg} \implies \tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{r g}$$ $$v^2 = r g \tan\theta$$

Step 3: Calculate Tangential Speed ($v$)

$$v = \sqrt{1.3106 \times 9.80 \times \tan(55^\circ)} = \sqrt{1.3106 \times 9.80 \times 1.4281} = \sqrt{18.342} \approx 4.28\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

Thus, the tangential speed of the mass is $\mathbf{4.28\text{ m s}^{-1}}$.

Question 13: Negative Total Energy Justification

[4 Marks]

Explain why the total mechanical energy of a bound satellite orbiting Earth is always negative.

Hint: Address three core elements: 1. Zero reference point for $U$ at infinity ($r = \infty$). 2. Why $U = -\frac{GMm}{r}$ is always negative. 3. How kinetic energy magnitude ($K = \frac{GMm}{2r}$) compares to potential energy magnitude ($|U| = \frac{GMm}{r}$).

Detailed Worked Solution:

1. Zero Reference Level at Infinity: By scientific convention, gravitational potential energy $U$ is defined as zero at infinite separation ($r = \infty$), where gravitational attraction vanishes.

2. Work Done Moving Inwards: Because gravity is an attractive force, bringing a satellite from infinity to orbital radius $r$ releases energy. Work is done by the field, causing potential energy to drop below zero: $U = -\frac{GMm}{r} < 0$.

3. Kinetic vs Potential Energy Magnitudes: For a stable circular orbit, centripetal force equals gravity ($\frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{GMm}{r^2} \implies K = \frac{GMm}{2r}$). Total mechanical energy is:

$$E_T = K + U = \frac{GMm}{2r} - \frac{GMm}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{2r}$$

Since potential energy $U$ is negative and twice the magnitude of positive kinetic energy $K$ ($|U| = 2K$), the total sum $E_T$ must remain negative. A negative total energy signifies a gravitationally bound system.
