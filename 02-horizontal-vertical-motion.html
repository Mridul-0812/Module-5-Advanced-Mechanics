02. Motion Independence ($a_x = 0$ vs $a_y = -g$)

Phase 1: Projectile Motion — Inquiry Question 1

1. NESA Syllabus Alignment & Learning Intentions

Inquiry Question 1: How can models that are used to explain projectile motion be used to analyse and make predictions?

NESA Stage 6 Syllabus Focus Points:

  • Galileo’s Analysis of Projectile Motion: Describe the independence of horizontal and vertical motion components for a projectile operating under a uniform gravitational field.
  • Horizontal Axis Behaviour ($a_x = 0\text{ m s}^{-2}$): Explain why a projectile experiences zero horizontal acceleration in the absence of air resistance, maintaining constant horizontal velocity ($v_x = u_x$).
  • Vertical Axis Behaviour ($a_y = -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$): Explain why a projectile experiences uniform vertical acceleration under Earth's local gravitational field $g$, altering vertical velocity uniformly over time.
  • Graphical Analysis of 2D Motion: Construct and interpret $s-t$, $v-t$, and $a-t$ graphs for both orthogonal components of projectile trajectories.

Fundamental NESA Axiom for Projectile Motion:

$$\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} a_x \\ a_y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -g \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2} \end{pmatrix}$$

Where the horizontal acceleration is zero and vertical acceleration is uniformly downward (taking upwards as positive $y$).

2. Deep-Dive: Galileo's Principle of Independence

Prior to the early 17th century, Aristotelian mechanics posited that an object required a continuous forward force to maintain horizontal movement, and that horizontal forward force would "prevent" an object from falling vertically until the forward impulse was exhausted. Galileo Galilei overturned this flawed dogma by establishing the Principle of Independence of Perpendicular Motions.

The Independence Axiom

Any vector motion in two-dimensional space can be resolved into two mutually perpendicular (orthogonal) component motions that operate completely independently of each other. The time parameter ($t$) is the sole scalar quantity that unites both independent dimensional equations.

This means that introducing a horizontal velocity $u_x$ to an object dropped from a cliff does not alter the time it takes to reach the ground below. The vertical motion is governed exclusively by vertical forces, and the horizontal motion is governed exclusively by horizontal forces.

Why is Horizontal Acceleration Zero ($a_x = 0\text{ m s}^{-2}$)?

In the standard NESA Stage 6 HSC Physics model, we operate under the foundational assumption of zero air resistance (no atmospheric drag or buoyant fluid forces). Applying Newton's First and Second Laws of Motion to the horizontal axis:

$$\sum F_x = F_{\text{drag}, x} = 0\text{ N}$$ $$\sum F_x = m a_x \implies 0 = m a_x \implies a_x = 0\text{ m s}^{-2}$$

Because there is zero net horizontal force, there is zero horizontal acceleration. Therefore, according to Newton's First Law (Inertia), the horizontal component of velocity remains constant at all times throughout the flight:

$$v_x(t) = u_x = u \cos\theta = \text{constant}$$

The horizontal displacement $s_x$ accumulates linearly with respect to time:

$$s_x = u_x t = (u \cos\theta) t$$

Why is Vertical Acceleration Uniform ($a_y = -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$)?

Near the surface of the Earth, the planet exerts an attractive radial gravitational force directed toward its centre of mass. For small projectile displacement ranges relative to the Earth's radius ($r_E \approx 6371\text{ km}$), the gravitational field strength is treated as uniform and constant.

Applying Newton's Second Law of Motion to the vertical axis (defining upwards as positive $y$):

$$\sum F_y = -F_g = -mg$$ $$\sum F_y = m a_y \implies -mg = m a_y \implies a_y = -g = -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$$

Because the mass $m$ cancels out, all projectiles—regardless of mass, shape, or horizontal speed—experience the exact same downward vertical acceleration under gravity in a vacuum.

The vertical velocity $v_y$ changes at a constant rate of $-9.80\text{ m s}^{-1}$ every second:

$$v_y(t) = u_y + a_y t = u \sin\theta - g t$$

The vertical displacement $s_y$ varies quadratically with time:

$$s_y(t) = u_y t + \frac{1}{2} a_y t^2 = (u \sin\theta) t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2$$

Summary Comparison: Horizontal vs Vertical Axis Parameters

Physical Parameter Horizontal Axis ($x$-axis) Vertical Axis ($y$-axis)
Net Force ($\sum F$) $\sum F_x = 0\text{ N}$ (No drag) $\sum F_y = -mg$ (Downward weight)
Acceleration ($a$) $a_x = 0\text{ m s}^{-2}$ (Uniform motion) $a_y = -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$ (Uniform acceleration)
Velocity Equation ($v$) $v_x = u_x$ (Constant over time) $v_y = u_y - gt$ (Decreases, zero at apex, increases down)
Displacement Equation ($s$) $s_x = u_x t$ (Linear relationship) $s_y = u_y t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$ (Parabolic relationship)
Graph Profile ($s-t$) Straight line passing through origin Parabola (concave downwards)
Graph Profile ($v-t$) Horizontal line ($v_x = u_x$) Diagonal line with slope $= -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$

4. Step-by-Step Worked Solutions

Worked Example 1: Simultaneous Impact Verification (Easy)

[Easy]

A steel sphere is dropped vertically from rest from a height of $35.0\text{ m}$ above the ground. At the exact same instant, a second identical sphere is projected horizontally from the same height with a horizontal speed of $18.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$.

Calculate the time taken for both spheres to hit the ground, and state the horizontal distance travelled by the second sphere. (Assume $g = 9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$).

Solution:

Step 1: Determine vertical flight time for dropped sphere (Sphere A)

Taking downwards as positive $y$ direction: $u_y = 0\text{ m s}^{-1}$, $s_y = 35.0\text{ m}$, $a_y = +9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$.

$$s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2$$ $$35.0 = (0)t + \frac{1}{2}(9.80)t^2$$ $$35.0 = 4.90 t^2 \implies t^2 = \frac{35.0}{4.90} = 7.1428$$ $$t = \sqrt{7.1428} = 2.6726\text{ s} \approx 2.67\text{ s}$$

Step 2: Apply Galileo's Principle to Sphere B

Because vertical motion is independent of horizontal speed ($u_x = 18.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$), Sphere B has an identical initial vertical velocity component ($u_y = 0\text{ m s}^{-1}$) and falls through the same vertical height ($35.0\text{ m}$).

Therefore, Sphere B hits the ground at the exact same instant: $t_B = 2.67\text{ s}$.

Step 3: Calculate horizontal range of Sphere B ($s_x$)

$$s_x = u_x \times t = 18.0 \times 2.6726 = 48.11\text{ m} \approx 48.1\text{ m}$$

Final Answer:
Both spheres reach the ground simultaneously at $t = 2.67\text{ s}$.
Sphere B travels a horizontal distance $s_x = 48.1\text{ m}$ (both to 3 sig. figs.).

Worked Example 2: Component Velocity Evolution (Medium)

[Medium]

A projectile is launched from ground level with an initial velocity of $30.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$ at an angle of $40.0^\circ$ above the horizontal. Calculate:

  1. The velocity vector components ($v_x$ and $v_y$) at time $t = 1.50\text{ s}$.
  2. The velocity vector components ($v_x$ and $v_y$) at time $t = 3.00\text{ s}$.
Solution:

Step 1: Resolve initial velocity components

$$u_x = u \cos\theta = 30.0 \cos(40.0^\circ) = 30.0 \times 0.76604 = 22.98\text{ m s}^{-1}$$ $$u_y = u \sin\theta = 30.0 \sin(40.0^\circ) = 30.0 \times 0.64279 = 19.28\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

Step 2: Calculate components at $t = 1.50\text{ s}$

Taking upwards as positive ($a_y = -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$):

$$v_x(1.50) = u_x = 22.98\text{ m s}^{-1}\quad (\text{Since } a_x = 0)$$ $$v_y(1.50) = u_y + a_y t = 19.28 + (-9.80 \times 1.50) = 19.28 - 14.70 = +4.58\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

(Positive sign indicates the projectile is still ascending).

Step 3: Calculate components at $t = 3.00\text{ s}$

$$v_x(3.00) = u_x = 22.98\text{ m s}^{-1}$$ $$v_y(3.00) = u_y + a_y t = 19.28 + (-9.80 \times 3.00) = 19.28 - 29.40 = -10.12\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

(Negative sign indicates the projectile is now descending downwards).

Final Answer:
At $t = 1.50\text{ s}$: $v_x = 23.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$, $v_y = +4.58\text{ m s}^{-1}$ (ascending).
At $t = 3.00\text{ s}$: $v_x = 23.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$, $v_y = -10.1\text{ m s}^{-1}$ (descending) [to 3 sig. figs.].

Worked Example 3: Kinematic Graph Reconstruction (Hard)

[Hard]

A projectile's flight is monitored using radar, producing a vertical velocity-time equation given by $v_y(t) = 24.5 - 9.80 t$ (where $v_y$ is in $\text{m s}^{-1}$ and $t$ is in seconds). The total flight time to return to launch altitude is $5.00\text{ s}$, and the total horizontal range is $130\text{ m}$.

Reconstruct the initial launch speed $u$, the launch angle $\theta$, and the maximum altitude reached $s_{y,\text{max}}$.

Solution:

Step 1: Extract initial vertical velocity ($u_y$)

At $t = 0\text{ s}$: $v_y(0) = u_y = 24.5\text{ m s}^{-1}$.

Step 2: Determine constant horizontal velocity ($u_x$)

Since horizontal velocity is constant ($a_x = 0$):

$$s_x = u_x \times t_{\text{total}} \implies 130 = u_x \times 5.00 \implies u_x = \frac{130}{5.00} = 26.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

Step 3: Reconstruct total initial launch speed $u$ and angle $\theta$

$$u = \sqrt{u_x^2 + u_y^2} = \sqrt{(26.0)^2 + (24.5)^2} = \sqrt{676 + 600.25} = \sqrt{1276.25} = 35.72\text{ m s}^{-1}$$ $$\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{u_y}{u_x}\right) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{24.5}{26.0}\right) = \tan^{-1}(0.9423) = 43.30^\circ$$

Step 4: Calculate peak altitude ($s_{y,\text{max}}$)

Apex occurs when vertical velocity $v_y = 0\text{ m s}^{-1}$ ($t = 2.50\text{ s}$):

$$v_y^2 = u_y^2 + 2a_y s_y \implies 0^2 = (24.5)^2 + 2(-9.80) s_{y,\text{max}}$$ $$19.6 s_{y,\text{max}} = 600.25 \implies s_{y,\text{max}} = \frac{600.25}{19.6} = 30.625\text{ m}$$

Final Answer:
Initial speed $u = 35.7\text{ m s}^{-1}$, launch angle $\theta = 43.3^\circ$ above horizontal, maximum altitude $s_{y,\text{max}} = 30.6\text{ m}$ (to 3 sig. figs.).

Worked Example 4: Breakdown of Independence under Fluid Drag (Extension)

[Extension]

In real-world atmospheric conditions, a projectile experiences quadratic air resistance forces given by $\mathbf{F}_d = -k v \mathbf{v}$, where $k$ is an atmospheric drag constant. Qualitatively and quantitatively evaluate how fluid drag invalidates Galileo's principle of independent perpendicular motions.

Analytical Evaluation:

1. Resolving the Drag Force Components:

The total drag force acts directly opposite to the instantaneous velocity vector $\mathbf{v}$. The magnitude of instantaneous velocity is $v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$.

Resolving drag along the orthogonal axes gives:

$$F_{d, x} = -k v v_x = -k \left(\sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\right) v_x$$ $$F_{d, y} = -mg - k v v_y = -mg - k \left(\sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\right) v_y$$

2. Mathematical Proof of Coupling (Dependence):

Applying Newton's Second Law to determine horizontal acceleration:

$$a_x = \frac{F_{d,x}}{m} = -\frac{k}{m} \left(\sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\right) v_x$$

Notice that the horizontal acceleration $a_x$ is now a direct function of the vertical velocity component $v_y$! Similarly, vertical acceleration $a_y$ depends explicitly on $v_x$.

Conclusion: In a medium with air resistance, horizontal and vertical motions are no longer independent—they become coupled differential equations. Zero horizontal acceleration ($a_x = 0$) is strictly valid only in a vacuum model where drag is assumed negligible.

Question 1: The Fired vs Dropped Bullet Paradox (Easy)

[2 Marks]

A rifle barrel is aligned perfectly horizontal $1.50\text{ m}$ above flat, level ground. A bullet is fired from the rifle with an initial muzzle velocity of $400\text{ m s}^{-1}$. At the exact same instant, a second identical bullet is dropped from rest from the height of the barrel opening.

Ignoring air resistance, justify which bullet strikes the ground first, and calculate the time taken to hit the ground. ($g = 9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$).

Hint: Recall Galileo's Principle of Independence! Does horizontal muzzle speed alter the initial vertical velocity $u_y$ or vertical acceleration $a_y$?

Worked Solution:

Both bullets strike the ground at the exact same instant. Because horizontal and vertical motions are independent, the fired bullet's large horizontal speed ($400\text{ m s}^{-1}$) has zero effect on its vertical acceleration ($a_y = -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$) or initial vertical speed ($u_y = 0\text{ m s}^{-1}$).

Calculation:

$$s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2 \implies 1.50 = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(9.80)t^2$$ $$4.90 t^2 = 1.50 \implies t^2 = 0.30612 \implies t = 0.5532\text{ s} \approx 0.553\text{ s}$$
Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: States correctly that both bullets hit the ground simultaneously based on Galileo's motion independence principle.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates the correct impact time of $0.553\text{ s}$ with correct SI units.

Question 2: Graphical $v_y-t$ Trajectory Analysis (Medium)

[3 Marks]

A ball is kicked off a roof. Its vertical velocity profile is plotted as a linear equation: $v_y(t) = +12.0 - 9.80 t$ (where upwards is positive). The total time until the ball strikes the ground below is $t = 3.20\text{ s}$.

Determine the height of the roof relative to the ground ($h$).

Hint: Identify $u_y = +12.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$ from the equation intercept. Use $s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2$ at $t = 3.20\text{ s}$. The magnitude of negative $s_y$ gives the roof height!

Worked Solution:

From the equation $v_y(t) = +12.0 - 9.80 t$, the initial vertical velocity is $u_y = +12.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$, and vertical acceleration is $a_y = -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$.

Calculate vertical displacement $s_y$ at landing time $t = 3.20\text{ s}$:

$$s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2 = (12.0)(3.20) + \frac{1}{2}(-9.80)(3.20)^2$$ $$s_y = 38.4 - 4.90(10.24) = 38.4 - 50.176 = -11.776\text{ m}$$

The negative sign indicates a net displacement $11.8\text{ m}$ below the launch point.

Therefore, the height of the roof is $h = 11.8\text{ m}$ (to 3 sig. figs.).

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies $u_y = +12.0\text{ m s}^{-1}$ and $a_y = -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$ correctly from the graph equation.
  • 2 Marks: Substitutes values correctly into the displacement kinematic equation.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains final roof height $11.8\text{ m}$ with correct SI units and significant figures.

Question 3: Two-Dimensional Barrier Clearance (Hard)

[4 Marks]

A stunt motorcycle leaps off a horizontal platform at speed $u_x$ to clear a canyon $22.0\text{ m}$ wide. The landing ledge on the opposite side of the canyon sits $12.0\text{ m}$ lower than the takeoff platform.

Calculate the minimum horizontal launch speed $u_x$ required to ensure the motorcycle successfully lands on the opposite ledge. ($g = 9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$).

Hint: Step 1: Use vertical motion ($s_y = -12.0\text{ m}$, $u_y = 0$) to solve for total fall time $t$. Step 2: Use horizontal range equation ($s_x = u_x t$) to solve for $u_x$.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Isolate vertical flight time ($t$)

Take downwards as positive: $s_y = 12.0\text{ m}$, $u_y = 0\text{ m s}^{-1}$, $a_y = +9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$.

$$s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2 \implies 12.0 = 0 + 4.90 t^2$$ $$t^2 = \frac{12.0}{4.90} = 2.4489 \implies t = 1.5649\text{ s}$$

Step 2: Calculate minimum horizontal speed ($u_x$)

The motorcycle must cover $s_x = 22.0\text{ m}$ horizontally within $t = 1.5649\text{ s}$:

$$s_x = u_x \times t \implies 22.0 = u_x \times 1.5649$$ $$u_x = \frac{22.0}{1.5649} = 14.058\text{ m s}^{-1} \approx 14.1\text{ m s}^{-1}$$
Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly sets up vertical equation with $u_y = 0\text{ m s}^{-1}$.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates correct fall time $t = 1.565\text{ s}$.
  • 3 Marks: Applies horizontal distance relation $s_x = u_x t$.
  • 4 Marks: Obtains correct minimum horizontal speed $14.1\text{ m s}^{-1}$ with correct SI units and significant figures.

Question 4: Synoptic Model Evaluation & Validity (Extension)

[5 Marks]

Assess the validity of assuming $a_x = 0\text{ m s}^{-2}$ and $a_y = -9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$ when modeling the trajectory of a fast-moving cricket ball ($v = 40\text{ m s}^{-1}$) thrown across an oval. In your response:

  1. Explain the theoretical assumptions required for this idealised model.
  2. Analyse how real-world environmental factors cause actual trajectory deviations from the theoretical parabola.

Hint: Address atmospheric drag ($F_d \propto v^2$), Magnus force (spin lift/drift), and the asymmetry of real trajectories compared to true symmetrical parabolas!

Worked Solution:

1. Theoretical Assumptions:
The standard NESA model assumes zero atmospheric fluid friction ($F_{\text{drag}} = 0$) and a uniform gravitational field ($g = 9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$). This establishes $a_x = 0$ (constant $v_x$) and $a_y = -g$, producing a symmetrical parabolic path.

2. Real-World Deviations for a Cricket Ball:
At $40\text{ m s}^{-1}$ ($\approx 144\text{ km/h}$), air resistance cannot be neglected. Atmospheric drag exerts a force opposing motion ($\mathbf{F}_d \propto v^2$), introducing a non-zero horizontal deceleration ($a_x < 0$). This causes horizontal speed to continually decrease ($v_x < u_x$), shortening the horizontal range.

Additionally, vertical drag accelerates downward fall during descent, causing the descending path to become steeper than the ascending path. If the ball spins, the Magnus effect creates asymmetric pressure differentials, generating aerodynamic lift or side-drift forces.

3. Conclusion / Validity Judgment:
The ideal model provides a reasonable first-order approximation for low-speed, dense objects over short distances, but is invalid for high-speed projectiles like cricket balls, where air drag reduces range and creates an asymmetrical, skewed trajectory.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies theoretical assumptions ($F_{\text{drag}} = 0$, uniform $g$).
  • 2 Marks: Explains that real air drag introduces horizontal deceleration ($a_x < 0$).
  • 3 Marks: Details the effect of air drag on vertical motion and trajectory asymmetry.
  • 4 Marks: Mentions spin/Magnus effect or non-parabolic steep descent profile.
  • 5 Marks: Provides a comprehensive, well-structured judgment evaluating the model's validity for high-speed sports balls.
