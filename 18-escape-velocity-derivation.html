18. Launch Energy Conservation & Escape Speed ($v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$)

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 4: Energy & Orbital Motion

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Core Mechanics

Inquiry Question Focus: How does energy conservation dictate the initial velocity required for a body to escape a gravitational field permanently?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus (calculus-free), students must demonstrate rigorous mathematical, conceptual, and graphical mastery over launch mechanics and escape velocity:

  • Apply the Law of Conservation of Mechanical Energy to derive the universal escape velocity formula without calculus: $$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$$
  • Define escape velocity as the minimum launch speed required for an unpowered projectile to reach an infinite distance ($r = \infty$) with zero remaining kinetic energy ($K_\infty = 0$).
  • Analyze why escape velocity depends solely on the mass ($M$) and radius ($r$) of the central body, and is completely independent of the mass ($m$) or angle of launch of the escaping projectile.
  • Distinguish between orbital velocity ($v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$) and escape velocity ($v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2} v_{\text{orbit}}$).
  • Calculate leftover kinetic energy and hyperbolic excess velocity ($v_\infty$) when launch speed exceeds escape velocity ($v_{\text{launch}} > v_{\text{esc}}$).
  • Evaluate the impact of atmospheric drag during real-world rocket launches vs ideal unpowered projectile launches.

Band 6 Exam Keyphrase Strategy: "Zero Total Energy at Infinity"

When asked on an HSC exam to derive or explain escape velocity, always begin with the fundamental energy boundary condition: "At the threshold of escape, the object reaches an infinite distance ($r \to \infty$) where potential energy is zero ($U_\infty = 0$) with zero remaining kinetic energy ($K_\infty = 0$). Therefore, total mechanical energy at infinity is zero ($E_\infty = 0$). By conservation of mechanical energy ($E_i = E_\infty$), initial kinetic energy must equal the magnitude of initial gravitational potential energy."

Part 1: The Intuitive Idea — Throwing a Ball Out of a Gravity Hole

Beginner Concept: Climbing Out of the Well

Think back to the "gravity hole" analogy from Lesson 16. Earth sits at the bottom of a deep energy well.

Imagine throwing a baseball straight up into the air:

  • If you throw it gently ($v = 5\text{ m/s}$), gravity slows it down, it stops at a height of a few meters, and falls back to your hand.
  • If you shoot it out of a cannon ($v = 1000\text{ m/s}$), it goes high up into the upper atmosphere, but gravity still wins and pulls it back down.
  • What if you launch it with a massive speed burst of 11.2 kilometers per second ($11,200\text{ m/s}$ or roughly $40,000\text{ km/h}$)?

At this critical speed—called the Escape Velocity ($v_{\text{esc}}$)—the baseball has so much initial motion energy (kinetic energy) that gravity can never bring its speed down to zero until it reaches infinity! It escapes Earth's gravitational grip forever.

1. The Surprising Fact: Rocket Mass Doesn't Matter!

A common doubt for beginners is: "Does a heavy rocket need a higher escape velocity than a tiny feather?"

The answer is NO! In the absence of air resistance, a $1$-gram pebble and a $100,000$-tonne starship require the exact same escape speed from Earth ($11.2\text{ km/s}$).

Why? Because while a heavier object requires more kinetic energy to move, gravity also pulls harder on it with a larger potential energy depth by the exact same mass factor ($m$). The mass $m$ cancels out cleanly on both sides of the energy equation!

Parameter Orbital Velocity ($v_{\text{orbit}}$) Escape Velocity ($v_{\text{esc}}$)
Definition Speed required to maintain a stable circular path at radius $r$. Minimum speed to escape a gravitational field to infinity ($r = \infty$).
Formula $$v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$$ $$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$$
Total Energy ($E$) Negative ($E = -\frac{GMm}{2r}$). Trapped in orbit. Zero ($E = 0$). Unbound parabolic trajectory.
Mathematical Ratio $$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2} \times v_{\text{orbit}} \approx 1.414 \times v_{\text{orbit}}$$

The Golden Rule of Orbital Escape ($\sqrt{2}$ Rule)

If a satellite is already in a stable circular orbit at speed $v_{\text{orbit}}$, you only need to boost its speed by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$ (an increase of about 41.4%) to transform its circular orbit into an open escape trajectory!

Part 2: Stepping Up — Formal Energy Conservation Derivation

To score full marks on HSC free-response questions, you must be able to perform the complete algebraic derivation from first principles using the Law of Conservation of Mechanical Energy.

Derivation of $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$ (NESA Syllabus Standard)

Step 1: State the Law of Conservation of Mechanical Energy:

In an isolated gravitational system with no non-conservative forces (like air resistance):

$$E_{\text{initial}} = E_{\text{final}}$$ $$K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f$$

Step 2: Define initial conditions at launch (distance $r$):

At launch from initial radial position $r$ (e.g., planet surface $r = R_E$):

$$K_i = \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2$$ $$U_i = -\frac{GMm}{r}$$

Step 3: Define boundary conditions at infinity ($r \to \infty$):

By definition, escape velocity is the minimum initial speed needed to just reach infinity, meaning the object arrives at $r = \infty$ with zero leftover speed ($v_f = 0$):

$$K_f = \frac{1}{2} m (0)^2 = 0$$ $$U_f = -\frac{GMm}{\infty} = 0$$ $$\implies E_{\text{final}} = K_f + U_f = 0 + 0 = 0$$

Step 4: Substitute energy terms into conservation equation:

$$\frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2 + \left( -\frac{GMm}{r} \right) = 0$$ $$\frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{GMm}{r}$$

Step 5: Cancel projectile mass $m$ and solve for $v_{\text{esc}}$:

Divide both sides by mass $m$:

$$\frac{1}{2} v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{GM}{r}$$ $$v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{2GM}{r}$$ $$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}} \quad \blacksquare$$

Figure 1: Trajectory types based on initial launch velocity relative to $v_{\text{esc}}$ and $v_{\text{orbit}}$.

Hyperbolic Excess Velocity ($v_\infty$) — What if $v_{\text{launch}} > v_{\text{esc}}$?

If a spacecraft is launched with speed $v_0 > v_{\text{esc}}$, it will NOT come to a stop at infinity. It arrives at infinity with leftover kinetic energy!

Leftover Speed at Infinity: $$v_\infty = \sqrt{v_0^2 - v_{\text{esc}}^2}$$

HSC Real-World Reality Check: Do Rockets Need $11.2\text{ km/s}$ at Launch?

Common Confusion: Students often ask: "If Saturn V rockets only travelled at a few km/s during launch in Earth's lower atmosphere, how did they escape Earth?"

The Explanation: $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$ is the theoretical minimum speed for an unpowered projectile (like a cannonball fired instantaneously with no engines). Real rockets carry continuous fuel and burn engines over time (powered flight). A continuous thruster could theoretically climb out of Earth's gravity well at $1\text{ km/h}$, as long as fuel is burned continuously to provide thrust against gravity!

Four Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Earth Surface Escape Velocity

[Direct Formula Calculation]

Calculate the escape velocity from the surface of Earth ($M_E = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$, $R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$). Express your answer in $\text{km/s}$ to 3 significant figures.

Figure 2: Surface escape velocity calculation geometry.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Write down universal escape velocity equation:

$$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM_E}{R_E}}$$

Step 2: Substitute given constant values:

$$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times (6.67 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2) \times (5.97 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg})}{6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}}}$$

Step 3: Calculate intermediate products:

$$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{7.964 \times 10^{14}}{6.371 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{1.250 \times 10^8\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2}$$

Step 4: Solve for speed and convert to $\text{km/s}$:

$$v_{\text{esc}} = 11,180\text{ m/s} = \mathbf{11.2\text{ km/s}}$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Escape Velocity from High-Altitude Orbit

[Altitude Correction]

A spacecraft is parked in a circular Low Earth Orbit at an altitude $h = 1000\text{ km}$ above Earth's surface ($R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$).

a) Calculate the orbital radius $r$.
b) Calculate the additional speed ($\Delta v$) needed to escape Earth from this altitude compared to its existing circular orbital speed.

Figure 3: Orbital radius including altitude $h = 1000\text{ km}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Calculate total radial distance $r$:

$$r = R_E + h = 6.371 \times 10^6 + 1.000 \times 10^6 = \mathbf{7.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}}$$

Part b) Calculate existing circular orbital speed ($v_{\text{orbit}}$) and escape speed ($v_{\text{esc}}$):

$$v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM_E}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}{7.371 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{5.401 \times 10^7} = \mathbf{7349\text{ m/s}} \quad (7.35\text{ km/s})$$ $$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2} \times v_{\text{orbit}} = 1.4142 \times 7349 = \mathbf{10,393\text{ m/s}} \quad (10.39\text{ km/s})$$

Part c) Calculate additional speed boost ($\Delta v$):

$$\Delta v = v_{\text{esc}} - v_{\text{orbit}} = 10,393 - 7349 = \mathbf{3044\text{ m/s}} \quad (3.04\text{ km/s})$$

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Comparing Planetary Surface Escape Velocities

[Ratio Analysis & Density Model]

Planet X has twice the mass ($M_X = 2 M_E$) and twice the radius ($R_X = 2 R_E$) of Earth.

a) Derive an expression for the escape velocity of Planet X ($v_{\text{esc, X}}$) in terms of Earth's escape velocity ($v_{\text{esc, E}}$).
b) State whether a spacecraft requires more, less, or equal speed to escape Planet X compared to Earth.

Figure 4: Comparative planetary parameters for Earth vs Planet X.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Algebraic Substitution into Escape Formula:

$$v_{\text{esc, X}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M_X}{R_X}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G (2 M_E)}{2 R_E}}$$

Cancel factor of 2 in numerator and denominator inside the square root:

$$v_{\text{esc, X}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M_E}{R_E}} = v_{\text{esc, E}} \quad \blacksquare$$

Part b) Conclusion:

The escape velocity of Planet X is identical to Earth's escape velocity ($11.2\text{ km/s}$). Doubling the mass doubles the gravitational energy well depth, but doubling the surface radius places the launch point twice as far from the center, perfectly balancing out the factor of 2!

Worked Example 4 (Extension - Band 6 Separator): Hyperbolic Excess Speed Calculation

[Energy Conservation with Leftover Speed]

An interplanetary probe is launched vertically from Earth's surface ($v_{\text{esc}} = 11.2\text{ km/s}$) with an initial launch velocity $v_0 = 15.0\text{ km/s}$.

Calculate the leftover speed of the probe ($v_\infty$) after it has completely exited Earth's gravitational field into deep space.

Figure 5: Over-escape trajectory leaving residual velocity at infinity.

Full Band 6 Solution:

Step 1: Write initial and final energy conservation equation:

$$E_i = E_f \implies \frac{1}{2} m v_0^2 - \frac{GM_E m}{R_E} = \frac{1}{2} m v_\infty^2 + 0$$

Step 2: Recognize that $\frac{GM_E m}{R_E} = \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2$:

$$\frac{1}{2} m v_0^2 - \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{1}{2} m v_\infty^2$$

Step 3: Cancel $\frac{1}{2} m$ across all terms:

$$v_0^2 - v_{\text{esc}}^2 = v_\infty^2 \implies v_\infty = \sqrt{v_0^2 - v_{\text{esc}}^2}$$

Step 4: Substitute launch values ($v_0 = 15.0\text{ km/s}, v_{\text{esc}} = 11.2\text{ km/s}$):

$$v_\infty = \sqrt{(15.0)^2 - (11.2)^2} = \sqrt{225 - 125.44} = \sqrt{99.56} = \mathbf{9.98\text{ km/s}}$$

Notice: Leftover speed ($9.98\text{ km/s}$) is NOT simply $15.0 - 11.2 = 3.8\text{ km/s}$ because energy depends on speed squared ($v^2$)!

Practise Question 1 (Easy): Lunar Escape Velocity Calculation

[3 Marks]

Calculate the escape velocity from the surface of the Moon ($M_M = 7.35 \times 10^{22}\text{ kg}$, $R_M = 1.737 \times 10^6\text{ m}$). Express your answer in $\text{km/s}$ to 3 significant figures.

Figure 6: Lunar surface launch diagram.

Hint: Substitute Moon parameters directly into $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$. Convert $\text{m/s}$ to $\text{km/s}$ by dividing by $1000$.

$$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times (6.67 \times 10^{-11}) \times (7.35 \times 10^{22})}{1.737 \times 10^6}}$$ $$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{9.8049 \times 10^{12}}{1.737 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{5.6447 \times 10^6} = \mathbf{2376\text{ m/s}} = \mathbf{2.38\text{ km/s}}$$

"Using $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM_M}{R_M}}$, substituting $M_M = 7.35 \times 10^{22}\text{ kg}$ and $R_M = 1.737 \times 10^6\text{ m}$ yields $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(7.35 \times 10^{22})}{1.737 \times 10^6}} = 2376\text{ m/s} = 2.38\text{ km/s}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Selects correct formula $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$.

Practise Question 2 (Medium): Derivation of Mass Independence

[4 Marks]

Explain why a heavy space station and a small wrench require the exact same escape velocity when launched from the same location in space. Support your response with a algebraic proof based on conservation of energy.

Figure 7: Comparing escape dynamics for contrasting payload masses.

Hint: Write $K_i + U_i = 0$ and show that projectile mass $m$ appears in both $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ and $U = -\frac{GMm}{r}$, allowing $m$ to be cancelled out.

Algebraic Proof:

$$\frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} = 0 \implies \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{GMm}{r}$$

Dividing both sides by $m$ cancels the payload mass completely:

$$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$$

Since payload mass $m$ does not appear in the final formula, escape velocity is independent of mass.

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"By conservation of mechanical energy, escape occurs when initial kinetic energy equals initial potential energy magnitude: $\frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{GMm}{r}$. Because both kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy are directly proportional to payload mass $m$, dividing both sides by $m$ yields $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$. Since $m$ cancels out, the required escape velocity depends solely on central body mass $M$ and radius $r$, making it identical for both the space station and the wrench."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Equates initial energy terms to zero ($K_i + U_i = 0$).
  • 2 Marks: Explicitly shows cancellation of payload mass $m$.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains final formula $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$.
  • 4 Marks: Connects formula absence of $m$ to physical independence of payload mass.

Practise Question 3 (Hard): Orbital Speed vs Escape Speed Ratio Proof

[5 Marks]

Prove algebraically that for any circular orbit around a central body, the escape velocity from that orbital radius is always $\sqrt{2}$ times the circular orbital velocity ($v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2} v_{\text{orbit}}$). Explain the physical significance of this ratio for spacecraft trajectory maneuvers.

Figure 8: Orbital vs escape velocity vector ratio visual.

Hint: Write $v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$ and $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$. Divide $v_{\text{esc}} / v_{\text{orbit}}$.

Mathematical Proof:

$$v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$$ $$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}} = \sqrt{2 \times \frac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$$ $$\implies v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2} \times v_{\text{orbit}} \quad \blacksquare$$

Physical Significance:

To break out of a circular orbit into an open escape path, a satellite only needs to increase its speed by approximately $41.4\%$ ($\sqrt{2} - 1 = 0.414$). This means a satellite already in orbit has already paid half of the kinetic energy cost required to escape to infinity!

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Circular orbital speed is $v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{GM/r}$. Escape speed is $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2GM/r} = \sqrt{2}\sqrt{GM/r} = \sqrt{2} v_{\text{orbit}}$. Physically, this shows that an orbiting satellite already possesses half the kinetic energy needed for escape ($K_{\text{orbit}} = \frac{1}{2} |U|$, whereas $K_{\text{esc}} = |U|$). Thus, a thruster boost of only $41.4\%$ additional velocity ($\Delta v = (\sqrt{2}-1)v_{\text{orbit}}$) is required to break orbit and escape to infinity."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Writes formulas for $v_{\text{orbit}}$ and $v_{\text{esc}}$.
  • 2 Marks: Executes algebraic proof showing $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2} v_{\text{orbit}}$.
  • 3 Marks: Connects speed ratio to kinetic energy double relationship ($K_{\text{esc}} = 2 K_{\text{orbit}}$).
  • 4 Marks: Explains that speed must increase by $41.4\%$.
  • 5 Marks: Highlights practical significance for spacecraft thruster burn requirements.

Practise Question 4 (JRAHS Extension): Super-Escape Velocity & Deep Space Kinetic Energy

[6 Marks]

A deep-space probe ($m = 1200\text{ kg}$) is launched from Earth's surface ($v_{\text{esc}} = 11.2\text{ km/s}$) with a kinetic energy of $1.50 \times 10^{11}\text{ J}$.

a) Calculate the launch velocity $v_0$.
b) Verify that the probe has enough energy to escape Earth.
c) Calculate the probe's residual velocity $v_\infty$ when it reaches deep space far beyond Earth's gravity.

Figure 9: Residual velocity evaluation scenario diagram.

Hint: Launch kinetic energy $K_0 = \frac{1}{2} m v_0^2 \implies v_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2 K_0}{m}}$. Potential energy magnitude $|U| = \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2$. Leftover $v_\infty = \sqrt{v_0^2 - v_{\text{esc}}^2}$.

Part a) Calculate Launch Speed ($v_0$):

$$K_0 = \frac{1}{2} m v_0^2 \implies v_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2 K_0}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.50 \times 10^{11})}{1200}} = \sqrt{2.50 \times 10^8} = \mathbf{15,811\text{ m/s}} \quad (15.81\text{ km/s})$$

Part b) Escape Energy Verification:

Minimum kinetic energy to escape ($K_{\text{esc}}$):

$$K_{\text{esc}} = \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1200)(11,200)^2 = 7.526 \times 10^{10}\text{ J} \quad (75.26\text{ GJ})$$

Since $K_0 = 150.0\text{ GJ} > 75.26\text{ GJ}$, the probe has more than enough energy to escape ($E_{\text{total}} > 0$).

Part c) Residual Velocity at Infinity ($v_\infty$):

$$v_\infty = \sqrt{v_0^2 - v_{\text{esc}}^2} = \sqrt{(15.811)^2 - (11.200)^2} = \sqrt{250.0 - 125.44} = \sqrt{124.56} = \mathbf{11.16\text{ km/s}} \quad (11,160\text{ m/s})$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Launch speed is $v_0 = \sqrt{2K/m} = \sqrt{2(1.5 \times 10^{11})/1200} = 15.81\text{ km/s}$. Since $v_0 > 11.2\text{ km/s}$, escape is guaranteed. Using energy conservation $\frac{1}{2} m v_0^2 - \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{1}{2} m v_\infty^2 \implies v_\infty = \sqrt{v_0^2 - v_{\text{esc}}^2} = \sqrt{15.811^2 - 11.2^2} = 11.16\text{ km/s}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Calculates launch velocity $v_0 = 15.81\text{ km/s}$.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates minimum escape energy $K_{\text{esc}} = 7.53 \times 10^{10}\text{ J}$.
  • 3 Marks: Verifies escape condition ($K_0 > K_{\text{esc}}$).
  • 4 Marks: Applies energy conservation equation for $v_\infty$.
  • 5 Marks: Substitutes values accurately into $v_\infty = \sqrt{v_0^2 - v_{\text{esc}}^2}$.
  • 6 Marks: Obtains final answer $11.16\text{ km/s}$ with correct units and 3 sig figs.
