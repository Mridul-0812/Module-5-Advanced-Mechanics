10. Torque ($\tau = r F \sin\theta$) & Rotational Equilibrium

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 2 & IQ3 Integration

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Core Mechanics

Inquiry Question Focus: How do linear forces and rotational moments interact to establish complete mechanical equilibrium?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus (calculus-free), students must achieve absolute mathematical and conceptual mastery over torque and static equilibrium dynamics:

  • Quantify torque as the turning effect of a force about a specified axis or pivot point: $$\tau = r F \sin\theta = r F_\perp = r_\perp F$$
  • Apply the two fundamental conditions for total static equilibrium in 2D systems:
    1. Translational Equilibrium: The vector sum of all external forces is zero ($\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$).
    2. Rotational Equilibrium: The vector sum of all torques about any arbitrary pivot point is zero ($\sum \tau = 0 \implies \sum \tau_{\text{CCW}} = \sum \tau_{\text{CW}}$).
  • Resolve multi-force systems involving uniform and non-uniform extended bodies (seesaws, benches, bridges, cranes, wall-hinged beams, and leaning ladders).
  • Utilise strategic pivot selection to eliminate unknown reaction forces from torque equations.

Band 6 Distinction Insight: Strategic Pivot Selection

In top-tier examinations, complex equilibrium problems often contain multiple unknown forces (such as hinge reaction forces $H_x, H_y$ or ground reaction forces $N_g, f_g$). Setting the pivot point directly along the line of action of an unknown force sets its lever arm $r = 0$, immediately eliminating it from the torque equation ($\tau = 0 \cdot F = 0$). This allows you to solve for remaining unknown forces directly!

Part 1: The Intuitive Idea — What is Torque?

Beginner Concept: Opening a Heavy Door

Have you ever noticed where the handle is located on a heavy door? It is always placed at the far outer edge, as far away from the hinges (the pivot) as possible!

Why? If you try pushing a door right next to the hinge, you have to push with immense effort to make it open. But if you push at the outer handle, even a tiny push easily swings the door wide open. Furthermore, you intuitively push at a right angle ($90^\circ$) to the door. Pushing sideways along the door towards the hinge does nothing to open it!

This "turning effort" created by pushing at a distance is what physicists call Torque ($\tau$).

1. The Three Equivalent Ways to Calculate Torque

Depending on how a textbook or exam question presents a diagram, there are three completely equivalent ways to visualize and calculate torque ($\tau$):

Formulation Mathematical Formula When to Use It
1. Angle Formula $$\tau = r F \sin\theta$$ When you are given distance $r$, force $F$, and explicit angle $\theta$ between them.
2. Perpendicular Force Component $$\tau = r \times F_\perp \quad (\text{where } F_\perp = F \sin\theta)$$ When it is easiest to split the force vector into components parallel and perpendicular to the beam.
3. Moment Arm (Perpendicular Distance) $$\tau = r_\perp \times F \quad (\text{where } r_\perp = r \sin\theta)$$ When you can draw a perpendicular line from the pivot directly to the force's line of action.

Figure 1: Visual representation of torque $\tau$. The force $F$ acts at angle $\theta$ to lever arm $r$. Only the perpendicular component $F_\perp = F \sin\theta$ causes rotation!

Direction of Torque: Counter-Clockwise vs Clockwise

Torque tries to turn an object in one of two directions around a pivot:

  • Counter-Clockwise Torque ($\text{CCW}$): Turns the object against clock hands. By standard convention, CCW torques are treated as **positive (+)**.
  • Clockwise Torque ($\text{CW}$): Turns the object with clock hands. CW torques are treated as **negative (-)**.

Part 2: The Rules of Static Balance & Pivot Choice

1. The Two Conditions for Total Balance

For an object (like a bridge, sign, or ladder) to remain completely still in static equilibrium, it must satisfy two distinct balance rules simultaneously:

The Dual Conditions of Static Equilibrium

1. Translational Equilibrium: $$\sum F_x = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \sum F_y = 0$$

2. Rotational Equilibrium: $$\sum \tau_{\text{CCW}} = \sum \tau_{\text{CW}} \quad (\text{or } \sum \tau = 0)$$

2. Simple Proof: Why Pivot Choice Does Not Change Net Torque

Beginners often ask: "How do I know where to place the pivot point when solving a problem? Will I get a different answer if I pick a different pivot?"

The answer is NO! If a system is in static balance ($\sum F = 0$), calculating net torque about ANY point in space gives the exact same result ($0$).

Simple 1D Demonstration:

Imagine a balanced beam with two upward support forces $F_1$ and $F_2$, and a downward load $W$. Since it is in force balance:

$$F_1 + F_2 - W = 0 \implies F_1 + F_2 = W$$

Now, calculate net torque about Pivot $A$ (located at $F_1$):

$$\tau_A = (F_1 \cdot 0) + (F_2 \cdot L) - (W \cdot x) = F_2 L - W x = 0$$

Now suppose we shift our pivot point to a new location $B$, which is a distance $d$ away from $A$. The new lever arms become $(0 + d)$, $(L + d)$, and $(x + d)$:

$$\tau_B = F_1 d + F_2 (L + d) - W (x + d)$$ $$\tau_B = F_1 d + F_2 L + F_2 d - W x - W d$$ $$\tau_B = (F_2 L - W x) + d \cdot (F_1 + F_2 - W)$$

Notice the term in the second set of brackets! Since force balance requires $F_1 + F_2 - W = 0$, that entire term becomes zero ($d \cdot 0 = 0$):

$$\tau_B = (F_2 L - W x) + 0 = \tau_A = 0$$

Conclusion: You can place the pivot anywhere! Smart students choose the pivot at the location of an UNKNOWN force so that its lever arm is zero ($r = 0$), removing it from the torque equation entirely!

Part 3: Stepping Up — Center of Mass, Cables & Leaning Ladders

1. Center of Mass ($x_{\text{com}}$)

Where does gravity pull on an extended object? For a uniform beam, Earth pulls on every single atom, but mathematically, gravity acts as a single combined force ($m g$) acting at the **Center of Mass** ($x_{\text{com}}$), which is at the exact midpoint ($L/2$).

For non-uniform objects with multiple attached masses $m_1, m_2, \dots$ at positions $x_1, x_2, \dots$:

$$x_{\text{com}} = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2 + \dots + m_n x_n}{m_1 + m_2 + \dots + m_n}$$

2. The Leaning Ladder System

A classic exam scenario is a ladder leaning against a wall. The ladder experiences four distinct forces:

  • Gravity ($m g$): Acts downward at the ladder's center of mass ($L/2$).
  • Ground Normal Force ($N_g$): Pushes straight up from the floor to support the weight.
  • Ground Friction Force ($f_g$): Pushes horizontally towards the wall to prevent the base from slipping outwards.
  • Wall Normal Force ($N_w$): Pushes horizontally away from the vertical wall.

Four Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Standard See-saw Balance

[Basic Moment Equation]

A uniform $15.0\text{ kg}$ wooden plank of total length $L = 6.00\text{ m}$ is supported by a central fulcrum. Child A ($m_1 = 35.0\text{ kg}$) sits $r_1 = 2.40\text{ m}$ to the left of the pivot.

a) Calculate where Child B ($m_2 = 42.0\text{ kg}$) must sit to achieve exact balance.
b) Calculate the upward normal force $N$ exerted by the central fulcrum ($g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$).

Figure 3: Free-body setup for Worked Example 1. Rotational balance occurs when $\tau_{\text{CCW}} = \tau_{\text{CW}}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Finding Child B's Position ($r_2$):

Because the wooden plank is uniform, its weight acts at the midpoint, directly over the fulcrum ($r = 0$). Thus plank weight contributes zero torque!

Applying Rotational Equilibrium ($\sum \tau_{\text{CCW}} = \sum \tau_{\text{CW}}$):

$$m_1 g r_1 = m_2 g r_2$$

Cancelling $g$:

$$35.0 \times 2.40 = 42.0 \times r_2 \implies 84.0 = 42.0 \times r_2 \implies r_2 = \mathbf{2.00\text{ m to the right of the pivot}}$$

Part b) Fulcrum Normal Reaction Force ($N$):

Applying Vertical Force Balance ($\sum F_y = 0$):

$$N - m_{\text{plank}} g - m_1 g - m_2 g = 0$$ $$N = (15.0 + 35.0 + 42.0) \times 9.80 = 92.0 \times 9.80 = \mathbf{901.6\text{ N}} \approx \mathbf{902\text{ N}}$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium-Easy): Two-Support Bench Bridge

[Bridging 1D to 2D Systems]

A uniform wooden bench of mass $m = 20.0\text{ kg}$ and length $L = 4.00\text{ m}$ rests on two vertical legs at its ends (Leg A at $x = 0$, Leg B at $x = 4.00\text{ m}$). A person of mass $M = 60.0\text{ kg}$ sits on the bench at distance $x = 1.00\text{ m}$ from Leg A.

Calculate the vertical support forces $F_A$ and $F_B$ exerted by Leg A and Leg B.

Figure 4: Free-body diagram for Worked Example 2. Setting pivot at Leg A isolates support force $F_B$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Calculate $F_B$ by placing the pivot at Leg A ($x = 0$):

Placing pivot at Leg A sets $r_A = 0$, eliminating $F_A$ from our torque equation!

$$\sum \tau_A = 0 \implies F_B \times 4.00 - m g \times 2.00 - M g \times 1.00 = 0$$ $$4.00 F_B = (20.0 \times 9.80 \times 2.00) + (60.0 \times 9.80 \times 1.00)$$ $$4.00 F_B = 392 + 588 = 980 \implies F_B = \mathbf{245\text{ N Upward}}$$

Step 2: Calculate $F_A$ using Vertical Force Balance ($\sum F_y = 0$):

$$F_A + F_B = (m + M) g = (20.0 + 60.0) \times 9.80 = 784\text{ N}$$ $$F_A = 784 - 245 = \mathbf{539\text{ N Upward}}$$

Worked Example 3 (Medium-Hard): Hinged Wall Boom with Angled Cable

[Hinged Boom & Angled Tension]

A uniform horizontal steel beam of mass $m = 45.0\text{ kg}$ and length $L = 3.20\text{ m}$ is hinged to a wall at Point A. A heavy sign ($M = 85.0\text{ kg}$) hangs from the outer end (Point B). A support cable connects Point B to the wall at an angle $\theta = 35.0^\circ$ above the beam.

a) Calculate the tension force $T$ in the support cable.
b) Calculate the horizontal and vertical hinge forces ($H_x, H_y$) at Point A.

Figure 5: Scenario setup for Worked Example 3 showing hinge reaction forces, cable tension angle, and loads.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Cable Tension Force ($T$):

Set pivot at Hinge A ($r_A = 0$) to eliminate unknown hinge forces $H_x, H_y$:

$$\sum \tau_A = 0 \implies (T \sin 35.0^\circ) L - m g \left( \frac{L}{2} \right) - M g L = 0$$

Dividing by $L$:

$$T \sin 35.0^\circ = 9.80 \times (0.5 \times 45.0 + 85.0) = 9.80 \times 107.5 = 1053.5\text{ N}$$ $$T = \frac{1053.5}{\sin 35.0^\circ} = \frac{1053.5}{0.57358} = \mathbf{1837\text{ N}} \approx \mathbf{1.84\text{ kN}}$$

Part b) Hinge Reaction Forces ($H_x, H_y$):

1. Horizontal Balance ($\sum F_x = 0$): $H_x - T \cos 35.0^\circ = 0 \implies H_x = 1836.7 \times \cos 35.0^\circ = \mathbf{1505\text{ N}}$

2. Vertical Balance ($\sum F_y = 0$): $H_y + T \sin 35.0^\circ - m g - M g = 0 \implies H_y = 1274.0 - 1053.5 = \mathbf{220.5\text{ N Upward}}$

Worked Example 4 (Extension - Band 6 Separator): Leaning Ladder Slip Threshold

[2D Friction & Torque Synthesis]

A uniform ladder of length $L = 5.00\text{ m}$ and mass $m = 20.0\text{ kg}$ leans against a smooth vertical wall ($f_w = 0$) and rests on a rough floor ($\mu_s = 0.400$) at an angle $\theta = 60.0^\circ$ to the floor. A person of mass $M = 80.0\text{ kg}$ climbs up the ladder.

Calculate how far up the ladder ($x$ metres) the person can climb before the base begins to slip.

Figure 6: Complete Free-Body diagram for Worked Example 4 showing slip threshold force interactions.

Full Band 6 JRAHS Solution:

Step 1: Vertical Force Balance ($\sum F_y = 0$):

$$N_g = (m + M) g = (20.0 + 80.0) \times 9.80 = 980\text{ N}$$

Step 2: Maximum Available Ground Static Friction ($f_{g,\max}$):

$$f_{g,\max} = \mu_s N_g = 0.400 \times 980 = 392\text{ N}$$

Step 3: Horizontal Force Balance ($\sum F_x = 0$):

$$N_w = f_{g,\max} = 392\text{ N}$$

Step 4: Torque Balance about Ground Base Pivot ($\sum \tau_{\text{base}} = 0$):

$$N_w (L \sin 60.0^\circ) - m g \left( \frac{L}{2} \cos 60.0^\circ \right) - M g (x \cos 60.0^\circ) = 0$$

Substituting numerical values:

$$392 \times (5.00 \times 0.8660) - (20.0 \times 9.80 \times 2.50 \times 0.50) - (80.0 \times 9.80 \times 0.50) x = 0$$ $$1697.4 - 245.0 - 392.0 x = 0$$ $$1452.4 = 392.0 x \implies x = \frac{1452.4}{392.0} = \mathbf{3.70\text{ m up the ladder}}$$

Practise Question 1 (Easy): Tower Crane Counterweight Balance

[3 Marks]

A tower crane jib extends horizontally $r_1 = 25.0\text{ m}$ to the right of its central tower, holding a load of $M = 2500\text{ kg}$. The counterweight arm extends $r_2 = 8.00\text{ m}$ to the left.

Calculate the counterweight mass $m_c$ required to achieve exact rotational balance, ignoring jib structural mass.

Figure 7: Diagram for Practise Question 1 illustrating tower crane counterweight balance.

Hint: Apply $\tau_{\text{CCW}} = \tau_{\text{CW}} \implies m_c g r_2 = M g r_1$. Mass of counterweight $m_c = M r_1 / r_2$.

Applying rotational equilibrium about the central tower axis ($\sum \tau = 0$):

$$m_c g r_2 = M g r_1 \implies m_c r_2 = M r_1$$ $$m_c \times 8.00 = 2500 \times 25.0 = 62500 \implies m_c = \frac{62500}{8.00} = \mathbf{7812.5\text{ kg}} \approx \mathbf{7.81 \times 10^3\text{ kg}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"For rotational equilibrium about the tower axis, $\sum \tau = 0 \implies m_c g r_2 = M g r_1$. Substituting values gives $m_c (8.00) = (2500)(25.0) = 62500$, yielding required counterweight mass $m_c = 7810\text{ kg}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Sets up the moment balance principle $\tau_{\text{CCW}} = \tau_{\text{CW}}$.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly substitutes distances ($25.0\text{ m}$ and $8.00\text{ m}$).
  • 3 Marks: Obtains final answer $7810\text{ kg}$ with correct units and 3 significant figures.

Practise Question 2 (Medium): Hinged Wall Boom with Angled Tie-Cable

[4 Marks]

A horizontal boom of length $L = 4.00\text{ m}$ and mass $m = 60.0\text{ kg}$ is hinged to a wall. A tie-rod cable connects the outer end of the boom to the wall at an angle $\theta = 40.0^\circ$ above the boom. A load $M = 150.0\text{ kg}$ hangs from the end of the boom.

a) Calculate the tension force $T$ in the tie-rod cable.
b) Calculate the horizontal reaction force $H_x$ exerted by the hinge on the boom.

Figure 8: Diagram for Practise Question 2 representing hinged wall boom with angled tie-cable.

Hint: Take moments about the wall hinge: $(T \sin 40^\circ) L = m g (L/2) + M g L$. Then $H_x = T \cos 40^\circ$.

a) Cable Tension ($T$):

Taking moments about the hinge ($\sum \tau_{\text{hinge}} = 0$):

$$(T \sin 40.0^\circ) L = m g \left( \frac{L}{2} \right) + M g L$$ $$T \sin 40.0^\circ = 0.5 m g + M g = 9.80 \times (30.0 + 150.0) = 1764\text{ N}$$ $$T = \frac{1764}{\sin 40.0^\circ} = \frac{1764}{0.64279} = \mathbf{2744\text{ N}} \approx \mathbf{2.74\text{ kN}}$$

b) Horizontal Hinge Force ($H_x$):

By horizontal equilibrium ($\sum F_x = 0$):

$$H_x = T \cos 40.0^\circ = 2744.3 \times \cos 40.0^\circ = \mathbf{2102\text{ N}} \approx \mathbf{2.10\text{ kN}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Taking moments about the hinge eliminates hinge reaction forces: $(T \sin 40^\circ) L = m g (L/2) + M g L$. Dividing by $L$ gives $T \sin 40^\circ = 9.80(30 + 150) = 1764\text{ N}$, yielding tension $T = 2740\text{ N}$. Horizontal equilibrium requires $H_x = T \cos 40^\circ = 2744 \cos 40^\circ = 2100\text{ N}$ directed away from the wall."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Sets pivot at hinge to eliminate unknown reaction forces.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly decomposes cable tension into vertical component $T \sin 40^\circ$.
  • 3 Marks: Solves correctly for cable tension $T = 2740\text{ N}$.
  • 4 Marks: Calculates horizontal hinge force $H_x = 2100\text{ N}$.

Practise Question 3 (Hard): Asymmetric Bridge Support Loading

[5 Marks]

A non-uniform footbridge beam of length $L = 10.0\text{ m}$ and mass $M = 800\text{ kg}$ has its center of mass located $x_{\text{com}} = 4.00\text{ m}$ from Pier A ($x = 0$). Pier B is at $x = 10.0\text{ m}$. A group of pedestrians weighing $m_{\text{group}} = 500\text{ kg}$ stands at $x = 7.00\text{ m}$.

a) Calculate the vertical support force $F_A$ exerted by Pier A.
b) Calculate the vertical support force $F_B$ exerted by Pier B.
c) Determine where the pedestrians must stand so that both piers exert equal support forces ($F_A = F_B$).

Figure 9: Diagram for Practise Question 3 illustrating non-uniform beam COM and asymmetric pedestrian loading.

Hint: Take moments about Pier B to find $F_A$. For part c, equal support forces mean $F_A = F_B = (M + m_{\text{group}}) g / 2$.

a) Support Force at Pier A ($F_A$):

Taking moments about Pier B ($x = 10.0\text{ m}$):

$$F_A \times 10.0 - M g \times (10.0 - 4.00) - m_{\text{group}} g \times (10.0 - 7.00) = 0$$ $$10.0 F_A = (800 \times 9.80 \times 6.00) + (500 \times 9.80 \times 3.00) = 47040 + 14700 = 61740 \implies F_A = \mathbf{6174\text{ N}}$$

b) Support Force at Pier B ($F_B$):

$$F_B = (800 + 500) \times 9.80 - 6174 = 12740 - 6174 = \mathbf{6566\text{ N}}$$

c) Pedestrian Position $x_{\text{ped}}$ for $F_A = F_B$:

For equal loads, $F_A = F_B = 6370\text{ N}$. Taking moments about Pier A ($x = 0$):

$$6370 \times 10.0 - (800 \times 9.80 \times 4.00) - (500 \times 9.80) x_{\text{ped}} = 0$$ $$63700 - 31360 = 4900 x_{\text{ped}} \implies 32340 = 4900 x_{\text{ped}} \implies x_{\text{ped}} = \mathbf{6.60\text{ m from Pier A}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Taking moments about Pier B gives $10 F_A = 800(9.80)(6.0) + 500(9.80)(3.0) = 61740\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} \implies F_A = 6170\text{ N}$. By vertical balance $F_B = (1300)(9.80) - 6174 = 6570\text{ N}$. For equal loads $F_A = F_B = 6370\text{ N}$, moments about Pier A gives $6370(10.0) - 800(9.80)(4.0) - 500(9.80) x_{\text{ped}} = 0$, yielding pedestrian position $x_{\text{ped}} = 6.60\text{ m}$ from Pier A."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Sets up moment balance accounting for non-uniform COM at $4.00\text{ m}$.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates force at Pier A $F_A = 6170\text{ N}$.
  • 3 Marks: Calculates force at Pier B $F_B = 6570\text{ N}$.
  • 4 Marks: Sets $F_A = F_B = 6370\text{ N}$ for equal pier loading.
  • 5 Marks: Solves correctly for pedestrian position $x = 6.60\text{ m}$.

Practise Question 4 (JRAHS Extension): Critical Inclination Angle for Uniform Rod Leaning on Smooth Peg

[6 Marks]

A uniform rod of length $L$ and mass $m$ rests with its lower end on a rough floor ($\mu_s = 0.500$) and leans against a smooth horizontal peg at height $h = 0.600 L$ above the floor.

a) Draw a complete Free-Body Diagram indicating all contact forces ($N_f, f_f, N_{\text{peg}}, m g$).
b) Derive an expression for the normal reaction force $N_{\text{peg}}$ as a function of angle $\theta$.
c) Calculate the minimum inclination angle $\theta_{\min}$ to the horizontal for which the rod remains in static equilibrium without slipping.

Figure 10: Complete Free-Body diagram for Practise Question 4 showing perpendicular peg normal force.

Hint: Peg distance along rod is $d = h / \sin\theta$. Take moments about base: $N_{\text{peg}} (h / \sin\theta) = m g (L/2) \cos\theta$.

Full Band 6 JRAHS Solution:

a) Free-Body Force Breakdown:

  • Ground Normal $N_f$ (Vertically Upward at base).
  • Ground Static Friction $f_f$ (Horizontally Towards Wall at base).
  • Peg Reaction Normal $N_{\text{peg}}$ (Perpendicular to rod at distance $d = h / \sin\theta$).
  • Gravity $m g$ (Vertically Downward at midpoint $L/2$).

b) Deriving $N_{\text{peg}}(\theta)$:

Taking moments about base pivot ($\sum \tau_{\text{base}} = 0$):

$$N_{\text{peg}} \times \left( \frac{h}{\sin\theta} \right) - m g \left( \frac{L}{2} \cos\theta \right) = 0 \implies N_{\text{peg}} = \frac{m g L \sin\theta \cos\theta}{2 h}$$

For $h = 0.600 L$:

$$N_{\text{peg}} = \frac{m g \sin\theta \cos\theta}{2 (0.600)} = \mathbf{\frac{5}{6} m g \sin\theta \cos\theta}$$

c) Minimum Angle $\theta_{\min}$:

Resolving forces horizontally and vertically:

$$\Sigma F_x = 0 \implies f_f = N_{\text{peg}} \sin\theta$$ $$\Sigma F_y = 0 \implies N_f = m g - N_{\text{peg}} \cos\theta$$

At slip threshold ($f_f = \mu_s N_f = 0.500 N_f$):

$$N_{\text{peg}} \sin\theta = 0.500 (m g - N_{\text{peg}} \cos\theta) \implies N_{\text{peg}} (\sin\theta + 0.500 \cos\theta) = 0.500 m g$$

Substituting $N_{\text{peg}} = \frac{5}{6} m g \sin\theta \cos\theta$ and dividing by $m g$:

$$\frac{5}{6} \sin\theta \cos\theta (\sin\theta + 0.500 \cos\theta) = 0.500$$ $$5 \sin\theta \cos\theta (\sin\theta + 0.500 \cos\theta) = 3.00$$

Solving numerically for $\theta_{\min}$ gives $\mathbf{\theta_{\min} = 48.6^\circ}$.

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Moments about the base yield $N_{\text{peg}} (h / \sin\theta) = m g (L/2) \cos\theta \implies N_{\text{peg}} = \frac{m g L \sin\theta \cos\theta}{2 h} = \frac{5}{6} m g \sin\theta \cos\theta$. Resolving horizontal and vertical forces gives $f_f = N_{\text{peg}} \sin\theta$ and $N_f = m g - N_{\text{peg}} \cos\theta$. Applying slip condition $f_f = \mu_f N_f$ yields equation $\frac{5}{6} \sin\theta \cos\theta (\sin\theta + 0.5 \cos\theta) = 0.5$. Solving numerically yields minimum stable inclination angle $\theta_{\min} = 48.6^\circ$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly draws free-body diagram showing perpendicular peg normal force.
  • 2 Marks: Expresses peg distance along rod $d = h / \sin\theta$.
  • 3 Marks: Derives expression for $N_{\text{peg}} = \frac{5}{6} m g \sin\theta \cos\theta$.
  • 4 Marks: Sets up force balance equations $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$.
  • 5 Marks: Applies static friction slip threshold condition $f_f = \mu_s N_f$.
  • 6 Marks: Solves trigonometric relation to obtain $\theta_{\min} = 48.6^\circ$.
