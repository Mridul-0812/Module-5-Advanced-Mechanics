05. First-Hand Investigation & Spreadsheet Modelling

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 1

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Inquiry Question

Inquiry Question 1: How can models that are used to explain projectile motion be used to analyse and make predictions?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus, students must master the following practical and computational outcomes:

  • Conduct a first-hand scientific investigation to collect quantitative data on the two-dimensional trajectory of a projectile (e.g. using video motion analysis software, photogate arrays, or strobe photography).
  • Analyse experimental data to determine horizontal and vertical displacement components ($s_x, s_y$), velocity components ($v_x, v_y$), and local acceleration due to gravity ($g$).
  • Construct a computational spreadsheet model using discrete time steps ($\Delta t$) and the finite-difference method (Euler's integration technique) to predict position and velocity over time.
  • Compare analytical $SUVAT$ predictions, computational spreadsheet models, and experimental first-hand data to evaluate model validity, numerical truncation errors, and physical limitations (such as air resistance).
  • Evaluate sources of systematic and random error in experimental setups and assess method reliability and accuracy.

Deep Dive Part A: Experimental Laboratory Investigation

1. Scientific Purpose & Equipment Setup

To scientifically test the independence of horizontal and vertical motion, students conduct a primary laboratory investigation. A standard setup involves launching a steel marble from a curved metal ramp mounted on a benchtop, capturing its two-dimensional flight against a calibrated vertical grid background using a high-speed digital camera (120 or 240 frames per second).

Core Experimental Components & Variables

  • Independent Variable: Time elapsed $t$ (s), measured in discrete frame increments ($\Delta t = 1 / \text{Frame Rate}$).
  • Dependent Variables: Horizontal displacement $s_x$ (m) and vertical displacement $s_y$ (m), extracted frame-by-frame.
  • Controlled Variables: Launch height off ramp, initial launch velocity $u$, mass of projectile, zero wind currents, and camera angle/distance.
  • Calibration Scale: A vertical $1.00\text{ m}$ ruler placed in the exact plane of motion to establish a pixel-to-metre conversion factor ($\text{Scale} = \text{pixels} / \text{metre}$).
  • Plumb Line & Spirit Level: Used to ensure the grid backdrop is perfectly vertical and the launching bench is horizontally aligned.

2. Data Processing & Graphical Extraction of Acceleration ($g$)

From the digitized video frames, coordinates $(x_i, y_i)$ are recorded at each time increment $t_i$. The raw position data is then analysed graphically using three standard diagnostic plots:

Graphical Analysis Procedures

  1. Horizontal Displacement vs Time ($s_x$ vs $t$): Yields a straight line passing through the origin. The gradient represents the constant horizontal launch speed: $$\text{Gradient} = \frac{\Delta s_x}{\Delta t} = u_x$$
  2. Vertical Velocity vs Time ($v_y$ vs $t$): Instantaneous vertical velocity is computed using central finite differences ($v_{y,i} = \frac{y_{i+1} - y_{i-1}}{2\Delta t}$). Plotting $v_y$ against $t$ produces a straight line with a negative gradient: $$\text{Gradient} = \frac{\Delta v_y}{\Delta t} = a_y = -g_{\text{exp}}$$ Comparing $g_{\text{exp}}$ to the accepted value ($9.80\text{ m/s}^2$) allows calculation of percentage error: $$\text{Percentage Error} = \left| \frac{g_{\text{exp}} - 9.80}{9.80} \right| \times 100\%$$
  3. Vertical Displacement vs Horizontal Displacement ($s_y$ vs $s_x$): Displays the physical parabolic spatial path. Fitting a quadratic function $y = A x^2 + B x + C$ yields an experimental coefficient $A = -\frac{g}{2 u_x^2}$, providing a secondary check on $g$.

3. Evaluating Experimental Validity, Accuracy, Reliability & Errors

NESA HSC examinations frequently assess student capacity to critique practical methodologies. Below is a structured summary of experimental error analysis:

Scientific Category Experimental Definition Specific Laboratory Causes in Projectile Motion Improvement & Mitigation Strategy
Parallax Error Systematic reading error caused by viewing calibration scales at an oblique angle. Camera lens not aligned perpendicularly to the centre of the plane of motion. Position camera axis precisely perpendicular to motion plane; place calibration scale in identical optical plane.
Lens Distortion Optical distortion warping pixel distances near frame edges (barrel distortion). Wide-angle camera lenses warping grid boundaries. Use telephoto zoom from further away; apply optical lens-correction software filters during video analysis.
Motion Blur Uncertainty in pinpointing projectile centre of mass due to finite exposure time. Slow shutter speed causing streaking of fast-moving marble across frames. Increase camera shutter speed ($> 1/1000\text{ s}$) and increase background illumination.
Frictional Energy Loss Non-zero external work reducing initial kinetic energy prior to launch. Friction between marble and launch track; rotational energy consumption. Use low-friction photogates at track exit to measure true $u$ directly at the instant of release.
Aerodynamic Drag Air resistance opposing motion, causing trajectory deviation from ideal $SUVAT$. Air resistance acting on light projectiles (e.g. table tennis balls). Use dense, small, spherical steel marbles to minimise surface-area-to-mass ratio ($A/m$).

Deep Dive Part B: Spreadsheet Modelling & Finite-Difference Step Integration

1. The Need for Computational Numerical Modelling

While analytical $SUVAT$ equations perfectly predict projectile paths in ideal vacuums, real-world engineering problems (such as ballistics, sports science, and atmospheric re-entry) involve complex forces like velocity-dependent air drag ($F_D \propto v^2$). Under drag, differential equations of motion become non-linear and cannot be solved algebraically without advanced calculus.

A computational spreadsheet model bypasses complex calculus by breaking continuous time into small, discrete increments ($\Delta t$). Using Euler's Finite-Difference Method, the spreadsheet calculates acceleration at the current instant, updates velocity for the next step, and then updates position iteratively.

2. Mathematical Derivation of Euler's Step-Integration Algorithm

Consider a time step interval $\Delta t$ (e.g. $0.01\text{ s}$). At step $i$, the projectile has known position $(x_i, y_i)$ and velocity $(v_{x,i}, v_{y,i})$.

Iterative Step Formulas

Step 1: Compute Acceleration at Step $i$:

For ideal vacuum motion:

$$a_{x,i} = 0\text{ m/s}^2 \quad \text{and} \quad a_{y,i} = -g = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$$

If linear air drag ($F_D = -k v$) is included:

$$a_{x,i} = -\frac{k}{m} v_{x,i} \quad \text{and} \quad a_{y,i} = -g - \frac{k}{m} v_{y,i}$$

Step 2: Update Velocity Components for Step $i+1$:

$$v_{x,i+1} = v_{x,i} + a_{x,i} \Delta t$$ $$v_{y,i+1} = v_{y,i} + a_{y,i} \Delta t$$

Step 3: Update Displacement Positions for Step $i+1$:

$$s_{x,i+1} = s_{x,i} + v_{x,i} \Delta t$$ $$s_{y,i+1} = s_{y,i} + v_{y,i} \Delta t$$

Step 4: Advance Time Counter:

$$t_{i+1} = t_i + \Delta t$$

4. Truncation Error vs Step Size $\Delta t$ Optimisation

Because Euler's method assumes acceleration and velocity remain constant across each interval $\Delta t$, it introduces a small systematic mathematical error known as discretisation truncation error.

Selecting the Optimal Time Step ($\Delta t$)

If $\Delta t$ is too large (e.g. $\Delta t = 0.50\text{ s}$): The spreadsheet overestimates distance travelled because velocity changes rapidly within half a second. The calculated parabolic path systematically drifts above the true path (accumulation of truncation error).

If $\Delta t$ is too small (e.g. $\Delta t = 0.000001\text{ s}$): The spreadsheet requires millions of computational rows, increasing processor load and introducing floating-point round-off error.

Optimal Computational Range: For HSC Physics models, choosing $\Delta t$ between $0.001\text{ s}$ and $0.01\text{ s}$ keeps numerical truncation error below $0.1\%$, rendering the spreadsheet model practically identical to exact theoretical $SUVAT$ equations!

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Manual Construction of First Two Integration Steps

[Computational Algorithm Fundamentals]

A projectile is launched from ground level with initial horizontal velocity $u_x = 15.0\text{ m/s}$ and initial vertical velocity $u_y = 20.0\text{ m/s}$. Using Euler's finite-difference method with a discrete time step $\Delta t = 0.10\text{ s}$ and $g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$ (assuming zero air resistance), manually calculate the position coordinates $(s_x, s_y)$ and velocity components $(v_x, v_y)$ at $t = 0.10\text{ s}$ and $t = 0.20\text{ s}$. Show all step formulas explicitly.

Step 1: Initial State ($t_0 = 0.00\text{ s}$)

$$s_{x,0} = 0.00\text{ m}, \quad s_{y,0} = 0.00\text{ m}, \quad v_{x,0} = 15.0\text{ m/s}, \quad v_{y,0} = 20.0\text{ m/s}$$

$$a_{x,0} = 0.00\text{ m/s}^2, \quad a_{y,0} = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$$

Step 2: Compute Step 1 ($t_1 = 0.10\text{ s}$)

Updating velocities:

$$v_{x,1} = v_{x,0} + a_{x,0} \Delta t = 15.0 + (0)(0.10) = \mathbf{15.0\text{ m/s}}$$

$$v_{y,1} = v_{y,0} + a_{y,0} \Delta t = 20.0 + (-9.80)(0.10) = 20.0 - 0.98 = \mathbf{19.02\text{ m/s}}$$

Updating displacements:

$$s_{x,1} = s_{x,0} + v_{x,0} \Delta t = 0.00 + (15.0)(0.10) = \mathbf{1.50\text{ m}}$$

$$s_{y,1} = s_{y,0} + v_{y,0} \Delta t = 0.00 + (20.0)(0.10) = \mathbf{2.00\text{ m}}$$

Step 3: Compute Step 2 ($t_2 = 0.20\text{ s}$)

Updating velocities:

$$v_{x,2} = v_{x,1} + a_{x,1} \Delta t = 15.0 + (0)(0.10) = \mathbf{15.0\text{ m/s}}$$

$$v_{y,2} = v_{y,1} + a_{y,1} \Delta t = 19.02 + (-9.80)(0.10) = 19.02 - 0.98 = \mathbf{18.04\text{ m/s}}$$

Updating displacements:

$$s_{x,2} = s_{x,1} + v_{x,1} \Delta t = 1.50 + (15.0)(0.10) = \mathbf{3.00\text{ m}}$$

$$s_{y,2} = s_{y,1} + v_{y,1} \Delta t = 2.00 + (19.02)(0.10) = 2.00 + 1.902 = \mathbf{3.902\text{ m}}$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Video Analysis Data Processing & Percentage Error

[Experimental Data Analysis]

A student uses video analysis software (recording at 60 frames per second) to track a ball launched off a bench. The table below displays extracted time $t$ and vertical velocity $v_y$ data points recorded during its flight:

Frame Number Time $t$ (s) Vertical Velocity $v_y$ (m/s)
00.000+4.50
60.100+3.54
120.200+2.58
180.300+1.59
240.400+0.62

a) Construct a linear gradient model to determine the experimental acceleration due to gravity ($g_{\text{exp}}$).
b) Calculate the percentage error relative to the accepted value ($9.80\text{ m/s}^2$).
c) Identify two physical sources of experimental error that could explain any discrepancy.

Step 1: Calculate Gradient of $v_y$ vs $t$ Data

Select two widely separated data points from the linear set: $(t_1 = 0.000\text{ s}, v_{y,1} = +4.50\text{ m/s})$ and $(t_2 = 0.400\text{ s}, v_{y,2} = +0.62\text{ m/s})$.

$$\text{Gradient} = \frac{v_{y,2} - v_{y,1}}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{0.62 - 4.50}{0.400 - 0.000} = \frac{-3.88}{0.400} = -9.70\text{ m/s}^2$$

Therefore, experimental acceleration $a_y = -9.70\text{ m/s}^2 \implies g_{\text{exp}} = \mathbf{9.70\text{ m/s}^2}$.

Step 2: Calculate Percentage Error
$$\text{Percentage Error} = \left| \frac{g_{\text{exp}} - g_{\text{accepted}}}{g_{\text{accepted}}} \right| \times 100\% = \left| \frac{9.70 - 9.80}{9.80} \right| \times 100\% = \frac{0.10}{9.80} \times 100\% = \mathbf{1.02\%}$$
Step 3: Identify Physical Error Sources
  • 1. Aerodynamic Drag: Air resistance exerts a upward force opposing downward motion, slightly reducing net downward acceleration below $9.80\text{ m/s}^2$.
  • 2. Optical Frame Rate Timing Jitter: Camera frame rate variations ($\pm 1\text{ frame}$) introduce systematic time measurement uncertainty.

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Spreadsheet Integration with Linear Air Drag

[Non-Linear Numerical Modeling]

A $0.20\text{ kg}$ projectile is launched horizontally at $u_x = 25.0\text{ m/s}$ from height $h = 20.0\text{ m}$. It experiences linear drag given by $\vec{F}_D = -k \vec{v}$, where resistance coefficient $k = 0.040\text{ N}\cdot\text{s/m}$. Using step size $\Delta t = 0.050\text{ s}$, compute the horizontal acceleration $a_{x,0}$ and vertical acceleration $a_{y,0}$ at $t = 0$, and determine updated velocity components $(v_{x,1}, v_{y,1})$ at $t = 0.050\text{ s}$.

Step 1: Compute Initial Forces and Accelerations at $t = 0$

At $t = 0$, $v_{x,0} = 25.0\text{ m/s}$ and $v_{y,0} = 0.00\text{ m/s}$. Mass $m = 0.20\text{ kg}$, $k = 0.040\text{ N s/m}$.

Horizontal Force & Acceleration:

$$F_{x,0} = -k v_{x,0} = -(0.040)(25.0) = -1.00\text{ N}$$ $$a_{x,0} = \frac{F_{x,0}}{m} = \frac{-1.00}{0.20} = \mathbf{-5.00\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Vertical Force & Acceleration:

$$F_{y,0} = -m g - k v_{y,0} = -(0.20)(9.80) - (0.040)(0.00) = -1.96\text{ N}$$ $$a_{y,0} = \frac{F_{y,0}}{m} = \frac{-1.96}{0.20} = \mathbf{-9.80\text{ m/s}^2}$$
Step 2: Perform Step Integration for $t_1 = 0.050\text{ s}$
$$v_{x,1} = v_{x,0} + a_{x,0} \Delta t = 25.0 + (-5.00)(0.050) = 25.0 - 0.25 = \mathbf{24.75\text{ m/s}}$$ $$v_{y,1} = v_{y,0} + a_{y,0} \Delta t = 0.00 + (-9.80)(0.050) = 0.00 - 0.49 = \mathbf{-0.49\text{ m/s}}$$

Notice how air resistance causes horizontal velocity to decrease from $25.0\text{ m/s}$ to $24.75\text{ m/s}$, breaking the constant horizontal velocity rule of ideal vacuums!

Worked Example 4 (Extension): Evaluating Discretisation Truncation Error

[Computational Error Analysis]

A ball dropped from rest under gravity ($a_y = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$) travels downward for $t = 1.00\text{ s}$.

a) Calculate the exact analytical displacement $s_y$ using $SUVAT$.
b) Calculate the numerical displacement $s_y$ predicted by Euler's method using a single coarse step $\Delta t = 1.00\text{ s}$.
c) Calculate the numerical displacement $s_y$ predicted by Euler's method using two fine steps $\Delta t = 0.50\text{ s}$.
d) Explain why reducing step size $\Delta t$ reduces computational truncation error.

Part a) Exact Analytical SUVAT Calculation:
$$s_{y,\text{exact}} = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2 = 0(1.00) + \frac{1}{2}(-9.80)(1.00)^2 = \mathbf{-4.90\text{ m}}$$
Part b) Euler Method with Single Step ($\Delta t = 1.00\text{ s}$):

At $t=0$, $v_{y,0} = 0\text{ m/s}$.

$$s_{y,1} = s_{y,0} + v_{y,0} \Delta t = 0.00 + (0.00)(1.00) = \mathbf{0.00\text{ m}}\quad (\text{Error} = 100\%!)$$
Part c) Euler Method with Two Steps ($\Delta t = 0.50\text{ s}$):

Step 1 ($t_1 = 0.50\text{ s}$):

$$v_{y,1} = 0 + (-9.80)(0.50) = -4.90\text{ m/s}$$ $$s_{y,1} = 0 + (0.00)(0.50) = 0.00\text{ m}$$

Step 2 ($t_2 = 1.00\text{ s}$):

$$s_{y,2} = s_{y,1} + v_{y,1} \Delta t = 0.00 + (-4.90)(0.50) = \mathbf{-2.45\text{ m}}\quad (\text{Error reduced to } 50\%)$$
Part d) Physical & Mathematical Explanation:

Euler's method assumes velocity remains constant at its initial value over each interval $\Delta t$. Because gravity continuously accelerates the object throughout the interval, using a large $\Delta t$ ignores the velocity increase that occurs inside the step. As $\Delta t \to 0$, the step approximation approaches continuous calculus integration, eliminating truncation error.

Practise Question 2 (Medium): Video Motion Analysis & Error Identification

[4 Marks]

A physics class uses high-speed video analysis to track a marble launched horizontally. The experimental horizontal velocity plot ($v_x$ vs $t$) exhibits a slight downward slope, dropping from $4.00\text{ m/s}$ at $t=0$ to $3.70\text{ m/s}$ at $t=0.50\text{ s}$.

a) Explain why ideal theory predicts a zero slope for $v_x$ vs $t$.
b) Identify the physical cause of the observed downward slope.
c) Calculate the magnitude of the experimental horizontal deceleration.

Hint: Ideal theory assumes zero horizontal force ($\Sigma F_x = 0$). Real air drag exerts a retarding force $F_D = -k v_x$. Deceleration is gradient $\frac{\Delta v_x}{\Delta t}$.

a) Theoretical Prediction:

Ideal projectile models assume zero net horizontal force ($\Sigma F_x = 0$). By Newton's First Law, acceleration $a_x = 0\text{ m/s}^2$, predicting a constant horizontal velocity and a zero-slope horizontal line on a $v_x-t$ graph.

b) Physical Cause:

Aerodynamic drag (air resistance) exerts a retarding force opposing horizontal motion, creating a non-zero horizontal deceleration ($a_x < 0$).

c) Deceleration Calculation:

$$a_x = \frac{\Delta v_x}{\Delta t} = \frac{3.70 - 4.00}{0.50 - 0.00} = \frac{-0.30}{0.50} = \mathbf{-0.60\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Magnitude of horizontal deceleration is $\mathbf{0.60\text{ m/s}^2}$.

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Ideal projectile theory assumes zero horizontal force ($\Sigma F_x = 0$), yielding $a_x = 0\text{ m/s}^2$ and a horizontal $v_x-t$ line. The observed negative gradient is caused by air resistance opposing horizontal velocity. Calculating the gradient $\frac{3.70 - 4.00}{0.50 - 0} = -0.60\text{ m/s}^2$ gives an experimental horizontal deceleration of $0.60\text{ m/s}^2$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Explains ideal theory assumption ($\Sigma F_x = 0 \implies a_x = 0$).
  • 2 Marks: Identifies air resistance as the physical cause of deceleration.
  • 3 Marks: Applies gradient formula correctly.
  • 4 Marks: Computes correct horizontal deceleration magnitude ($0.60\text{ m/s}^2$) with correct units.

Practise Question 3 (Hard): Evaluating Step Size & Truncation Error

[5 Marks]

A student creates two spreadsheet models (Model A with $\Delta t = 0.20\text{ s}$ and Model B with $\Delta t = 0.01\text{ s}$) to model a ball launched vertically upward at $19.6\text{ m/s}$.

a) Calculate the exact theoretical apex time using $SUVAT$.
b) Determine which model (A or B) predicts the maximum apex height with greater accuracy, explaining the mathematical mechanism of truncation error.
c) Describe the trade-off of choosing an excessively small time step ($\Delta t = 0.000001\text{ s}$).

Hint: Exact apex time occurs when $v_y = 0 \implies t = u_y / g$. Smaller step size $\Delta t$ reduces Euler truncation error because velocity changes within the step are smaller.

a) Exact Theoretical Apex Time:

$$v_y = u_y + a_y t \implies 0 = 19.6 - 9.80 t \implies t = \frac{19.6}{9.80} = \mathbf{2.00\text{ s}}$$

b) Model Accuracy & Truncation Error Mechanism:

Model B ($\Delta t = 0.01\text{ s}$) is significantly more accurate. Euler's method assumes velocity remains constant over each time step $\Delta t$. In reality, velocity changes continuously under gravity. Large time steps ($\Delta t = 0.20\text{ s}$) accumulate significant discretisation truncation error across each step. Smaller time steps ($\Delta t = 0.01\text{ s}$) make the constant-velocity assumption valid over the tiny interval, matching true calculus integration.

c) Trade-Off of Excessively Small Time Step:

While extremely small steps reduce truncation error, they dramatically increase the required row count (millions of rows), increasing computer processing time and introducing floating-point round-off errors in memory.

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"The exact apex time is $t = \frac{19.6}{9.80} = 2.00\text{ s}$. Model B ($\Delta t = 0.01\text{ s}$) is vastly more accurate because Euler step-integration assumes constant velocity over $\Delta t$. Large steps ($\Delta t = 0.20\text{ s}$) cause systematic truncation error as velocity changes within each step are ignored. However, an excessively small step ($\Delta t = 10^{-6}\text{ s}$) requires excessive computational memory and risks floating-point rounding errors."

Marking Criteria:

Practise Question 4 (Extension): Spreadsheet Algorithm with Quadratic Air Drag

[6 Marks]

A spherical projectile of mass $m = 0.50\text{ kg}$ is dropped from rest ($u = 0\text{ m/s}$) under gravity ($g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$). It experiences quadratic air drag given by $F_D = c v^2$, where drag constant $c = 0.020\text{ N}\cdot\text{s}^2/\text{m}^2$.

a) Derive the theoretical terminal velocity $v_T$ where net acceleration becomes zero.
b) Construct the explicit Euler update equations for $a_{y,i}$, $v_{y,i+1}$, and $s_{y,i+1}$.
c) Manually compute $a_{y,1}$, $v_{y,1}$, and $s_{y,1}$ for the first step ($\Delta t = 0.10\text{ s}$).

Hint: Terminal velocity occurs when drag force equals weight force ($c v_T^2 = m g \implies v_T = \sqrt{\frac{mg}{c}}$). At step 0, $v=0 \implies a_{y,0} = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$. Update $v_{y,1} = 0 + (-9.80)(0.10) = -0.98\text{ m/s}$.

a) Terminal Velocity Derivation:

At terminal velocity, net acceleration $a_y = 0 \implies \Sigma F_y = 0$.

$$m g = c v_T^2 \implies v_T^2 = \frac{m g}{c} = \frac{(0.50)(9.80)}{0.020} = \frac{4.90}{0.020} = 245.0$$ $$v_T = \sqrt{245.0} = \mathbf{15.7\text{ m/s}}$$

b) Euler Update Formulas:

$$a_{y,i} = -g + \frac{c}{m} v_{y,i}^2$$ $$v_{y,i+1} = v_{y,i} + a_{y,i} \Delta t$$ $$s_{y,i+1} = s_{y,i} + v_{y,i} \Delta t$$

c) First Step Computation ($t_1 = 0.10\text{ s}$):

At $t=0$, $v_{y,0} = 0\text{ m/s}$.

$$a_{y,0} = -9.80 + \frac{0.020}{0.50}(0)^2 = \mathbf{-9.80\text{ m/s}^2}$$ $$v_{y,1} = 0.00 + (-9.80)(0.10) = \mathbf{-0.980\text{ m/s}}$$ $$s_{y,1} = 0.00 + (0.00)(0.10) = \mathbf{0.00\text{ m}}$$
