04. Asymmetrical & Height Launches

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 1

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Inquiry Question

Inquiry Question 1: How can models that are used to explain projectile motion be used to analyse and make predictions?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus, students must master the following specific outcomes regarding non-zero initial height launches:

  • Analyse the motion of projectiles launched from elevated origin points ($y_0 \neq 0$) across three distinct launch configurations: horizontal launches ($\theta = 0^\circ$), upward angled launches ($\theta > 0^\circ$), and downward angled launches ($\theta < 0^\circ$).
  • Quantitative calculation of asymmetrical flight dynamics where landing displacement $s_y$ is non-zero relative to the origin ($s_y \neq 0$).
  • Evaluation of landing conditions using energy conservation principles ($E_k + E_p = \text{constant}$) alongside vector resolution to differentiate between impact speed magnitude and impact vector direction angle.
  • Application of the quadratic formula to establish non-symmetric time of flight parameters ($t_{\text{flight}}$) and physical interpretation of positive versus negative time roots.
  • Solving complex multi-stage boundary problems involving elevated launching ramps, cliff obstacles, and sloping target landing zones using the systematic UVTAXY framework.

Deep Dive: Breaking Symmetry in Projectile Trajectories

1. Why Ground-to-Ground Formulas Completely Fail on Cliffs

In standard ground-to-ground projectile motion where launch elevation matches landing elevation ($y_{\text{initial}} = y_{\text{final}}$), the trajectory is perfectly symmetrical. Under those specific conditions, the time taken to reach the apex peak equals the time taken to descend from the apex to the ground ($t_{\text{ascent}} = t_{\text{descent}}$), and the vertical landing velocity equals the negative of the initial vertical velocity ($v_y = -u_y$).

However, when a projectile is launched from an elevated cliff edge, a hilltop, or an aircraft at initial height $h_0$, this symmetry breaks down entirely:

The Fundamental Breakdown of Trajectory Symmetry

  • Asymmetrical Flight Times: The time spent descending from the apex peak to the lower ground level ($t_{\text{descent}}$) is strictly greater than the time spent ascending to the apex ($t_{\text{ascent}}$). Therefore, total flight time $t_{\text{total}} > 2 \times t_{\text{apex}}$.
  • Asymmetrical Impact Velocities: The vertical component of velocity at impact ($v_y$) is significantly larger in magnitude than the initial vertical launch velocity ($u_y$). That is, $|v_y| > |u_y|$.
  • Formula Failure Warning: The quick-formula shortcuts derived for symmetrical launches—namely $R = \frac{u^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}$, $t = \frac{2u\sin\theta}{g}$, and $s_{y,\text{max}} = \frac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}$—are invalid and strictly forbidden for cliff launches. Attempting to use them will yield completely incorrect answers in NESA HSC examinations.

2. Categorising Non-Zero Initial Height Launch Geometries

When solving elevated launch problems, you will encounter three distinct trigonometric initial conditions. Understanding how launch angle $\theta$ shapes the UVTAXY initial state is paramount:

Launch Configuration Launch Angle $\theta$ Initial Horizontal Velocity ($u_x$) Initial Vertical Velocity ($u_y$) Physical Behaviour at Launch
1. Horizontal Launch $\theta = 0^\circ$ $u_x = u \cos(0^\circ) = u$ $u_y = u \sin(0^\circ) = 0\text{ m/s}$ No initial upward motion. The launch point $(0, h_0)$ acts as the parabolic apex peak!
2. Upward Angled Launch $\theta > 0^\circ$ (Positive) $u_x = u \cos\theta > 0$ $u_y = u \sin\theta > 0$ Projectile rises to an apex peak above cliff height before descending all the way to ground level.
3. Downward Angled Launch $\theta < 0^\circ$ (Negative) $u_x = u \cos(-\theta) > 0$ $u_y = -u \sin(|\theta|) < 0$ Initial vertical velocity points downward. The projectile accelerates immediately toward the ground without rising.

3. Sign Conventions in UVTAXY for Elevated Origin Points

The single most common source of student error in HSC Physics elevated launch problems is improper algebraic sign assignment. Establishing a consistent Cartesian coordinate frame at the exact launch point $(0,0)$ is vital:

Mastering the UVTAXY Cartesian Coordinate Rules

  1. Origin Location: Always set the origin $(0,0)$ at the exact point of launch (the cliff top edge).
  2. Vertical Displacement ($s_y$): Because the landing zone is located a distance $h_0$ below the launch height, the vertical displacement at impact is negative: $$s_y = -h_0$$ Example: If a cliff is $45.0\text{ m}$ high, substitute $s_y = -45.0\text{ m}$ into $SUVAT$ equations!
  3. Vertical Acceleration ($a_y$): Earth's gravitational acceleration acts downward toward the centre of Earth: $$a_y = -g = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$$
  4. Horizontal Acceleration ($a_x$): Zero drag assumption means horizontal velocity is constant: $$a_x = 0\text{ m/s}^2$$

4. Deriving Flight Time via the Quadratic Formula

To determine the flight time $t$ for an upward angled launch off a cliff of height $h_0$, we substitute $s_y = -h_0$, $u_y = u\sin\theta$, and $a_y = -g$ into the fundamental vertical displacement equation:

$$s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2 \implies -h_0 = (u\sin\theta)t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$$

Rearranging this relation into standard quadratic form $A t^2 + B t + C = 0$ yields:

$$\left(\frac{1}{2}g\right)t^2 - (u\sin\theta)t - h_0 = 0$$

Applying the algebraic quadratic formula $t = \frac{-B \pm \sqrt{B^2 - 4AC}}{2A}$ produces two mathematical roots:

$$t = \frac{u\sin\theta \pm \sqrt{(u\sin\theta)^2 - 4\left(\frac{1}{2}g\right)(-h_0)}}{2\left(\frac{1}{2}g\right)} = \frac{u\sin\theta \pm \sqrt{u^2 \sin^2\theta + 2gh_0}}{g}$$
Physical Interpretation of the Two Quadratic Roots

1. The Positive Root ($t_+$): Taking the positive sign (+ inside the numerator) gives the true, physical forward time elapsed from the instant of launch to ground impact. This is your valid answer.

2. The Negative Root ($t_-$): Taking the minus sign (- inside the numerator) yields a negative time value ($t < 0$). Physically, this represents the theoretical time prior to launch ($t = 0$) at which a hypothetical projectile, launched from ground level with the same velocity vector, would have had to pass through the cliff edge location!

5. Energy Conservation vs Impact Vector Determination

A crucial physical principle often assessed in NESA HSC examinations involves comparing the energy perspective of impact against the vector kinematics perspective.

Conservation of Mechanical Energy at Impact

Because gravity is a conservative force and air resistance is zero, total mechanical energy is conserved throughout the entire trajectory ($E_{\text{total}} = E_k + E_p = \text{constant}$):

$$E_{k,\text{initial}} + E_{p,\text{initial}} = E_{k,\text{final}} + E_{p,\text{final}}$$ $$\frac{1}{2} m u^2 + m g h_0 = \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{impact}}^2 + 0$$

Dividing by mass $m$ and multiplying by 2 yields the scalar impact speed equation:

$$v_{\text{impact}}^2 = u^2 + 2 g h_0 \implies \mathbf{v_{\text{impact}} = \sqrt{u^2 + 2 g h_0}}$$

Remarkable Physical Insight: Notice that the final impact speed magnitude ($v_{\text{impact}}$) depends only on the initial launch speed $u$ and cliff height $h_0$! It is completely independent of the launch angle $\theta$! Whether a stone is thrown straight up, horizontally, or downward off a cliff at $20\text{ m/s}$, it will strike the water below at the exact same scalar speed!

However, the impact direction angle ($\phi$) depends heavily on launch angle $\theta$, because $\theta$ dictates how initial energy is partitioned between horizontal ($u_x$) and vertical ($u_y$) vector components!

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Pure Horizontal Launch off a Cliff

[Syllabus Core Application]

A rescue crate is pushed horizontally at $18.0\text{ m/s}$ off the edge of a flat coastal cliff $35.0\text{ m}$ above sea level. Assuming $g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$ and zero air drag, construct a complete UVTAXY table and calculate:

a) The time taken for the crate to reach the water.
b) The horizontal range from the base of the cliff to the splash point.
c) The vertical velocity component at impact ($v_y$).

Step 1: Construct & Populate the UVTAXY Table

Because launch is horizontal, $\theta = 0^\circ$. Origin set at cliff top. Landing displacement $s_y = -35.0\text{ m}$.

$$u_x = 18.0 \cos(0^\circ) = 18.0\text{ m/s}$$

$$u_y = 18.0 \sin(0^\circ) = 0\text{ m/s}$$

T$t = ?$$t = ?$
A$0\text{ m/s}^2$$-9.80\text{ m/s}^2$

Part a) Flight Time Calculation:

Apply vertical position equation with $u_y = 0\text{ m/s}$ and $s_y = -35.0\text{ m}$:

$$s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2 \implies -35.0 = 0(t) + \frac{1}{2}(-9.80)t^2$$

$$-35.0 = -4.90 t^2 \implies t^2 = \frac{-35.0}{-4.90} = 7.1429$$

$$t = \sqrt{7.1429} = \mathbf{2.67\text{ s}}\text{ (to 3 sig. figs.)}$$

Part b) Horizontal Range Calculation:

$$s_x = u_x t = 18.0 \times 2.6726 = \mathbf{48.1\text{ m}}\text{ (to 3 sig. figs.)}$$

Part c) Vertical Impact Velocity Component:

$$v_y = u_y + a_y t = 0 + (-9.80)(2.6726) = \mathbf{-26.2\text{ m/s}}$$

(The negative sign signifies a downward direction).

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Upward Angled Cliff Launch with Complete Impact Vector

[Asymmetrical Quadratic Analysis]

A distress flare is fired from the top of a $50.0\text{ m}$ high cliff with an initial speed of $30.0\text{ m/s}$ at an elevation angle of $25.0^\circ$ above the horizontal. Construct a UVTAXY table and determine the final impact velocity vector $\vec{v}_{\text{impact}}$ (both magnitude speed and direction angle below horizontal) when the flare strikes the ocean below.

Step 1: Construct & Populate the UVTAXY Table

Resolve launch velocity components ($u = 30.0\text{ m/s}, \theta = +25.0^\circ$):

$$u_x = 30.0 \cos(25.0^\circ) = 27.189\text{ m/s}$$

$$u_y = 30.0 \sin(25.0^\circ) = 12.679\text{ m/s}$$

A$0\text{ m/s}^2$$-9.80\text{ m/s}^2$
X / Y$s_x = ?$$s_y = -50.0\text{ m}$

Step 2: Calculate Vertical Velocity Component $v_y$ at Water Surface ($s_y = -50.0\text{ m}$):

$$v_y^2 = u_y^2 + 2a_y s_y$$

$$v_y^2 = (12.679)^2 + 2(-9.80)(-50.0) = 160.76 + 980.00 = 1140.76$$

$$v_y = -\sqrt{1140.76} = -33.775\text{ m/s}\quad (\text{downward direction})$$

Step 3: Calculate Total Flight Time $t$:

$$v_y = u_y + a_y t \implies -33.775 = 12.679 - 9.80 t$$

$$-46.454 = -9.80 t \implies t = \frac{46.454}{9.80} = 4.7402\text{ s}$$

Step 4: Recombine Components to Find Impact Velocity Vector:

$$v_{\text{impact}} = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2} = \sqrt{(27.189)^2 + (-33.775)^2} = \sqrt{739.24 + 1140.76} = \sqrt{1880.0} = \mathbf{43.4\text{ m/s}}$$

Direction angle $\phi$ below the horizontal:

$$\tan\phi = \left|\frac{v_y}{v_x}\right| = \frac{33.775}{27.189} = 1.2422 \implies \phi = \tan^{-1}(1.2422) = \mathbf{51.2^\circ}$$

Final Answer: The flare strikes the water with a velocity of $\mathbf{43.4\text{ m/s}}$ acting at $\mathbf{51.2^\circ\text{ below the horizontal}}$.

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Downward Angled Launch & Rock Stack Obstacle Clearance

[Boundary Constraint Evaluation]

A water cannon mounted on a sea cliff at elevation $h_0 = 42.0\text{ m}$ fires a stream at an initial speed $u = 22.0\text{ m/s}$ directed downward at an angle of $15.0^\circ$ below the horizontal ($\theta = -15.0^\circ$). $$u_y = 22.0 \sin(-15.0^\circ) = -5.694\text{ m/s}\quad (\text{negative initial vertical component})$$

$$s_x = u_x t \implies 25.0 = 21.250 t \implies t = \frac{25.0}{21.250} = 1.1765\text{ s}$$

[Upper-Level HSC Synthesis]

A artillery battery on a mountain peak elevated $h_1 = 120\text{ m}$ above a valley fires a shell at launch angle $\theta = +20.0^\circ$ toward an enemy bunker built on an opposing plateau elevated $h_2 = 45.0\text{ m}$ above the valley floor. An intervening offshore sea wall of height $h_2 = 25.0\text{ m}$ is situated $180\text{ m}$ horizontally from the cannon. Calculate the minimum initial launch speed $u$ required to clear the sea wall, and evaluate the total horizontal range $s_x$ to the ocean splash point at that speed.

Hint: Relative displacement at sea wall is $s_y = 25.0 - 85.0 = -60.0\text{ m}$ at $s_x = 180\text{ m}$. Express $t = \frac{180}{u\cos(25^\circ)}$, substitute into $s_y = u\sin(25^\circ)t - 4.90t^2$ to solve for $u$, then find total flight time to water level ($s_y = -85.0\text{ m}$).

Time expression at wall: $t = \frac{180}{0.90631 u} = \frac{198.608}{u}$

Substitute into vertical motion:

$$-60.0 = (0.42262 u)\left(\frac{198.608}{u}\right) - 4.90 \left(\frac{198.608}{u}\right)^2$$

$$-60.0 = 83.936 - \frac{193282.8}{u^2} \implies \frac{193282.8}{u^2} = 143.936$$

$$u^2 = 1342.84 \implies \mathbf{u = 36.6\text{ m/s}}$$

Total Range to Ocean Surface ($s_y = -85.0\text{ m}$):

$$u_x = 36.645 \cos(25^\circ) = 33.212\text{ m/s}, \quad u_y = 36.645 \sin(25^\circ) = 15.487\text{ m/s}$$

$$-85.0 = 15.487 t - 4.90 t^2 \implies 4.90 t^2 - 15.487 t - 85.0 = 0$$

Using quadratic formula: $t_{\text{ocean}} = 5.922\text{ s}$

$$\text{Total Range } s_x = u_x t_{\text{ocean}} = 33.212 \times 5.922 = \mathbf{197\text{ m}}$$

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Setting origin at cannon height, relative displacement at wall is $s_y = -60.0\text{ m}$. Expressing time $t = \frac{198.61}{u}$ and substituting into vertical kinematic equation eliminates $u$ from the linear term, yielding $u = 36.6\text{ m/s}$. To find total ocean range, solving $4.90 t^2 - 15.49 t - 85.0 = 0$ gives total flight time $t = 5.92\text{ s}$. Multiplying by horizontal velocity $u_x = 33.21\text{ m/s}$ gives a total range of $197\text{ m}$."
