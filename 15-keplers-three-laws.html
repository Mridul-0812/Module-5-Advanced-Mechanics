15. Kepler's Three Laws of Planetary Motion

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 4 (Part 3)

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Core Learning Goals

Inquiry Question 4: How does Kepler's laws of planetary motion describe the movements of natural and artificial satellites?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus (calculus-free), students must achieve absolute conceptual and mathematical fluency in Johannes Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion:

  • Describe Kepler's Three Laws qualitatively and quantitatively:
    1. 1st Law (Law of Ellipses): Planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus.
    2. 2nd Law (Law of Equal Areas): A line segment joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time.
    3. 3rd Law (Law of Periods): The square of the orbital period ($T^2$) is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis ($r^3$): $$\frac{r^3}{T^2} = k$$
  • Derive Kepler's Third Law for circular orbits by equating centripetal force ($F_c = \frac{m v^2}{r}$) to Newton's universal gravitational force ($F_g = \frac{G M m}{r^2}$).
  • Calculate Kepler's constant $k = \frac{G M}{4\pi^2}$ for different central bodies (e.g. Sun, Earth, Jupiter) and apply ratio calculations ($\frac{r_1^3}{T_1^2} = \frac{r_2^3}{T_2^2}$) across satellite systems.

Part 1: The Intuitive Idea — How Planets Really Move

Beginner Bridge: From Perfect Circles to Oval Loops

For thousands of years, ancient astronomers believed that everything in the heavens had to move in "perfect circles" at constant speed. But when Johannes Kepler analysed precise star-and-planet observations in the early 1600s, circles simply did not match the real numbers!

Kepler discovered three beautifully simple rules that govern every orbiting body in the universe—from planets orbiting the Sun, to moons orbiting Jupiter, to artificial satellites orbiting Earth.

1. Kepler's First Law: The Law of Ellipses

In Simple Terms: Orbits are not perfect circles; they are slightly flattened ovals called ellipses. The central body (like the Sun) sits at one special off-center point called a focus (plural: foci).

What is an Ellipse?

Think of an ellipse as a circle with two center points (foci) instead of one. If you push the two foci together until they meet in the middle, the ellipse becomes a perfect circle!

  • Semi-Major Axis ($a$ or $r$): The average distance from the center of the ellipse to its outer edge (half of the longest diameter). For circular orbits, $a$ is simply the radius $r$.
  • Eccentricity ($e$): A measure of how "stretched out" the ellipse is. A perfect circle has $e = 0$. Earth's orbit is almost a circle ($e = 0.017$), while a comet has a highly stretched orbit ($e = 0.95$).
  • Perihelion ($r_p$): The point of closest approach to the Sun ($r_p = a(1 - e)$).
  • Aphelion ($r_a$): The point furthest away from the Sun ($r_a = a(1 + e)$).

Figure 1: Anatomy of an elliptical orbit. The Sun sits at Focus 1. The average separation distance equals the semi-major axis $a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2}$.

2. Kepler's Second Law: The Law of Equal Areas

In Simple Terms: A planet speeds up as it gets closer to the Sun (near perihelion) and slows down as it moves further away (near aphelion). An imaginary string connecting the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas of space in equal amounts of time.

Why Does the Speed Change? (The Gravity & Energy Explanation)

As a planet travels toward perihelion (closer to the Sun), gravity pulls it forward, speeding it up as gravitational potential energy converts into kinetic energy. As it moves away toward aphelion, gravity pulls backward on it, slowing it down!

Mathematically, by conservation of angular momentum ($L = m v r \sin\phi = \text{constant}$):

$$v_p r_p = v_a r_a$$

Since perihelion distance $r_p$ is smaller than aphelion distance $r_a$, perihelion speed $v_p$ MUST be much faster than aphelion speed $v_a$!

3. Kepler's Third Law: The Law of Periods

In Simple Terms: Planets that are further from the Sun take much longer to complete an orbit than planets close to the Sun. Not only is their orbital path longer, but they also move at a slower average speed!

Kepler's Third Law (The $r^3 / T^2$ Ratio)

$$\frac{r^3}{T^2} = k \quad \text{or} \quad T^2 \propto r^3$$

"The cube of the average orbital radius ($r^3$) divided by the square of the orbital period ($T^2$) is a CONSTANT ($k$) for all objects orbiting the same central body!"

Part 2: Complete Step-by-Step Derivation of Kepler's Third Law

In the Stage 6 Physics exam, you are frequently required to derive Kepler's 3rd Law from fundamental mechanics principles ($F_c = F_g$). Here is the complete step-by-step mathematical proof:

Step-by-Step Proof: $\frac{r^3}{T^2} = \frac{G M}{4\pi^2}$

Step 1: State the physical condition for stable orbital motion.

For a planet or satellite of mass $m$ in circular orbit of radius $r$ around a central mass $M$, the required centripetal force ($F_c$) is supplied entirely by the universal gravitational attraction force ($F_g$):

$$F_c = F_g$$

Step 2: Substitute the fundamental force equations.

$$\frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{G M m}{r^2}$$

Step 3: Cancel the satellite mass $m$ and multiply by $r$ to isolate $v^2$.

$$v^2 = \frac{G M}{r}$$

Step 4: Express circular orbital velocity $v$ in terms of period $T$.

Since speed equals circumference divided by period ($v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$), square both sides:

$$v^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2}$$

Step 5: Equate the two expressions for $v^2$.

$$\frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2} = \frac{G M}{r}$$

Step 6: Cross-multiply to group radius terms ($r$) and period terms ($T$).

$$4\pi^2 r^3 = G M T^2$$

Step 7: Divide to isolate the Kepler ratio $\frac{r^3}{T^2}$.

Kepler's Constant Equation ($k$)

$$\frac{r^3}{T^2} = \frac{G M}{4\pi^2} = k$$

CRITICAL EXAM TAKEAWAY: What does Kepler's Constant $k$ depend on?

Notice that $k = \frac{G M}{4\pi^2}$ contains ONLY constants ($G, \pi$) and the mass of the central body ($M$) being orbited!

  • All 8 planets orbiting the Sun share the EXACT SAME Kepler constant ($k_{\text{Sun}} = \frac{G M_{\text{Sun}}}{4\pi^2} \approx 3.35 \times 10^{18}\text{ m}^3/\text{s}^2$).
  • All satellites (ISS, Moon, GPS) orbiting Earth share a DIFFERENT Kepler constant ($k_{\text{Earth}} = \frac{G M_E}{4\pi^2} \approx 1.01 \times 10^{13}\text{ m}^3/\text{s}^2$).
  • You CANNOT use the same $k$ value to compare a planet orbiting the Sun with a moon orbiting Jupiter!

Part 3: Advanced Orbital Ratio Analysis & Elliptical Speeds

1. The Two-Body Ratio Method ($\frac{r_1^3}{T_1^2} = \frac{r_2^3}{T_2^2}$)

If two bodies (Body 1 and Body 2) orbit the exact same central mass $M$, their Kepler constants are identical ($k_1 = k_2$):

$$\frac{r_1^3}{T_1^2} = \frac{r_2^3}{T_2^2} \implies \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^3 = \left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right)^2$$

This powerful ratio relation allows you to solve orbital problems without needing Newton's constant $G$ or the mass $M$!

2. Conservation of Energy in Elliptical Orbits

In an elliptical orbit, total mechanical energy $E = K + U = -\frac{G M m}{2 a}$ remains strictly constant. At perihelion ($r_p$) and aphelion ($r_a$):

$$\frac{1}{2} m v_p^2 - \frac{G M m}{r_p} = \frac{1}{2} m v_a^2 - \frac{G M m}{r_a}$$

Combined with conservation of angular momentum ($v_p r_p = v_a r_a$), we obtain the exact perihelion and aphelion speeds:

$$v_p = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{a} \left(\frac{1 + e}{1 - e}\right)} \quad \text{and} \quad v_a = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{a} \left(\frac{1 - e}{1 + e}\right)}$$

Four Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Using Kepler's 3rd Law Ratio for Jupiter

[Basic Kepler Ratio Calculation]

Earth orbits the Sun at an average radius $r_E = 1.00\text{ AU}$ with an orbital period $T_E = 1.00\text{ year}$. Jupiter orbits the Sun at an average radius $r_J = 5.20\text{ AU}$.

Calculate Jupiter's orbital period $T_J$ in Earth years using Kepler's Third Law ratio method.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Applying Kepler's Third Law Ratio ($\frac{r_1^3}{T_1^2} = \frac{r_2^3}{T_2^2}$):

$$\frac{r_E^3}{T_E^2} = \frac{r_J^3}{T_J^2}$$

Substituting values ($r_E = 1.00\text{ AU}, T_E = 1.00\text{ yr}, r_J = 5.20\text{ AU}$):

$$\frac{(1.00)^3}{(1.00)^2} = \frac{(5.20)^3}{T_J^2}$$ $$1.00 = \frac{140.608}{T_J^2} \implies T_J^2 = 140.608$$

Taking the square root:

$$T_J = \sqrt{140.608} = \mathbf{11.86\text{ Earth years}} \approx \mathbf{11.9\text{ years}}$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Calculating Exoplanet Central Star Mass from $k$

[Kepler Constant & Mass Determination]

An exoplanet orbits a distant star at a radius $r = 1.80 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}$ with an orbital period $T = 4.20 \times 10^7\text{ seconds}$ ($\sim 1.33\text{ years}$).

a) Calculate Kepler's constant $k$ for this stellar system in $\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$.
b) Calculate the mass $M_*$ of the central star ($G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Calculating Kepler's Constant ($k$):

$$k = \frac{r^3}{T^2} = \frac{(1.80 \times 10^{11})^3}{(4.20 \times 10^7)^2} = \frac{5.832 \times 10^{33}}{1.764 \times 10^{15}} = \mathbf{3.306 \times 10^{18}\text{ m}^3/\text{s}^2}$$

Part b) Calculating Central Star Mass ($M_*$):

Using $k = \frac{G M_*}{4\pi^2} \implies M_* = \frac{4\pi^2 k}{G}$:

$$M_* = \frac{4\pi^2 \times (3.306 \times 10^{18})}{6.674 \times 10^{-11}} = \frac{39.4784 \times 3.306 \times 10^{18}}{6.674 \times 10^{-11}} = \frac{1.3051 \times 10^{20}}{6.674 \times 10^{-11}} = \mathbf{1.955 \times 10^{30}\text{ kg}}$$

(Notice this is very close to our Sun's mass of $1.989 \times 10^{30}\text{ kg}$!)

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Elliptical Comet Perihelion vs Aphelion Velocity Analysis

[Kepler 2nd Law & Conservation Dynamics]

Halley's Comet has an elliptical orbit around the Sun with perihelion distance $r_p = 8.75 \times 10^{10}\text{ m}$ ($0.585\text{ AU}$) and aphelion distance $r_a = 5.25 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}$ ($35.1\text{ AU}$). Its speed at perihelion is $v_p = 54.6\text{ km/s}$.

a) Calculate Halley's Comet speed at aphelion $v_a$ using Kepler's Second Law.
b) Calculate the semi-major axis $a$ and calculate the comet's orbital period $T$ in years ($M_{\text{Sun}} = 1.989 \times 10^{30}\text{ kg}$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Aphelion Speed ($v_a$):

By Kepler's Second Law / Conservation of Angular Momentum ($v_p r_p = v_a r_a$):

$$v_a = v_p \left(\frac{r_p}{r_a}\right) = 54.6\text{ km/s} \times \left(\frac{8.75 \times 10^{10}\text{ m}}{5.25 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}}\right) = 54.6 \times \left(\frac{1}{60}\right) = \mathbf{0.910\text{ km/s}} \quad (\mathbf{910\text{ m/s}})$$

Part b) Semi-Major Axis ($a$) & Orbital Period ($T$):

$$a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2} = \frac{8.75 \times 10^{10} + 5.25 \times 10^{12}}{2} = \frac{5.3375 \times 10^{12}}{2} = \mathbf{2.6688 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}}$$

Using Kepler's 3rd Law ($T = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G M_{\text{Sun}}}}$):

$$a^3 = (2.6688 \times 10^{12})^3 = 1.8982 \times 10^{37}\text{ m}^3$$ $$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 \times (1.8982 \times 10^{37})}{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(1.989 \times 10^{30})} = \frac{7.4938 \times 10^{38}}{1.3275 \times 10^{20}} = 5.645 \times 10^{18}\text{ s}^2$$ $$T = \sqrt{5.645 \times 10^{18}} = 2.3759 \times 10^9\text{ seconds}$$

Converting to Earth years ($\frac{2.3759 \times 10^9}{3.1536 \times 10^7}$):

$$T = \mathbf{75.3\text{ Earth years}} \quad (\text{matches Halley's famous } 75-76\text{ year return cycle!})$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension - JRAHS Style): Modified Kepler Constant for Binary Star System

[Dual Mass Vector Center Derivation]

In a binary star system, Star A ($M_1$) and Star B ($M_2$) orbit around their common center of mass at separation distance $d = r_1 + r_2$.

a) Show that the modified Kepler 3rd Law for a binary star system is given by: $$\frac{d^3}{T^2} = \frac{G (M_1 + M_2)}{4\pi^2}$$

b) Two equal-mass stars ($M_1 = M_2 = M$) orbit with period $T = 3.00\text{ years}$ ($9.46 \times 10^7\text{ s}$) at separation $d = 6.00 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}$. Calculate individual star mass $M$.

Full Band 6 JRAHS Solution:

Part a) Binary System Derivation:

Position of center of mass relative to Star 1: $r_1 = d \left(\frac{M_2}{M_1 + M_2}\right)$.

Gravitational attraction provides centripetal force for Star 1's circular orbit of radius $r_1$:

$$F_g = F_c \implies \frac{G M_1 M_2}{d^2} = M_1 \omega^2 r_1 = M_1 \left(\frac{2\pi}{T}\right)^2 \left[ d \left(\frac{M_2}{M_1 + M_2}\right) \right]$$

Cancelling $M_1$ and $M_2$ from both sides:

$$\frac{G}{d^2} = \frac{4\pi^2}{T^2} \frac{d}{M_1 + M_2}$$

Cross-multiplying to isolate $\frac{d^3}{T^2}$:

$$\mathbf{\frac{d^3}{T^2} = \frac{G (M_1 + M_2)}{4\pi^2}} \quad \text{Q.E.D.}$$

Part b) Numerical Mass Calculation ($M_1 = M_2 = M \implies M_1 + M_2 = 2 M$):

$$\frac{d^3}{T^2} = \frac{G (2 M)}{4\pi^2} \implies M = \frac{4\pi^2 d^3}{2 G T^2} = \frac{2\pi^2 d^3}{G T^2}$$ $$M = \frac{2\pi^2 \times (6.00 \times 10^{11})^3}{(6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \times (9.46 \times 10^7)^2} = \frac{19.7392 \times 2.16 \times 10^{35}}{(6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \times (8.949 \times 10^{15})}$$ $$M = \frac{4.2637 \times 10^{36}}{5.9726 \times 10^5} = \mathbf{7.14 \times 10^{30}\text{ kg}} \quad (\mathbf{3.59\text{ Solar Masses}})$$

Practise Question 1 (Easy): Ratio Calculation for Exoplanet Orbit

[3 Marks]

Exoplanet Alpha orbits a star at distance $r_A = 2.00\text{ AU}$ with a period $T_A = 4.00\text{ years}$. Exoplanet Beta orbits the same star at distance $r_B = 8.00\text{ AU}$.

Calculate the orbital period $T_B$ of Exoplanet Beta in years using Kepler's Third Law ratio.

Hint: Use $\frac{r_A^3}{T_A^2} = \frac{r_B^3}{T_B^2} \implies T_B = T_A \times \left(\frac{r_B}{r_A}\right)^{3/2}$.

$$\frac{(2.00)^3}{(4.00)^2} = \frac{(8.00)^3}{T_B^2} \implies \frac{8.00}{16.0} = \frac{512}{T_B^2}$$ $$0.500 = \frac{512}{T_B^2} \implies T_B^2 = \frac{512}{0.500} = 1024$$ $$T_B = \sqrt{1024} = \mathbf{32.0\text{ Earth years}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"By Kepler's Third Law ratio $\frac{r_A^3}{T_A^2} = \frac{r_B^3}{T_B^2}$. Substituting values gives $\frac{2^3}{4^2} = \frac{8^3}{T_B^2} \implies 0.5 = \frac{512}{T_B^2}$. Solving for period yields $T_B^2 = 1024 \implies T_B = 32.0\text{ years}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Sets up Kepler's Third Law ratio equation.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly substitutes distances ($2.00\text{ AU}, 8.00\text{ AU}$) and period ($4.00\text{ years}$).
  • 3 Marks: Obtains correct period $T_B = 32.0\text{ years}$.

Practise Question 2 (Medium): Deriving $\frac{r^3}{T^2} = \frac{GM}{4\pi^2}$ Written Response

[4 Marks]

Derive Kepler's Third Law $\frac{r^3}{T^2} = \frac{GM}{4\pi^2}$ for a circular orbit from Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation and centripetal acceleration, defining all terms and explaining why $k$ is constant for a given central body.

Hint: Start from $F_c = F_g$, substitute $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$, isolate $\frac{r^3}{T^2}$, and note that $G, \pi, M$ are fixed.

1. Equate centripetal force to gravitational force: $\frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{G M m}{r^2}$.

2. Cancel $m$ and multiply by $r$: $v^2 = \frac{G M}{r}$.

3. Substitute circular speed $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$: $\frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2} = \frac{G M}{r}$.

4. Cross-multiply: $4\pi^2 r^3 = G M T^2 \implies \frac{r^3}{T^2} = \frac{G M}{4\pi^2}$.

5. The term $\frac{GM}{4\pi^2}$ depends only on universal gravitational constant $G$ and central body mass $M$. Thus $k$ is constant for all objects orbiting the same central mass $M$.

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"For a satellite of mass $m$ orbiting central mass $M$ at radius $r$, gravity provides centripetal force: $\frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{G M m}{r^2} \implies v^2 = \frac{G M}{r}$. Substituting circular velocity $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$ gives $\frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2} = \frac{G M}{r}$. Rearranging yields $\frac{r^3}{T^2} = \frac{G M}{4\pi^2}$. Since $G, \pi$, and $M$ are constant for a specific central mass, the ratio $\frac{r^3}{T^2}$ is a constant $k$ for all orbiting bodies in that system."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Equates $F_c = F_g$ with correct formulas.
  • 2 Marks: Substitutes $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains $\frac{r^3}{T^2} = \frac{GM}{4\pi^2}$.
  • 4 Marks: Explains clearly why $k$ depends only on central mass $M$.

Practise Question 3 (Hard): Elliptical Comet Orbit Analysis & Aphelion Speed

[5 Marks]

A newly discovered comet has an elliptical orbit around the Sun ($M_{\text{Sun}} = 1.989 \times 10^{30}\text{ kg}$) with an orbital period $T = 64.0\text{ Earth years}$ ($2.019 \times 10^9\text{ s}$). Its perihelion distance is $r_p = 1.00 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}$ ($0.668\text{ AU}$).

a) Calculate the comet's semi-major axis $a$ in metres.
b) Calculate the aphelion distance $r_a$ in metres.
c) If the comet's speed at perihelion is $v_p = 48.0\text{ km/s}$, calculate its speed at aphelion $v_a$.

Hint: Use $a = \sqrt[3]{\frac{G M T^2}{4\pi^2}}$, then $r_a = 2 a - r_p$, then $v_a = v_p \left(\frac{r_p}{r_a}\right)$.

Part a) Semi-Major Axis ($a$):

$$a^3 = \frac{G M_{\text{Sun}} T^2}{4\pi^2} = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(1.989 \times 10^{30})(2.019 \times 10^9)^2}{4\pi^2}$$ $$a^3 = \frac{(1.3275 \times 10^{20}) \times (4.076 \times 10^{18})}{39.4784} = 1.3706 \times 10^{37}\text{ m}^3$$ $$a = (1.3706 \times 10^{37})^{1/3} = \mathbf{2.393 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}} \quad (16.0\text{ AU})$$

Part b) Aphelion Distance ($r_a$):

Since $a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2} \implies r_a = 2 a - r_p$:

$$r_a = 2(2.393 \times 10^{12}) - 0.100 \times 10^{12} = 4.786 \times 10^{12} - 0.100 \times 10^{12} = \mathbf{4.686 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}}$$

Part c) Aphelion Speed ($v_a$):

$$v_a = v_p \left(\frac{r_p}{r_a}\right) = 48.0\text{ km/s} \times \left(\frac{1.00 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}}{4.686 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}}\right) = 48.0 \times 0.02134 = \mathbf{1.02\text{ km/s}} \quad (\mathbf{1020\text{ m/s}})$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Using Kepler's Third Law $a = \sqrt[3]{\frac{G M_{\text{Sun}} T^2}{4\pi^2}} = \sqrt[3]{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(1.989 \times 10^{30})(2.019 \times 10^9)^2}{4\pi^2}} = 2.39 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}$. Since $a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2}$, $r_a = 2 a - r_p = 2(2.393 \times 10^{12}) - 1.00 \times 10^{11} = 4.69 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}$. By conservation of angular momentum $v_a = v_p \left(\frac{r_p}{r_a}\right) = 48.0 \left(\frac{1.00 \times 10^{11}}{4.69 \times 10^{12}}\right) = 1.02\text{ km/s}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Calculates period in seconds ($2.019 \times 10^9\text{ s}$).
  • 2 Marks: Calculates semi-major axis $a = 2.39 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}$.
  • 3 Marks: Uses geometry $a = (r_p + r_a)/2$ to find aphelion distance $r_a = 4.69 \times 10^{12}\text{ m}$.
  • 4 Marks: Applies Kepler's 2nd Law $v_p r_p = v_a r_a$.
  • 5 Marks: Obtains aphelion speed $v_a = 1.02\text{ km/s}$.

Practise Question 4 (JRAHS Extension): Multi-Moon Jovian System Kepler Constant Verification

[6 Marks]

The Galilean moons Io and Ganymede orbit Jupiter ($M_J$). Io orbits at $r_1 = 4.217 \times 10^8\text{ m}$ with period $T_1 = 1.529 \times 10^5\text{ s}$ ($1.769\text{ days}$). Ganymede orbits at $r_2 = 1.070 \times 10^9\text{ m}$.

a) Calculate Kepler's constant $k_{\text{Jupiter}}$ using Io's orbital parameters.
b) Predict Ganymede's orbital period $T_2$ in days.
c) Calculate Jupiter's mass $M_J$ from $k_{\text{Jupiter}}$ and express it as a fraction of Earth's mass ($M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$).

Hint: $k_J = \frac{r_1^3}{T_1^2}$. Then $T_2 = \sqrt{\frac{r_2^3}{k_J}}$. Then $M_J = \frac{4\pi^2 k_J}{G}$.

Part a) Kepler's Constant for Jupiter ($k_J$):

$$k_J = \frac{r_1^3}{T_1^2} = \frac{(4.217 \times 10^8)^3}{(1.529 \times 10^5)^2} = \frac{7.500 \times 10^{25}}{2.3378 \times 10^{10}} = \mathbf{3.208 \times 10^{15}\text{ m}^3/\text{s}^2}$$

Part b) Ganymede's Orbital Period ($T_2$):

$$T_2^2 = \frac{r_2^3}{k_J} = \frac{(1.070 \times 10^9)^3}{3.208 \times 10^{15}} = \frac{1.225 \times 10^{27}}{3.208 \times 10^{15}} = 3.8186 \times 10^{11}\text{ s}^2$$ $$T_2 = \sqrt{3.8186 \times 10^{11}} = 6.1794 \times 10^5\text{ seconds}$$

Converting to days ($\frac{6.1794 \times 10^5}{86400}$):

$$T_2 = \mathbf{7.152\text{ days}} \quad (\text{matches Ganymede's actual } 7.15\text{-day period!})$$

Part c) Jupiter's Mass ($M_J$):

$$M_J = \frac{4\pi^2 k_J}{G} = \frac{4\pi^2 \times (3.208 \times 10^{15})}{6.674 \times 10^{-11}} = \frac{1.2665 \times 10^{17}}{6.674 \times 10^{-11}} = \mathbf{1.898 \times 10^{27}\text{ kg}}$$

Ratio to Earth mass:
