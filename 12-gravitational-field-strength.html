12. Radial Gravitational Field Strength ($g$)

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 3 (Part 2)

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Core Learning Goals

Inquiry Question 3: How does the force of gravity determine the motion of celestial objects?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus (calculus-free), students must build a clear intuitive and quantitative understanding of gravitational fields:

  • Define a gravitational field as a region of space surrounding a mass in which another mass experiences an attractive gravitational force.
  • Define gravitational field strength $g$ as the force per unit mass ($\vec{g} = \frac{\vec{F}_g}{m}$).
  • Derive the radial gravitational field strength formula ($g = \frac{G M}{r^2}$) by combining Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation with Newton's Second Law.
  • Distinguish clearly between uniform gravitational fields (local scale, near Earth's surface) and radial gravitational fields (astronomical scale).
  • Analyse how gravitational field strength varies with planetary mass, radius, density, altitude ($r = R + h$), and interior depth ($r < R$).

Part 1: The Intuitive Idea — What is a Gravitational Field?

Beginner Bridge: Thinking About Invisible "Pull Zones"

When you drop an apple, how does Earth know the apple is there without touching it? How does the Moon "feel" Earth's pull across 384,000 kilometres of empty space?

Physics answers this using the idea of a Field. Think of a massive body like Earth as creating an invisible "distortion" or "pull zone" in the space around it. Every single point in space has a specific "pull strength". If you place a mass anywhere in that zone, it immediately feels a gravitational force!

1. Defining Gravitational Field Strength ($g$)

Imagine taking a small $1\text{ kg}$ mass (we call this a test mass) and placing it at a specific spot in space. The gravitational force felt by that $1\text{ kg}$ object is the gravitational field strength ($g$) at that location.

In simple words:

Gravitational Field Strength Definition

$$g = \frac{F_g}{m}$$

"Field strength is simply the gravitational force felt per kilogram of mass placed at that point."

What are the units of $g$?

From the definition $g = \frac{F_g}{m}$, the unit is Newtons per kilogram ($\text{N/kg}$).

However, recalling Newton's Second Law ($F = m a \implies a = \frac{F}{m}$), the unit of acceleration is metres per second squared ($\text{m/s}^2$). These two units are completely identical!

$$\mathbf{1\text{ N/kg} = 1\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Clearing Up a Common Student Doubt: Is $g$ Acceleration or Field Strength?

It is BOTH!

  • When viewed as a field property, $g = 9.80\text{ N/kg}$ means "Earth pulls on every $1\text{ kg}$ of mass with $9.80\text{ N}$ of force".
  • When an object is allowed to fall freely under that force, $g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$ represents its free-fall acceleration.

Part 2: Step-by-Step Derivation of Radial Field Strength

1. Uniform vs Radial Fields

Before doing the derivation, let's understand two types of field models:

Field Model Field Line Pattern Field Strength Behavior Where We Use It
Uniform Field Parallel, evenly spaced lines pointing straight down Constant ($g \approx 9.80\text{ N/kg}$) everywhere Local scale (inside a classroom, throwing a ball)
Radial Field Lines pointing inward towards the center like wheel spokes Gets weaker with distance ($g \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$) Astronomical scale (satellites, planets, stars, moons)

Figure 1: Near Earth's surface, field lines are parallel and uniform. On a planetary scale, field lines radiate inward toward the center of mass.

2. Deriving $g = \frac{GM}{r^2}$ step-by-step

Let's derive the formula for field strength around a body of mass $M$ (like Earth or a star):

Step 1: Start with the gravitational force between mass $M$ and a small test mass $m$ at distance $r$:

$$F_g = \frac{G M m}{r^2}$$

Step 2: Use the definition of gravitational field strength ($g = \frac{F_g}{m}$):

$$g = \frac{\left( \frac{G M m}{r^2} \right)}{m}$$

Step 3: Cancel out the small test mass $m$:

$$\mathbf{g = \frac{G M}{r^2}}$$

Radial Gravitational Field Strength Formula

$$g = \frac{G M}{r^2}$$

Key Insight: Notice that the test mass $m$ disappeared! This means the gravitational field strength at a point in space depends ONLY on the central mass $M$ creating the field and the distance $r$ from its center. It does NOT depend on what object you place there!

Part 3: Stepping Up — Altitude, Density & Interior Fields

1. Field Strength Above Surface ($r = R + h$)

When an object (like a satellite or astronaut) is at an altitude $h$ above a planet's surface (where $R$ is planetary radius):

$$r = R + h \implies g = \frac{G M}{(R + h)^2}$$

Exam Warning: Don't Forget Planet Radius!

If an exam states a satellite is orbiting at altitude $h = 1000\text{ km}$, do NOT put $r = 1000\text{ km}$ into the denominator! You MUST convert $h$ to metres ($1.0 \times 10^6\text{ m}$) and add Earth's radius ($R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$), giving $r = 7.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$.

2. Surface Field Strength and Density ($\rho$)

We can express surface gravity in terms of a planet's average density ($\rho$) and radius ($R$).

Deriving $g$ in terms of Planet Density

Assuming a uniform spherical planet of volume $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$ and mass $M = \rho V = \rho \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right)$:

$$g_{\text{surface}} = \frac{G M}{R^2} = \frac{G \left( \frac{4}{3}\pi \rho R^3 \right)}{R^2}$$

Cancelling $R^2$ in denominator with $R^3$ in numerator:

$$\mathbf{g_{\text{surface}} = \frac{4}{3} \pi G \rho R}$$

Physical Meaning: If two planets have the same average density $\rho$, the planet with twice the radius will have TWICE the surface gravity!

3. Gravity Inside a Planet ($r < R$)

What happens if you travel down a tunnel toward the center of a uniform planet?

  • Outside the planet ($r \ge R$): $g(r) \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$ (Inverse-square drop-off).
  • Inside a uniform planet ($r < R$): By the Shell Theorem, only the sphere of mass beneath you pulls you inward. The mass enclosed scales as $r^3$, so $g(r) \propto r$ (Linear increase from zero at center to maximum at surface).
  • At the exact center ($r = 0$): $g = 0\text{ N/kg}$ (Weightlessness due to symmetric outward pulls from all surrounding mass!).

Four Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Surface Gravity of Mars

[Basic Field Calculation]

Mars has a mass $M = 6.42 \times 10^{23}\text{ kg}$ and a mean radius $R = 3.39 \times 10^6\text{ m}$.

a) Calculate the gravitational field strength $g_{\text{Mars}}$ at the Martian surface.
b) Calculate the weight of a $120\text{ kg}$ Martian rover on Earth vs on Mars ($G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Surface Gravity of Mars ($g_{\text{Mars}}$):

$$g = \frac{G M}{R^2} = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \times (6.42 \times 10^{23})}{(3.39 \times 10^6)^2}$$ $$g = \frac{4.2847 \times 10^{13}}{1.1492 \times 10^{13}} = \mathbf{3.73\text{ N/kg}} \quad (\text{or } 3.73\text{ m/s}^2)$$

Part b) Weight Comparison ($W = m g$):

  • Weight on Earth: $W_{\text{Earth}} = 120 \times 9.80 = \mathbf{1176\text{ N}}$
  • Weight on Mars: $W_{\text{Mars}} = 120 \times 3.728 = \mathbf{447\text{ N}}$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Altitude for Specified Gravitational Field Strength

[Altitude & Distance Algebra]

At what altitude $h$ above Earth's surface does the gravitational field strength drop to exactly $g = 2.45\text{ N/kg}$ (which is $\frac{1}{4}$ of its surface value $9.80\text{ N/kg}$)? ($R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}, M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Method 1: Proportional Scaling Shortcut

Since $g \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$, for $g$ to drop to $\frac{1}{4}$ of its surface value, the distance $r$ from Earth's center must double:

$$r = 2 R_E$$ $$r = R_E + h \implies R_E + h = 2 R_E \implies h = R_E = \mathbf{6.37 \times 10^6\text{ m}} \quad (6370\text{ km})$$

Method 2: Direct Formula Calculation

$$g = \frac{G M_E}{r^2} \implies r^2 = \frac{G M_E}{g}$$ $$r = \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}{2.45}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.9857 \times 10^{14}}{2.45}} = \sqrt{1.6268 \times 10^{14}} = 1.2755 \times 10^7\text{ m}$$

Subtracting Earth's radius to find altitude $h$:

$$h = r - R_E = 1.2755 \times 10^7 - 0.6371 \times 10^7 = \mathbf{6.38 \times 10^6\text{ m}}$$

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Comparing Exoplanet Surface Gravities using Density Ratio

[Density & Proportional Analysis]

Exoplanet Kepler-B has an average mass density $1.50$ times that of Earth ($\rho_B = 1.50 \rho_E$) and a radius $2.00$ times that of Earth ($R_B = 2.00 R_E$).

a) Calculate the surface gravitational field strength $g_B$ on Kepler-B.
b) Calculate the mass $M_B$ of Kepler-B in terms of Earth mass $M_E$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Surface Field Strength ($g_B$):

Using the density formula $g = \frac{4}{3}\pi G \rho R$:

$$\frac{g_B}{g_E} = \frac{\rho_B}{\rho_E} \times \frac{R_B}{R_E} = 1.50 \times 2.00 = 3.00$$ $$g_B = 3.00 \times g_E = 3.00 \times 9.80 = \mathbf{29.4\text{ N/kg}}$$

Part b) Planet Mass ($M_B$):

$$M = \rho V = \rho \left( \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \right) \implies M \propto \rho R^3$$ $$\frac{M_B}{M_E} = \left(\frac{\rho_B}{\rho_E}\right) \times \left(\frac{R_B}{R_E}\right)^3 = 1.50 \times (2.00)^3 = 1.50 \times 8.00 = \mathbf{12.0 M_E}$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension - JRAHS Style): Equatorial vs Polar Apparent Gravity Difference

[Oblateness & Rotation Effect]

Earth is an oblate spheroid. Its polar radius is $R_p = 6.357 \times 10^6\text{ m}$ and its equatorial radius is $R_e = 6.378 \times 10^6\text{ m}$. Earth rotates with angular velocity $\omega = 7.292 \times 10^{-5}\text{ rad/s}$.

a) Calculate true gravitational field strength at the poles ($g_{\text{pole}}$) vs equator ($g_{\text{eq}}$) due to shape alone ($M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$).
b) Calculate the centripetal acceleration $a_c = \omega^2 R_e$ at the equator.
c) Determine the total effective apparent gravity $g_{\text{apparent}}$ measured by a scale at the equator.

Full Band 6 JRAHS Solution:

Part a) True Gravitational Field Strength at Poles vs Equator:

$$g_{\text{pole}} = \frac{G M_E}{R_p^2} = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}{(6.357 \times 10^6)^2} = \mathbf{9.863\text{ N/kg}}$$ $$g_{\text{eq,true}} = \frac{G M_E}{R_e^2} = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}{(6.378 \times 10^6)^2} = \mathbf{9.798\text{ N/kg}}$$

Part b) Centripetal Acceleration at Equator ($a_c$):

$$a_c = \omega^2 R_e = (7.292 \times 10^{-5})^2 \times (6.378 \times 10^6) = (5.317 \times 10^{-9}) \times (6.378 \times 10^6) = \mathbf{0.0339\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Part c) Apparent Surface Gravity ($g_{\text{apparent}}$):

At the equator, part of Earth's gravitational pull is required to provide centripetal acceleration for rotation. The normal force felt by a scale is:

$$N = m g_{\text{eq,true}} - m a_c \implies g_{\text{apparent}} = g_{\text{eq,true}} - a_c$$ $$g_{\text{apparent}} = 9.798 - 0.0339 = \mathbf{9.764\text{ N/kg}}$$

Conclusion: You weigh approximately 1% less at the equator than at the poles due to Earth's bulge and rotation!

Practise Question 1 (Easy): Lunar Surface Field & Weight

[3 Marks]

The Moon has a mass $M = 7.35 \times 10^{22}\text{ kg}$ and radius $R = 1.74 \times 10^6\text{ m}$.

a) Calculate the lunar gravitational field strength $g_{\text{Moon}}$.
b) Calculate the weight of an $85.0\text{ kg}$ astronaut on the Moon.

Hint: Use $g = \frac{G M}{R^2}$ and $W = m g$.

a) Lunar Field Strength ($g_{\text{Moon}}$):

$$g = \frac{G M}{R^2} = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \times (7.35 \times 10^{22})}{(1.74 \times 10^6)^2} = \frac{4.905 \times 10^{12}}{3.0276 \times 10^{12}} = \mathbf{1.62\text{ N/kg}}$$

b) Astronaut Weight ($W$):

$$W = m g = 85.0 \times 1.62 = \mathbf{137.7\text{ N}} \approx \mathbf{138\text{ N}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Lunar surface gravity is $g = \frac{G M}{R^2} = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(7.35 \times 10^{22})}{(1.74 \times 10^6)^2} = 1.62\text{ N/kg}$. The astronaut's weight on the Moon is $W = m g = (85.0)(1.62) = 138\text{ N}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly substitutes into $g = \frac{G M}{R^2}$.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates $g_{\text{Moon}} = 1.62\text{ N/kg}$.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains final weight $138\text{ N}$ with correct units.

Practise Question 2 (Medium): Altitude for Specified $g = 5.00\text{ N/kg}$

[4 Marks]

Calculate the altitude $h$ above Earth's surface where the gravitational field strength is $g = 5.00\text{ N/kg}$. ($M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}, R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$).

Hint: Solve $r = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{g}}$, then subtract $R_E$ to find $h = r - R_E$.

Step 1: Calculate distance $r$ from Earth's center:

$$r = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{g}} = \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}{5.00}} = \sqrt{7.9714 \times 10^{13}} = \mathbf{8.928 \times 10^6\text{ m}}$$

Step 2: Subtract Earth's radius to get altitude $h$:

$$h = r - R_E = 8.928 \times 10^6 - 6.371 \times 10^6 = \mathbf{2.56 \times 10^6\text{ m}} \quad (2560\text{ km})$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Rearranging $g = \frac{G M}{r^2}$ for distance gives $r = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{g}} = \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}{5.00}} = 8.928 \times 10^6\text{ m}$. Subtracting Earth's radius gives altitude $h = r - R_E = 8.928 \times 10^6 - 6.371 \times 10^6 = 2.56 \times 10^6\text{ m}$ ($2560\text{ km}$)."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Rearranges formula to make $r$ the subject.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly calculates distance from center $r = 8.928 \times 10^6\text{ m}$.
  • 3 Marks: Recognises $h = r - R_E$.
  • 4 Marks: Calculates altitude $h = 2.56 \times 10^6\text{ m}$.

Practise Question 3 (Hard): Planet Density & Surface Gravity Derivation

[5 Marks]

a) Show that for any uniform spherical planet of density $\rho$ and radius $R$, surface gravity is $g = \frac{4}{3}\pi G \rho R$.
b) Planet X has double Earth's average density ($\rho_X = 2 \rho_E$) and half Earth's radius ($R_X = 0.5 R_E$). Determine the surface gravity $g_X$ on Planet X in terms of Earth's surface gravity $g_E$.

Hint: Substitute $M = \rho \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right)$ into $g = \frac{G M}{R^2}$. For part b, multiply factors $(2) \times (0.5)$.

a) Algebraic Proof:

$$M = \rho V = \rho \left( \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \right)$$ $$g = \frac{G M}{R^2} = \frac{G \left( \rho \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \right)}{R^2} = \mathbf{\frac{4}{3}\pi G \rho R}$$

b) Ratio Calculation:

$$\frac{g_X}{g_E} = \frac{\rho_X}{\rho_E} \times \frac{R_X}{R_E} = (2.0) \times (0.5) = \mathbf{1.0}$$ $$g_X = 1.0 \times g_E = \mathbf{9.80\text{ N/kg}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Mass of a uniform sphere is $M = \rho V = \frac{4}{3}\pi \rho R^3$. Substituting into surface gravity gives $g = \frac{G M}{R^2} = \frac{G (\frac{4}{3}\pi \rho R^3)}{R^2} = \frac{4}{3}\pi G \rho R$. For Planet X, $\frac{g_X}{g_E} = \left(\frac{\rho_X}{\rho_E}\right)\left(\frac{R_X}{R_E}\right) = (2)(0.5) = 1.0$. Thus $g_X = g_E = 9.80\text{ N/kg}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: States volume formula $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$.
  • 2 Marks: Expresses mass in terms of density $M = \frac{4}{3}\pi \rho R^3$.
  • 3 Marks: Successfully proves $g = \frac{4}{3}\pi G \rho R$.
  • 4 Marks: Sets up density/radius ratio equation.
  • 5 Marks: Concludes $g_X = g_E = 9.80\text{ N/kg}$.

Practise Question 4 (JRAHS Extension): Internal Tunnel Gravity Profile Analysis

[6 Marks]

A hypothetical tunnel is drilled through a uniform planet of total mass $M$, radius $R$, and surface gravity $g_s = 12.0\text{ N/kg}$.

a) Express the field strength $g(r)$ as a piecewise function for both $r < R$ (interior) and $r \ge R$ (exterior).
b) Calculate the field strength at internal depth $d = 0.25 R$ below the surface ($r = 0.75 R$).
c) Sketch $g(r)$ vs $r$ from $r = 0$ to $r = 3 R$, labeling peak coordinates.

Hint: Inside, $g(r) = g_s \left(\frac{r}{R}\right)$. Outside, $g(r) = g_s \left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^2$.

a) Piecewise Function:

$$g(r) = \begin{cases} g_s \left( \frac{r}{R} \right) & \text{for } 0 \le r < R \\ g_s \left( \frac{R}{r} \right)^2 & \text{for } r \ge R \end{cases}$$

b) Field Strength at Depth $d = 0.25 R$ ($r = 0.75 R$):

$$g(0.75 R) = g_s \left( \frac{0.75 R}{R} \right) = 0.75 \times 12.0 = \mathbf{9.00\text{ N/kg}}$$

c) Key Graph Features:

  • At $r = 0$: $g = 0\text{ N/kg}$.
  • Straight line from $(0, 0)$ to peak $(R, 12.0\text{ N/kg})$.
  • Inverse-square curve for $r > R$, passing through $(2R, 3.0\text{ N/kg})$ and $(3R, 1.33\text{ N/kg})$.
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Inside the planet, mass enclosed scales as $r^3$, giving $g(r) = g_s \left(\frac{r}{R}\right)$. Outside, $g(r) = g_s \left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^2$. At radial position $r = 0.75 R$, $g = 12.0 \times 0.75 = 9.00\text{ N/kg}$. The graph is linear from $(0,0)$ to peak $(R, 12.0\text{ N/kg})$, then decays smoothly as $\frac{1}{r^2}$ for $r > R$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly formulates linear interior formula $g \propto r$.
  • 2 Marks: Formulates inverse square exterior formula $g \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$.
  • 3 Marks: Correctly states full piecewise function.
  • 4 Marks: Calculates field at depth $r = 0.75 R$ as $9.00\text{ N/kg}$.
  • 5 Marks: Identifies peak value occurring at surface $(R, g_s)$.
  • 6 Marks: Describes complete graph features accurately.
