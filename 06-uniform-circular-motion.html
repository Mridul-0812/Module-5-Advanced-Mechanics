06. Speed, Period, Frequency & Angular Velocity

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 2

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Inquiry Question

Inquiry Question 2: Why do objects move in circles?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus, students must master the following specific outcomes regarding rotational kinematics and uniform circular motion:

  • Conduct a quantitative analysis of practical situations involving uniform circular motion to evaluate the relationships between orbital radius ($r$), linear speed ($v$), period ($T$), frequency ($f$), and angular velocity ($\omega$).
  • Express angular displacement ($\Delta \theta$) in dimensionless radians ($\text{rad}$) and convert fluently between angular measure units (degrees, radians, revolutions).
  • Derive, explain, and apply the primary mathematical expressions relating linear tangential speed and angular velocity: $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$ and $\omega = \frac{\Delta \theta}{\Delta t} = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$.
  • Distinguish between constant scalar speed ($v$) and continuously changing vector velocity ($\vec{v}$) in uniform circular motion.
  • Analyse coupled rotational systems (such as connected gears, sprockets, and belt-driven pulleys) where tangential speed is conserved across boundary contacts.

Deep Dive: Rotational Kinematics & Scalar vs Vector Dynamics

1. Defining Uniform Circular Motion (UCM)

When an object traces a circular path of fixed radius $r$ at a constant scalar speed $v$, it is executing Uniform Circular Motion (UCM). Examples abound in nature and technology: a satellite in a circular Earth orbit, a marble rolling around a circular track, a point on a spinning hard drive platter, or a rider on a fairground carousel.

The Paradox of UCM: Constant Speed, Changing Velocity!

In linear mechanics, "constant speed" often implies zero acceleration. In circular motion, however, this intuition fails completely!

  • Scalar Speed ($v$): The distance covered along the circular arc per unit time is constant. If a car completes one circular lap every $10\text{ s}$ at $15\text{ m/s}$, its scalar speed never fluctuates.
  • Vector Velocity ($\vec{v}$): Velocity is a vector quantity possessing both magnitude and spatial direction. At every instantaneous point along the circular arc, the direction of motion is directed tangentially to the circle. Because the direction of motion continuously rotates as the particle moves, the velocity vector $\vec{v}$ is continuously changing at every instant!
  • Physical Implication: Because velocity $\vec{v}$ changes direction continuously, an object in uniform circular motion is permanently accelerating, even though its speed is 100% constant! (We will explore the nature of this centripetal acceleration in Lesson 7).

Figure 1: Tangential velocity vectors $\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3$ at various points along a uniform circular path. Magnitude $|\vec{v}|$ is constant, but direction rotates continuously.

2. Time Parameters: Period ($T$) and Frequency ($f$)

To quantify the repetition rate of circular motion, we define two inversely related scalar time parameters:

1. Period ($T$):

The period ($T$) is defined as the time taken for an object to complete one full circular revolution ($360^\circ$ or $2\pi\text{ radians}$).

$$\text{SI Unit of Period } T = \text{seconds (s)}$$

If an object completes $N$ total revolutions in time $t_{\text{total}}$, its period is:

$$T = \frac{t_{\text{total}}}{N}$$

2. Frequency ($f$):

The frequency ($f$) is defined as the number of complete revolutions executed per unit time (per second).

$$\text{SI Unit of Frequency } f = \text{hertz (Hz)} = \text{revolutions per second (s}^{-1}\text{)}$$ $$f = \frac{N}{t_{\text{total}}}$$

Fundamental Inverse Relationship:

Period and frequency are exact mathematical reciprocals of each other:

Period-Frequency Reciprocal Relation

$$T = \frac{1}{f} \quad \iff \quad f = \frac{1}{T}$$

3. Angular Measure: The Radians Scale vs Degrees

In secondary school geometry, angles are traditionally measured in degrees ($360^\circ$ per full circle). However, in physics and rotational mechanics, measuring angles in degrees is clumsy because it introduces arbitrary factors of 360 into distance calculations.

Instead, NESA HSC Physics measures angular displacement $\Delta \theta$ in radians ($\text{rad}$). A radian is defined as the central angle subtended by a circular arc length $s$ that is exactly equal in length to the radius $r$ of the circle.

Arc Length & Radian Definition Formula

The angular displacement $\theta$ in radians is defined as the dimensionless ratio of arc length $s$ to radius $r$:

$$\theta (\text{in radians}) = \frac{\text{Arc Length } s}{\text{Radius } r} \implies \mathbf{s = r \theta}$$

Because the total circumference of a circle of radius $r$ is $s_{\text{full}} = 2\pi r$, one complete revolution ($360^\circ$) corresponds to:

$$\theta_{\text{full}} = \frac{2\pi r}{r} = 2\pi\text{ radians}$$

Unit Conversion Benchmarks:

  • Full Circle ($360^\circ$): $360^\circ = 2\pi\text{ rad} \approx 6.2832\text{ rad}$
  • Half Circle ($180^\circ$): $180^\circ = \pi\text{ rad} \approx 3.1416\text{ rad}$
  • Right Angle ($90^\circ$): $90^\circ = \frac{\pi}{2}\text{ rad} \approx 1.5708\text{ rad}$
  • One Radian ($1\text{ rad}$): $1\text{ rad} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.30^\circ$
  • Degrees to Radians Conversion Factor: Multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180^\circ}$
  • Radians to Degrees Conversion Factor: Multiply by $\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}$

4. Angular Velocity ($\omega$) and Tangential Linear Speed ($v$)

Just as linear velocity is the rate of change of linear displacement ($v = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$), angular velocity ($\omega$) is defined as the rate of change of angular displacement over time:

1. Angular Velocity ($\omega$) Formulations

The average angular velocity $\omega$ is defined as:

$$\omega = \frac{\Delta \theta}{\Delta t}$$ $$\text{SI Unit of Angular Velocity } \omega = \text{radians per second (rad/s or s}^{-1}\text{)}$$

For one complete revolution, the angular displacement is $\Delta \theta = 2\pi\text{ rad}$ and the time taken is one period $\Delta t = T$. Substituting these values gives the fundamental NESA formulas:

Core NESA Angular Velocity Equations

$$\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$$

2. Deriving the Relation Between Linear Speed $v$ and Angular Velocity $\omega$

Consider an object travelling along a circular path of radius $r$. In one period $T$, the total linear distance travelled along the circumference is $s = 2\pi r$.

Using the standard definition of linear speed $v = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}}$:

$$v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$$

By substituting $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}$ directly into this expression, we obtain the crucial bridge equation connecting linear tangential speed $v$ to angular velocity $\omega$:

Tangential Linear Speed & Angular Velocity Relation

$$v = \omega r$$

Physical Interpretation of $v = \omega r$: For a rigid rotating body (such as a spinning wheel or record turntable), every point on the body shares the exact same angular velocity $\omega$ (completing the same number of radians per second). However, points further from the central pivot axis (larger radius $r$) must travel a greater physical arc distance in the same time frame, and therefore possess a proportionally larger linear tangential speed $v$!

5. Unit Conversion: Revolutions Per Minute (RPM) to SI Units

HSC examination questions frequently state rotational rates in engineering units such as Revolutions Per Minute (RPM). Converting RPM to SI units ($\text{rad/s}$ and $\text{Hz}$) is a mandatory skill:

Standard Conversion Algorithm for RPM

To convert $N\text{ RPM}$ into angular velocity $\omega\text{ (in rad/s)}$:

  1. Convert revolutions to radians: Multiply by $2\pi\text{ rad/rev}$.
  2. Convert minutes to seconds: Divide by $60\text{ s/min}$.
$$\omega (\text{rad/s}) = N \text{ RPM} \times \frac{2\pi \text{ rad}}{60 \text{ s}} = N \times \frac{\pi}{30}$$

To convert $N\text{ RPM}$ into frequency $f\text{ (in Hz)}$:

$$f (\text{Hz}) = \frac{N \text{ RPM}}{60}$$

6. Coupled Rotational Systems: Gears, Belt Drives & Sprockets

In multi-component mechanical systems (such as a bicycle chain connecting front and rear sprockets, or meshed gear trains), the boundary conditions dictate how rotational parameters transmit:

Coupling Type Physical Mechanism Conserved Dynamic Parameter Governing Mathematical Ratio
1. Co-Axial Rotation (Same Shaft) Two disks or wheels locked on the same central spinning axle (e.g. inner and outer tracks of a record, or car wheel and axle). Angular Velocity is Conserved:
$\omega_1 = \omega_2$		 $$\frac{v_1}{r_1} = \frac{v_2}{r_2} \implies v_2 = v_1 \left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)$$
Outer radius points travel faster linearly.
2. Tangential Coupling (Meshed Gears / Belt Drive) Two wheels connected by a non-slip chain/belt, or interlocked gear teeth pushing against each other at their outer rims. Linear Tangential Speed is Conserved:
$v_1 = v_2$		 $$\omega_1 r_1 = \omega_2 r_2 \implies \frac{\omega_2}{\omega_1} = \frac{r_1}{r_2}$$
Smaller wheel rotates at a higher angular velocity!

Four Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 (Easy): RPM Conversion & Carousel Kinematics

[Rotational Unit Conversion & Basic Mechanics]

A fairground carousel rotates at a steady rate of $18.0\text{ RPM}$ (revolutions per minute). A child sits on a wooden horse positioned at a radial distance $r = 4.20\text{ m}$ from the central pivot axis. Calculate:

a) The rotation frequency $f$ in hertz ($\text{Hz}$) and period $T$ in seconds ($\text{s}$).
b) The angular velocity $\omega$ of the carousel in radians per second ($\text{rad/s}$).
c) The linear tangential speed $v$ of the child.

Step-by-Step Mathematical Solution:

Part a) Frequency ($f$) and Period ($T$):

Frequency is the number of revolutions per second:

$$f = \frac{N \text{ RPM}}{60} = \frac{18.0}{60} = \mathbf{0.300\text{ Hz}}$$

Period $T$ is the reciprocal of frequency:

$$T = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{0.300} = \mathbf{3.33\text{ s}}\quad (\text{to 3 sig. figs.})$$

Part b) Angular Velocity ($\omega$):

Apply the NESA formula $\omega = 2\pi f$:

$$\omega = 2\pi f = 2 \times \pi \times 0.300 = 0.600 \pi = \mathbf{1.88\text{ rad/s}}\quad (\text{to 3 sig. figs.})$$

Part c) Linear Tangential Speed ($v$):

Apply the bridge formula $v = \omega r$:

$$v = \omega r = (1.8850) \times 4.20 = \mathbf{7.92\text{ m/s}}\quad (\text{to 3 sig. figs.})$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Planetary Rotation & Equatorial Speed

[Geophysics Rotational Application]

Earth rotates on its axis once every $23\text{ hours, } 56\text{ minutes, and } 4.0\text{ seconds}$ (one sidereal day = $86164\text{ s}$). Assuming Earth is a perfect sphere of mean radius $R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$, calculate:

a) Earth's axial angular velocity $\omega_E$ in $\text{rad/s}$.
b) The linear tangential speed $v_{\text{eq}}$ of a person standing on the equator.
c) The linear tangential speed $v_{60}$ of a research station located at $60.0^\circ\text{ North latitude}$ (where radial distance to the rotation axis is $r = R_E \cos(60.0^\circ)$).

Step-by-Step Mathematical Solution:

Part a) Angular Velocity of Earth ($\omega_E$):

Period $T = 86164\text{ s}$. Apply $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}$:

$$\omega_E = \frac{2\pi}{86164} = \mathbf{7.292 \times 10^{-5}\text{ rad/s}}\quad (\text{to 4 sig. figs.})$$

Part b) Equatorial Linear Speed ($v_{\text{eq}}$):

At the equator, radial distance from the spin axis equals full Earth radius $r = R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}$.

$$v_{\text{eq}} = \omega_E R_E = (7.2921 \times 10^{-5}) \times (6.371 \times 10^6) = \mathbf{464.6\text{ m/s}}\quad (\approx 1673\text{ km/h}!)$$

Part c) Linear Speed at $60.0^\circ\text{ North Latitude}$ ($v_{60}$):

Calculate radial distance $r_{60}$ perpendicular to the Earth's rotation axis:

$$r_{60} = R_E \cos(60.0^\circ) = (6.371 \times 10^6) \times 0.5000 = 3.1855 \times 10^6\text{ m}$$

Apply $v = \omega r$ using the same axial angular velocity $\omega_E$:

$$v_{60} = \omega_E r_{60} = (7.2921 \times 10^{-5}) \times (3.1855 \times 10^6) = \mathbf{232.3\text{ m/s}}$$

Notice that because the research station is closer to the spin axis, its linear speed is exactly half its equatorial value!

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Coupled Bicycle Drive Sprocket Analysis

[Tangential Coupling & Ratio Analysis]

A racing bicycle drive train consists of a front chainring (radius $r_1 = 11.0\text{ cm}$) connected by a non-slip steel chain to a rear wheel cog (radius $r_2 = 4.50\text{ cm}$). The rear cog is fixed co-axially to the rear bicycle wheel (radius $R_W = 34.0\text{ cm}$). A cyclist pedals the front chainring at a steady cadence of $90.0\text{ RPM}$.

a) Calculate the angular velocity $\omega_1$ of the front chainring in $\text{rad/s}$.
b) Calculate the linear speed $v_{\text{chain}}$ of the connecting drive chain.
c) Calculate the angular velocity $\omega_2$ of the rear cog/wheel assembly.
d) Calculate the forward speed $v_{\text{bike}}$ of the bicycle along the road surface.

Step-by-Step Mechanical System Analysis:

Part a) Angular Velocity of Front Chainring ($\omega_1$):

$$\omega_1 = 90.0 \text{ RPM} \times \frac{2\pi}{60} = 3.00 \pi = \mathbf{9.425\text{ rad/s}}$$

Part b) Chain Linear Speed ($v_{\text{chain}}$):

Convert $r_1 = 11.0\text{ cm} = 0.110\text{ m}$. Applying $v = \omega r$ at the front chainring rim:

$$v_{\text{chain}} = \omega_1 r_1 = (9.4248) \times 0.110 = \mathbf{1.037\text{ m/s}}$$

Part c) Angular Velocity of Rear Cog ($\omega_2$):

Because the chain does not slip, the tangential speed at the rim of the rear cog equals the chain speed ($v_2 = v_{\text{chain}} = 1.037\text{ m/s}$). Convert $r_2 = 4.50\text{ cm} = 0.0450\text{ m}$.

$$v_2 = \omega_2 r_2 \implies \omega_2 = \frac{v_{\text{chain}}}{r_2} = \frac{1.0367}{0.0450} = \mathbf{23.04\text{ rad/s}}$$

Alternative Gear Ratio Method: $\omega_2 = \omega_1 \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right) = 9.4248 \times \left(\frac{11.0}{4.50}\right) = 23.04\text{ rad/s}$.

Part d) Forward Bicycle Road Speed ($v_{\text{bike}}$):

The rear wheel rotates co-axially with the rear cog, sharing the exact same angular velocity ($\omega_{\text{wheel}} = \omega_2 = 23.037\text{ rad/s}$). Convert wheel radius $R_W = 34.0\text{ cm} = 0.340\text{ m}$. Assuming zero tire slip on the road, forward speed equals tangential rim speed:

$$v_{\text{bike}} = \omega_{\text{wheel}} R_W = 23.037 \times 0.340 = \mathbf{7.83\text{ m/s}}\quad (\approx 28.2\text{ km/h}!)$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension): Relativistic Satellite Speed & Angular Frequency Shift

[Upper-Level HSC Spatial Mechanics]

A satellite moves in a circular Low Earth Orbit (LEO) at an altitude $h = 400\text{ km}$ above Earth's surface ($R_E = 6371\text{ km}$). Its orbital period is measured to be $T = 92.6\text{ minutes}$.

a) Calculate the total orbital radius $r$ from Earth's centre in metres.
b) Calculate the orbital angular velocity $\omega_{\text{sat}}$ in $\text{rad/s}$.
c) Calculate the orbital linear speed $v_{\text{orbit}}$ in $\text{m/s}$ and $\text{km/h}$.
d) Determine the angle $\theta_{\text{sweep}}$ (in degrees) swept out by the satellite in a time interval of $\Delta t = 15.0\text{ minutes}$.

Step-by-Step Orbital Solutions:

Part a) Total Orbital Radius ($r$):

$$r = R_E + h = 6371\text{ km} + 400\text{ km} = 6771\text{ km} = \mathbf{6.771 \times 10^6\text{ m}}$$

Part b) Angular Velocity ($\omega_{\text{sat}}$):

Convert period $T$ from minutes to seconds:

$$T = 92.6\text{ minutes} \times 60\text{ s/min} = 5556\text{ s}$$ $$\omega_{\text{sat}} = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{5556} = \mathbf{1.131 \times 10^{-3}\text{ rad/s}}$$

Part c) Orbital Linear Speed ($v_{\text{orbit}}$):

$$v_{\text{orbit}} = \omega_{\text{sat}} r = (1.13089 \times 10^{-3}) \times (6.771 \times 10^6) = \mathbf{7657\text{ m/s}} \approx \mathbf{7.66\text{ km/s}}$$

In $\text{km/h}$:

$$v = 7.6572 \text{ km/s} \times 3600 \text{ s/h} = \mathbf{27566\text{ km/h}}$$

Part d) Angular Displacement Swept Out in $15.0\text{ minutes}$ ($\theta_{\text{sweep}}$):

Convert time interval $\Delta t = 15.0\text{ min} \times 60 = 900\text{ s}$.

$$\Delta \theta = \omega_{\text{sat}} \Delta t = (1.13089 \times 10^{-3}) \times 900 = 1.0178\text{ radians}$$

Convert radians to degrees:

$$\theta_{\text{sweep}} = 1.0178 \times \left(\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}\right) = \mathbf{58.3^\circ}$$

Practise Question 1 (Easy): Computer Hard Drive Angular Velocity

[3 Marks]

A computer mechanical hard drive platter spins at a constant rate of $7200\text{ RPM}$. Calculate:

a) The rotation frequency $f$ in $\text{Hz}$.
b) The angular velocity $\omega$ in $\text{rad/s}$.
c) The time period $T$ for one complete revolution in milliseconds ($\text{ms}$).

Hint: Divide RPM by 60 to find $f$ in Hz. Use $\omega = 2\pi f$ for angular velocity, and $T = 1/f$ for period. Multiply seconds by 1000 to get milliseconds.

a) Frequency ($f$):

$$f = \frac{7200}{60} = \mathbf{120\text{ Hz}}$$

b) Angular Velocity ($\omega$):

$$\omega = 2\pi f = 2 \times \pi \times 120 = 240 \pi = \mathbf{754\text{ rad/s}}\quad (\text{to 3 sig. figs.})$$

c) Time Period ($T$):

$$T = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{120} = 0.008333\text{ s} = \mathbf{8.33\text{ ms}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Converting 7200 RPM to frequency gives $f = \frac{7200}{60} = 120\text{ Hz}$. The angular velocity is $\omega = 2\pi f = 240\pi = 754\text{ rad/s}$. The period for one revolution is $T = \frac{1}{120} = 8.33 \times 10^{-3}\text{ s} = 8.33\text{ ms}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates rotation frequency $f = 120\text{ Hz}$.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly computes angular velocity $\omega = 754\text{ rad/s}$.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains time period $8.33\text{ ms}$ with correct SI prefix and units.

Practise Question 2 (Medium): Ultracentrifuge Radial Speed Gradient

[4 Marks]

A laboratory ultracentrifuge spins biological samples at $45000\text{ RPM}$. Test tube A is situated at radial distance $r_A = 6.00\text{ cm}$ from the central rotor axis, while test tube B is situated at $r_B = 12.0\text{ cm}$.

a) State with reason whether test tube A or test tube B experiences the greater angular velocity $\omega$.
b) Calculate the angular velocity $\omega$ of the rotor in $\text{rad/s}$.
c) Calculate the linear tangential speeds $v_A$ and $v_B$ for both test tubes.

Hint: Both test tubes are locked on the same rigid rotor shaft, so they share the exact same angular velocity $\omega$. Use $v = \omega r$ with radii converted to metres ($0.0600\text{ m}$ and $0.120\text{ m}$).

a) Angular Velocity Comparison:

Both test tubes experience the exact same angular velocity ($\omega_A = \omega_B$). Because they are co-axially mounted on the same rigid spinning rotor, they complete identical angular displacements ($\Delta \theta$) in any given time interval.

b) Angular Velocity Calculation ($\omega$):

$$\omega = 45000 \text{ RPM} \times \frac{2\pi}{60} = 1500 \pi = \mathbf{4712\text{ rad/s}}\quad (\text{to 4 sig. figs.})$$

c) Linear Speeds $v_A$ and $v_B$:

Convert radii to metres: $r_A = 0.0600\text{ m}$, $r_B = 0.120\text{ m}$.

$$v_A = \omega r_A = 4712.4 \times 0.0600 = \mathbf{283\text{ m/s}}$$ $$v_B = \omega r_B = 4712.4 \times 0.120 = \mathbf{565\text{ m/s}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Because both test tubes are mounted co-axially on the same rigid rotor, their angular velocity is identical ($\omega_A = \omega_B$). Converting 45000 RPM gives $\omega = \frac{45000 \times 2\pi}{60} = 4712\text{ rad/s}$. Applying $v = \omega r$ yields $v_A = 4712 \times 0.0600 = 283\text{ m/s}$ and $v_B = 4712 \times 0.120 = 565\text{ m/s}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly states that angular velocity $\omega$ is identical for both co-axial positions.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates rotor angular velocity $\omega = 4712\text{ rad/s}$.
  • 3 Marks: Computes linear speed $v_A = 283\text{ m/s}$.
  • 4 Marks: Computes linear speed $v_B = 565\text{ m/s}$ with correct SI units.

Practise Question 3 (Hard): Belt-Driven Pulley System Ratio

[5 Marks]

An electric motor drives Pulley 1 (radius $r_1 = 8.00\text{ cm}$) at $1200\text{ RPM}$. A non-slip rubber belt connects Pulley 1 to a larger Pulley 2 (radius $r_2 = 24.0\text{ cm}$).

a) State the conserved parameter across the non-slip drive belt.
b) Calculate the linear tangential speed $v_{\text{belt}}$ of the drive belt.
c) Calculate the angular velocity $\omega_2$ and RPM rotation rate $N_2$ of Pulley 2.
d) Explain why factory machinery uses pulley size variations to modify rotational speeds.

Hint: Non-slip belt coupling conserves linear tangential speed ($v_1 = v_2 = v_{\text{belt}}$). Calculate $v_1 = \omega_1 r_1$, then set $\omega_2 = v_{\text{belt}} / r_2$.

a) Conserved Parameter:

Linear tangential speed ($v_{\text{belt}}$) is conserved across the outer rims of both pulleys because the belt does not slip.

b) Belt Speed ($v_{\text{belt}}$):

$$\omega_1 = 1200 \text{ RPM} \times \frac{2\pi}{60} = 40\pi = 125.66\text{ rad/s}$$ $$v_{\text{belt}} = \omega_1 r_1 = 125.66 \times 0.0800 = \mathbf{10.05\text{ m/s}} \quad (\approx 10.1\text{ m/s})$$

c) Angular Velocity & RPM of Pulley 2:

$$\omega_2 = \frac{v_{\text{belt}}}{r_2} = \frac{10.053}{0.240} = \mathbf{41.89\text{ rad/s}} \quad (\approx 41.9\text{ rad/s})$$ $$N_2 = \omega_2 \times \frac{60}{2\pi} = 41.888 \times 9.5493 = \mathbf{400\text{ RPM}}$$

Notice the 1:3 inverse ratio: because Pulley 2 has 3 times the radius, it rotates at 1/3 the RPM!

d) Mechanical Explanation:

Pulleys act as mechanical torque and speed transformers. Stepping down rotational speed (from $1200\text{ RPM}$ to $400\text{ RPM}$) proportionally increases output torque, allowing high-speed low-torque motors to drive heavy industrial machinery.

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Because the drive belt does not slip, linear tangential speed is conserved ($v_1 = v_2 = v_{\text{belt}}$). Pulley 1 spins at $\omega_1 = \frac{1200 \times 2\pi}{60} = 125.7\text{ rad/s}$, giving belt speed $v_{\text{belt}} = 125.7 \times 0.0800 = 10.1\text{ m/s}$. Equating rim speeds gives $\omega_2 = \frac{10.05}{0.240} = 41.9\text{ rad/s}$, corresponding to $400\text{ RPM}$. Varying pulley radius ratios allows machinery to step down motor speed and multiply output torque."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies linear tangential speed conservation across the non-slip belt.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates correct drive belt linear speed $10.1\text{ m/s}$.
  • 3 Marks: Computes angular velocity $\omega_2 = 41.9\text{ rad/s}$.
  • 4 Marks: Converts angular velocity to RPM rate ($400\text{ RPM}$).
  • 5 Marks: Provides clear mechanical explanation linking speed reduction to torque multiplication.

Practise Question 4 (Extension): Geostationary Orbital Radius Derivation

[6 Marks]

A geostationary satellite orbits Earth directly above the equator such that its orbital position remains completely fixed relative to a ground station on Earth's surface.

a) Explain why a geostationary satellite must possess an orbital period $T$ exactly equal to Earth's axial rotation period ($86164\text{ s}$).
b) Calculate the mandatory orbital angular velocity $\omega_{\text{geo}}$ of a geostationary satellite.
c) Given that orbital linear speed for a circular gravity orbit satisfies $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$ (where $GM_{\text{Earth}} = 3.986 \times 10^{14}\text{ m}^3\text{s}^{-2}$), combine this expression with $v = \omega r$ to derive the explicit algebraic formula for geostationary orbital radius $r_{\text{geo}}$ in terms of $G, M,$ and $\omega$.
d) Calculate the numerical orbital radius $r_{\text{geo}}$ in kilometres ($\text{km}$).

Hint: Equate the two linear velocity expressions: $\omega r = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} \implies \omega^2 r^2 = \frac{GM}{r} \implies r^3 = \frac{GM}{\omega^2}$. Substitute $\omega_{\text{geo}} = \frac{2\pi}{86164}$.

a) Requirement for Geostationary Period:

To remain stationary relative to a ground location, the satellite must match Earth's axial rotation rate ($\omega_{\text{sat}} = \omega_E$). Thus, its orbital period must equal one sidereal day ($T = 86164\text{ s}$).

b) Angular Velocity ($\omega_{\text{geo}}$):

$$\omega_{\text{geo}} = \frac{2\pi}{86164} = \mathbf{7.2921 \times 10^{-5}\text{ rad/s}}$$

c) Algebraic Derivation of $r_{\text{geo}}$:

Equate the kinematic relation $v = \omega r$ to the orbital velocity equation $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$:

$$\omega r = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$$

Square both sides:

$$\omega^2 r^2 = \frac{GM}{r}$$

Multiply both sides by $r$ and divide by $\omega^2$:

$$r^3 = \frac{GM}{\omega^2} \implies \mathbf{r_{\text{geo}} = \sqrt[3]{\frac{GM}{\omega^2}}}$$

d) Numerical Calculation of Orbital Radius ($r_{\text{geo}}$):

$$r_{\text{geo}} = \sqrt[3]{\frac{3.986 \times 10^{14}}{(7.2921 \times 10^{-5})^2}} = \sqrt[3]{\frac{3.986 \times 10^{14}}{5.3175 \times 10^{-9}}} = \sqrt[3]{7.496 \times 10^{22}}$$ $$r_{\text{geo}} = 4.2164 \times 10^7\text{ m} = \mathbf{42164\text{ km}}$$

(Subtracting Earth's radius $6371\text{ km}$ gives the famous geostationary altitude of $\approx 35793\text{ km}$ above sea level!)
