14. Satellite Orbits: LEO vs GEO (and MEO / Polar)

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 4 (Part 2)

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Core Learning Goals

Inquiry Question 4: How does Kepler's laws of planetary motion describe the movements of natural and artificial satellites?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus (calculus-free), students must thoroughly analyse and compare different satellite orbits used around Earth:

  • Compare Low Earth Orbit (LEO) and Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO) satellites in terms of altitude ($h$), orbital period ($T$), orbital velocity ($v$), inclination angle, and applications.
  • Evaluate the physical criteria required for a satellite to achieve a Geostationary Orbit (equatorial plane, $0^\circ$ inclination, prograde rotation, period $T = 23\text{h } 56\text{m } 4\text{s}$).
  • Distinguish Geostationary Orbit (GEO) from Geosynchronous Orbit (GSO).
  • Analyse Low Earth Orbit variants such as Sun-Synchronous and Polar Orbits for complete Earth mapping and observation.
  • Compare trade-offs of LEO vs GEO regarding radio signal latency ($t = \frac{2r}{c}$), atmospheric drag/decay, launch energy, and surface coverage.

Part 1: The Intuitive Idea — Two Different Jobs for Satellites

Beginner Analogy: The "Express Train" vs The "Hovering Billboard"

Imagine two very different ways to observe or communicate with a city on Earth from above:

  1. Low Earth Orbit (LEO): The Fast Express Train. Imagine flying in a jet right above the rooftops ($160\text{ to } 2,000\text{ km}$ up). You get an incredibly sharp, close-up view of details on the ground! But because you are so close, you are zooming across the sky at $28,000\text{ km/h}$, passing overhead in just 10 minutes before disappearing over the horizon.
  2. Geostationary Orbit (GEO): The Stationary Hovering Platform. Imagine sitting in a hot air balloon extremely high in the sky ($35,786\text{ km}$ up). You are so far away that you can see an entire hemisphere at once! More importantly, as Earth spins underneath, you move in sync with it. To someone standing on the ground below, you appear to hang frozen in the exact same spot in the sky 24 hours a day!

Clearing Up a Huge Student Misconception: "Does a Geostationary Satellite Stay Up Because Gravity is Zero?"

NO! Gravity at GEO altitude is NOT zero!

At geostationary altitude ($r \approx 42,164\text{ km}$ from Earth's center), Earth's gravitational acceleration is still $g = \frac{G M_E}{r^2} \approx 0.22\text{ N/kg}$ (about 2.3% of surface gravity). The satellite stays in orbit because it moves sideways at an exact speed of $v = 3.07\text{ km/s}$ ($11,000\text{ km/h}$), so its curved falling path matches Earth's circular radius, while its orbital rotation speed matches Earth's daily spin!

Part 3: Deep Technical Insights & Exam-Level Distinction

1. The 4 Mandatory Criteria for a Geostationary Orbit (GEO)

In HSC Physics exams, students are frequently asked to "State and explain the conditions necessary for a satellite to be geostationary." To earn full marks, you must state all 4 criteria:

The Four Geostationary Requirements

  1. Orbital Period Equals Earth's Sidereal Rotation Period: $T = 23\text{ hours } 56\text{ minutes } 4.1\text{ seconds}$ ($86,164\text{ s}$). This ensures the satellite completes one rotation in the exact time Earth spins once relative to background stars.
  2. Circular Equatorial Plane (Inclination $i = 0^\circ$): The orbit MUST lie directly above Earth's equator. If the orbit is inclined at an angle (e.g., $20^\circ$), the satellite will drift North and South across the sky during the day.
  3. Prograde Direction (West-to-East Rotation): The satellite must orbit in the exact same direction as Earth's rotation (counter-clockwise when viewed from above the North Pole). If it orbits East-to-West, it will sweep across Earth's surface twice per day!
  4. Specific Altitude / Radius ($h = 35,786\text{ km}$): By $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G M_E}$, matching $T = 86,164\text{ s}$ uniquely fixes the orbital radius to $r = 42,164\text{ km}$, placing the altitude strictly at $h = 35,786\text{ km}$.

Band 6 Distinction: GEO vs GSO (Geostationary vs Geosynchronous)

Geosynchronous Orbit (GSO): ANY orbit with a period of 24 hours ($T = 86,164\text{ s}$). It can have an elliptical shape or an inclined plane ($i > 0^\circ$). To an observer on Earth, a GSO satellite traces a figure-eight path ("analemma") in the sky every day.

Geostationary Orbit (GEO): A special subset of GSO that is strictly **CIRCULAR** and strictly **EQUATORIAL** ($i = 0^\circ$). It remains completely motionless relative to the ground!

$$\text{All GEO satellites are GSO, but NOT all GSO satellites are GEO!}$$

2. Signal Latency & The Speed of Light Limit

Radio signals travel at the speed of light ($c = 3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$). The round-trip delay (latency) for a radio signal sent from ground station $\to$ satellite $\to$ ground station is:

$$t_{\text{latency}} = \frac{2 \times \text{Distance}}{c}$$
  • For LEO ($h = 500\text{ km}$): Round-trip distance $\approx 1,000\text{ km}$. Latency $t \approx \frac{1.0 \times 10^6}{3.00 \times 10^8} \approx \mathbf{0.0033\text{ s}} \quad (3.3\text{ ms})$. Perfect for high-speed online gaming and voice calls!
  • For GEO ($h = 35,786\text{ km}$): Round-trip distance $\approx 71,572\text{ km}$. Latency $t \approx \frac{7.1572 \times 10^7}{3.00 \times 10^8} \approx \mathbf{0.238\text{ s}} \quad (238\text{ ms})$. Causes noticeable audio delays during live TV news broadcasts!

Four Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 (Easy): LEO vs GEO Speed & Period Comparison

[Basic Orbital Comparison]

Satellite A is in Low Earth Orbit at altitude $h_A = 500\text{ km}$ ($r_A = 6.871 \times 10^6\text{ m}$). Satellite B is in Geostationary Orbit at altitude $h_B = 35,786\text{ km}$ ($r_B = 4.2157 \times 10^7\text{ m}$). ($M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$).

a) Calculate the orbital speeds $v_A$ and $v_B$.
b) Calculate the ratio of their orbital periods ($T_B / T_A$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Orbital Speeds ($v = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{r}}$):

$$v_A = \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}{6.871 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.9857 \times 10^{14}}{6.871 \times 10^6}} = \mathbf{7616\text{ m/s}} \quad (\mathbf{7.62\text{ km/s}})$$ $$v_B = \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}{4.2157 \times 10^7}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.9857 \times 10^{14}}{4.2157 \times 10^7}} = \mathbf{3075\text{ m/s}} \quad (\mathbf{3.08\text{ km/s}})$$

Part b) Period Ratio ($T_B / T_A$):

$$T_A = \frac{2\pi r_A}{v_A} = \frac{2\pi (6.871 \times 10^6)}{7616} = 5668\text{ s} \quad (94.5\text{ mins})$$ $$T_B = \frac{2\pi r_B}{v_B} = \frac{2\pi (4.2157 \times 10^7)}{3075} = 86,133\text{ s} \quad (23.9\text{ hours})$$ $$\frac{T_B}{T_A} = \frac{86,133}{5668} = \mathbf{15.2 \text{ times longer period for GEO}}$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Signal Delay Calculation for GEO Satellite Communications

[Speed of Light Latency Analysis]

A mobile phone user in Sydney makes an international VoIP call via a geostationary communications satellite ($h = 35,786\text{ km}$). Assuming the ground station is directly below the satellite ($r = 4.216 \times 10^7\text{ m}$):

a) Calculate the minimum time required for a radio signal to travel from Sydney to the satellite and back to a receiving ground station ($c = 3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$).
b) Explain why high online gaming performance is significantly superior using a LEO Starlink constellation ($h = 550\text{ km}$) compared to a GEO satellite dish.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Latency Calculation:

The signal travels up to the satellite ($35,786\text{ km}$) and back down ($35,786\text{ km}$). Total distance $d = 2 \times 3.5786 \times 10^7\text{ m} = 7.1572 \times 10^7\text{ m}$.

$$t = \frac{d}{c} = \frac{7.1572 \times 10^7\text{ m}}{3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}} = \mathbf{0.239\text{ s}} \quad (\mathbf{239\text{ milliseconds}})$$

Part b) LEO vs GEO Latency Explanation:

For LEO ($h = 550\text{ km}$), the round-trip distance is $2 \times 550 = 1,100\text{ km}$. The signal latency is $t = \frac{1.10 \times 10^6}{3.00 \times 10^8} \approx \mathbf{3.67\text{ ms}}$. This is over 60 times faster than GEO, eliminating lag in real-time online applications!

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Evaluating LEO Atmospheric Decay & Re-boost Energy

[Atmospheric Drag & Orbital Energy]

The International Space Station ($m = 420,000\text{ kg}$) orbits at $h = 400\text{ km}$ ($r = 6.771 \times 10^6\text{ m}$). Due to subtle atmospheric drag from the thermosphere, its altitude drops by $2.0\text{ km}$ per month ($r_{\text{new}} = 6.769 \times 10^6\text{ m}$).

a) Calculate the station's initial and final orbital speed.
b) Explain the paradox of why atmospheric drag causes the ISS to *speed up* as it decays!
c) Calculate the energy required to boost the ISS back to $400\text{ km}$ ($M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Orbital Speeds:

$$v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{6.771 \times 10^6}} = \mathbf{7672.1\text{ m/s}}$$ $$v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{6.769 \times 10^6}} = \mathbf{7673.2\text{ m/s}} \quad (\text{Station sped up by } 1.1\text{ m/s}!)$$

Part b) Orbital Decay Speed Paradox:

Atmospheric drag removes total mechanical energy ($E = -\frac{G M m}{2 r}$). As total energy decreases (becomes more negative), orbital radius $r$ shrinks. Because $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$, a smaller radius requires a HIGHER orbital speed to maintain orbit. Gravitational potential energy lost is twice the kinetic energy gained ($\Delta U = -2 \Delta K$), so half the potential energy lost goes into increasing kinetic speed, while half is lost as thermal friction!

Part c) Re-boost Energy ($\Delta E$):

$$\Delta E = E_1 - E_2 = \left( -\frac{G M_E m}{2 r_1} \right) - \left( -\frac{G M_E m}{2 r_2} \right) = \frac{G M_E m}{2} \left( \frac{1}{r_2} - \frac{1}{r_1} \right)$$ $$\Delta E = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})(420000)}{2} \left( \frac{1}{6.769 \times 10^6} - \frac{1}{6.771 \times 10^6} \right)$$ $$\Delta E = (8.370 \times 10^{19}) \times (4.37 \times 10^{-11}) = \mathbf{3.66 \times 10^9\text{ Joules}} \quad (\mathbf{3.66\text{ GJ}})$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension - JRAHS Style): Polar Orbit Earth Mapping Overpass Frequency

[Polar Geometry & Earth Spin Integration]

A Earth-observation satellite is placed in a circular Polar LEO orbit ($i = 90^\circ$) at altitude $h = 850\text{ km}$ ($r = 7.221 \times 10^6\text{ m}$).

a) Calculate the satellite's orbital period $T$ in minutes.
b) Calculate the longitude shift ($\Delta \lambda$ in degrees) of Earth's surface beneath the satellite during each successive orbit pass ($M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}, T_{\text{Earth}} = 23\text{h } 56\text{m } 4\text{s} = 86,164\text{ s}$).
c) Explain why polar orbits are chosen over equatorial orbits for global environmental mapping.

Full Band 6 JRAHS Solution:

Part a) Orbital Period ($T$):

$$T = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G M_E}} = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 \times (7.221 \times 10^6)^3}{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.487 \times 10^{22}}{3.9857 \times 10^{14}}} = \sqrt{3.731 \times 10^7} = \mathbf{6108\text{ s}} \quad (\mathbf{101.8\text{ mins}})$$

Part b) Longitude Shift per Pass ($\Delta \lambda$):

As the satellite flies North-to-South over the poles, Earth rotates West-to-East beneath it. The fraction of a full $360^\circ$ turn made by Earth during one satellite orbit is:

$$\Delta \lambda = 360^\circ \times \left( \frac{T_{\text{sat}}}{T_{\text{Earth}}} \right) = 360^\circ \times \left( \frac{6108\text{ s}}{86,164\text{ s}} \right) = 360^\circ \times 0.070887 = \mathbf{25.5^\circ \text{ Westward Shift}}$$

Part c) Advantage of Polar Orbits for Global Mapping:

An equatorial orbit ($i = 0^\circ$) stays directly above the equator and can never scan high-latitude regions (like North America, Europe, Antarctica). A polar orbit ($i = 90^\circ$) crosses both poles on every revolution while Earth rotates $25.5^\circ$ underneath each orbit. Over approximately 14 orbits ($\approx 24\text{ hours}$), the satellite scans every single square kilometer of Earth's surface!

Practise Question 1 (Easy): Geostationary Orbit Criteria Identification

[3 Marks]

State THREE necessary orbital conditions for a satellite to remain stationary relative to a point on Earth's surface.

Hint: Think about orbital plane (equator), period (24h), direction (prograde), and altitude ($35,786\text{ km}$).

1. Must be in a circular orbit directly above Earth's equator ($0^\circ$ inclination).

2. Must have an orbital period equal to Earth's sidereal rotation period ($23\text{h } 56\text{m } 4\text{s} \approx 24\text{ hours}$).

3. Must orbit in the same direction as Earth's rotation (prograde / West to East) at altitude $35,786\text{ km}$.

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"For a satellite to be geostationary, it must satisfy three core conditions: (1) Its orbit must lie in Earth's equatorial plane ($0^\circ$ inclination). (2) Its orbital period must match Earth's rotational period ($T = 23\text{h } 56\text{m } 4\text{s}$). (3) It must travel in the same direction as Earth's rotation (prograde, West-to-East)."

Practise Question 2 (Medium): Comparing LEO vs GEO Communication Trade-offs

[4 Marks]

Compare Low Earth Orbit (LEO) and Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO) satellites for global telecommunication services, outlining ONE advantage and ONE disadvantage of each.

Hint: Consider latency, ground dish tracking, coverage area per satellite, and atmospheric drag/lifespan.

LEO Satellites ($160 - 2000\text{ km}$):

  • Advantage: Very low signal latency ($\sim 5-15\text{ ms}$), enabling real-time video calls and fast internet.
  • Disadvantage: Small surface footprint requires large constellations (thousands of satellites) with motorized ground tracking dish receivers.

GEO Satellites ($35,786\text{ km}$):

  • Advantage: Remains stationary in sky; a single satellite covers $40\%$ of Earth's surface using fixed, non-tracking receiver dishes.
  • Disadvantage: High signal latency ($\sim 240\text{ ms}$) due to large speed-of-light distance delay.
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"LEO satellites offer the advantage of extremely low signal latency ($\sim 10\text{ ms}$) due to low altitude, but suffer the disadvantage of small coverage footprints requiring thousands of satellites in a constellation. Conversely, GEO satellites offer the advantage of remaining fixed overhead so a stationary dish can provide wide-area coverage ($\approx 40\%$ Earth surface), but suffer high signal latency ($\approx 240\text{ ms}$) due to the $35,786\text{ km}$ distance."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies LEO advantage (low latency) or disadvantage (constellation needed).
  • 2 Marks: Identifies GEO advantage (fixed dish / wide coverage) or disadvantage (high latency).
  • 3 Marks: Provides detailed technical comparison including numerical values/altitude context.
  • 4 Marks: Writes a balanced comparison clearly connecting physics principles to applications.

Practise Question 3 (Hard): Calculating Geostationary Radius & Altitude Derivation

[5 Marks]

Using Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation and circular motion mechanics, derive the exact orbital altitude $h$ of a geostationary satellite. ($M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}, R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\text{ m}, G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2, T = 86,164\text{ s}$).

Hint: Start from $F_c = F_g \implies T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G M_E}$. Solve for $r = \sqrt[3]{\frac{G M_E T^2}{4\pi^2}}$, then $h = r - R_E$. Use sidereal period $86,164\text{ s}$.

Step 1: Equate forces and rearrange for $r$:

$$\frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{G M_E m}{r^2} \implies v = \frac{2\pi r}{T} \implies \frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2} = \frac{G M_E}{r}$$ $$r^3 = \frac{G M_E T^2}{4\pi^2}$$

Step 2: Substitute values with sidereal period $T = 86,164\text{ s}$:

$$r^3 = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})(86164)^2}{4\pi^2} = \frac{(3.9857 \times 10^{14})(7.4242 \times 10^9)}{39.4784} = 7.4955 \times 10^{22}\text{ m}^3$$

Step 3: Cube root for orbital radius $r$:

$$r = (7.4955 \times 10^{22})^{1/3} = \mathbf{4.2164 \times 10^7\text{ m}} \quad (42,164\text{ km})$$

Step 4: Subtract Earth radius $R_E$:

$$h = r - R_E = 4.2164 \times 10^7 - 0.6371 \times 10^7 = \mathbf{3.5793 \times 10^7\text{ m}} \quad (\mathbf{35,793\text{ km}} \approx \mathbf{35,800\text{ km}})$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Equating centripetal force to gravity $\frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{G M_E m}{r^2}$ and substituting $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$ gives $r^3 = \frac{G M_E T^2}{4\pi^2}$. Using Earth's sidereal period $T = 86,164\text{ s}$, $r^3 = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})(86164)^2}{4\pi^2} = 7.496 \times 10^{22}\text{ m}^3$. Taking the cube root yields $r = 4.216 \times 10^7\text{ m}$. Altitude is $h = r - R_E = 4.216 \times 10^7 - 6.371 \times 10^6 = 3.579 \times 10^7\text{ m}$ ($35,790\text{ km}$)."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly sets up force equation $F_c = F_g$.
  • 2 Marks: Derives formula $r^3 = \frac{G M_E T^2}{4\pi^2}$.
  • 3 Marks: Uses correct sidereal period $86,164\text{ s}$ (or $86,400\text{ s}$).
  • 4 Marks: Obtains orbital radius $r = 4.216 \times 10^7\text{ m}$.
  • 5 Marks: Correctly subtracts Earth radius to give altitude $3.58 \times 10^7\text{ m}$.

Practise Question 4 (JRAHS Extension): Launch Energy Efficiency (Equatorial vs Polar Launch Sites)

[6 Marks]

Space agencies prefer launching geostationary communication satellites from launch sites near the equator (e.g. ESA Guiana Space Centre in Kourou, $5^\circ\text{N}$) rather than high-latitude sites (e.g. Baikonur, $46^\circ\text{N}$).

a) Calculate Earth's rotational speed $v_{\text{rot}}$ at the equator ($R_E = 6.378 \times 10^6\text{ m}, T = 86,164\text{ s}$).
b) Calculate the additional kinetic energy provided "for free" by Earth's rotation to a $3000\text{ kg}$ satellite launched eastward from the equator vs from the North Pole ($0\text{ m/s}$).
c) Explain TWO distinct physics reasons why equatorial launches save massive amounts of rocket fuel for GEO satellite placement.

Hint: $v_{\text{rot}} = \frac{2\pi R_E}{T}$. Energy saved is $K = \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{rot}}^2$. Consider initial boost + avoiding expensive orbital inclination plane changes!

a) Surface Rotational Speed at Equator ($v_{\text{rot}}$):

$$v_{\text{rot}} = \frac{2 \pi R_E}{T} = \frac{2 \pi \times 6.378 \times 10^6\text{ m}}{86,164\text{ s}} = \mathbf{465.1\text{ m/s}} \quad (\mathbf{1674\text{ km/h}})$$

b) Initial Kinetic Energy Boost ($\Delta K$):

$$\Delta K = \frac{1}{2} m v_{\text{rot}}^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 3000\text{ kg} \times (465.1\text{ m/s})^2 = \mathbf{3.24 \times 10^8\text{ Joules}} \quad (\mathbf{324\text{ MJ}})$$

c) Two Physics Reasons for Equatorial Launch Efficiency:

1. Rotational Speed Boost: Launching eastward at the equator provides an initial $465\text{ m/s}$ velocity boost toward the required orbital speed ($3.07\text{ km/s}$ at GEO), reducing the necessary rocket propulsive $\Delta v$.

2. Eliminating Plane Changes: GEO orbits MUST have an inclination of $0^\circ$. Launching from latitude $\theta$ (e.g. $46^\circ\text{N}$) places the satellite initially into a $46^\circ$ inclined orbit. Performing an orbital plane change maneuver to reduce inclination to $0^\circ$ at high altitude requires a massive vector thruster burn ($\Delta v = 2 v \sin(\theta/2)$), consuming huge quantities of extra propellant!

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Earth's equatorial surface speed is $v_{\text{rot}} = \frac{2\pi R_E}{T} = \frac{2\pi (6.378 \times 10^6)}{86164} = 465\text{ m/s}$. Launching eastward from the equator gives a $3000\text{ kg}$ payload $\frac{1}{2} (3000) (465)^2 = 324\text{ MJ}$ of initial kinetic energy for free. Equatorial launches save fuel for two reasons: (1) The rotational speed directly contributes toward orbital speed ($3.07\text{ km/s}$), reducing required $\Delta v$. (2) Launching from the equator automatically places the rocket into a $0^\circ$ inclination orbit, eliminating the need for high-energy orbital plane change burns."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Calculates equatorial rotation speed $v_{\text{rot}} = 465\text{ m/s}$.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates initial kinetic energy boost $324\text{ MJ}$.
  • 3 Marks: Explains rotational velocity boost effect on rocket propellant.
  • 4 Marks: Identifies that GEO requires $0^\circ$ equatorial inclination.
  • 5 Marks: Explains the energy cost of orbital plane change maneuvers ($\Delta v$).
  • 6 Marks: Writes a comprehensive, mathematically supported Band 6 evaluation.
