01. Vector Fundamentals

Phase 1: Projectile Motion — Inquiry Question 1

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Inquiry Question

Inquiry Question 1: How can models that are used to explain projectile motion be used to analyse and make predictions?

Syllabus Outcomes Addressed:

  • PH12-12: Analyses qualitative and quantitative data for two-dimensional motion to solve problems and make predictions.
  • PH11/12-1, 2 & 4: Develops models, processes quantitative vector data, and uses mathematical representations to communicate physical relationships.

Core NESA Knowledge Point: Analyse vectors in two dimensions by resolving them into two independent, mutually perpendicular components using right-angled trigonometry:

$$u_x = u \cos\theta \quad \text{and} \quad u_y = u \sin\theta$$

Preliminary Revision: Scalar vs Vector Quantities

In Year 11 Preliminary Physics (Modules 1 and 2), motion was analyzed primarily along a single straight line. Before extending kinematics into two dimensions, it is essential to re-establish the formal mathematical distinction between scalar and vector quantities.

  • Scalar Quantities: Physical quantities described fully by magnitude (size) and an appropriate SI unit, but possessing no direction in space. Examples include time ($t$), mass ($m$), distance ($d$), speed ($v$), work ($W$), and energy ($E$).
  • Vector Quantities: Physical quantities specified completely by a magnitude, an SI unit, and a spatial direction relative to an established Cartesian coordinate system. Examples include displacement ($\mathbf{s}$ or $\vec{s}$), velocity ($\mathbf{v}$ or $\vec{v}$), acceleration ($\mathbf{a}$ or $\vec{a}$), force ($\mathbf{F}$ or $\vec{F}$), and momentum ($\mathbf{p}$ or $\vec{p}$).

In two-dimensional space, a vector cannot be added or subtracted using simple arithmetic. Instead, geometric vector addition (using the head-to-tail method or parallelogram law) or analytical trigonometric decomposition must be employed.

Deep Dive: Trigonometric Vector Decomposition

When an object is launched into space at an angle $\theta$ relative to the horizontal, its initial velocity vector $\mathbf{u}$ acts simultaneously in two spatial dimensions ($x$ and $y$). To analyze this two-dimensional trajectory using standard one-dimensional kinematic equations, we must decompose the vector into two perpendicular components.

1. The Trigonometric Resolution Triangle

Consider an object launched with an initial speed $u$ at an angle $\theta$ above the horizontal. The vector arrow forms the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle, where:

  • The horizontal component $u_x$ lies adjacent to the launch angle $\theta$.
  • The vertical component $u_y$ lies opposite to the launch angle $\theta$.

Figure 1.1: Right-angled trigonometric decomposition of initial launch vector $\mathbf{u}$ into orthogonal components $u_x$ and $u_y$.

Applying standard right-angled trigonometry ($\text{SOH CAH TOA}$):

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{u_x}{u} \implies u_x = u \cos\theta$$ $$\sin\theta = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{u_y}{u} \implies u_y = u \sin\theta$$

2. Vector Reconstitution (Pythagoras & Tangent Rule)

If the horizontal ($v_x$) and vertical ($v_y$) velocity components at any instant in time are known, the overall resultant magnitude $v$ and direction angle $\theta$ can be reconstituted using the Pythagorean theorem and the inverse tangent function:

$$v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$$ $$\tan\theta = \frac{v_y}{v_x} \implies \theta = \tan^{-1}\left( \frac{v_y}{v_x} \right)$$

3. Galileo's Principle of Independence of Perpendicular Motions

In 1638, Italian scientist Galileo Galilei published Two New Sciences, establishing that perpendicular motion components act completely independently of one another. A horizontal force cannot alter vertical motion, and a vertical force (such as gravity) cannot alter horizontal motion.

  • Horizontal Axis ($x$-axis): Assuming zero air resistance, no horizontal forces act on a projectile ($F_x = 0$). According to Newton's First Law ($F_{net} = ma$), the horizontal acceleration is identically zero ($a_x = 0\text{ m/s}^2$). Consequently, horizontal velocity remains constant throughout the entire trajectory ($v_x = u_x$).
  • Vertical Axis ($y$-axis): The force of gravity acts continuously in the downward direction ($F_y = -mg$). This causes a uniform vertical acceleration downward ($a_y = -g = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$). The vertical velocity $v_y$ changes continuously over time ($v_y = u_y + a_y t$).

✦ Note for Mathematics Extension 1 (3U) Students

If you study 3U Mathematics, you derive projectile equations using vector calculus integrations ($\ddot{x}(t) = 0 \implies \dot{x}(t) = u\cos\theta$ and $\ddot{y}(t) = -g \implies \dot{y}(t) = -gt + u\sin\theta$).

NESA HSC Physics Assessment Boundary: Calculus is strictly not required or assessed in HSC Physics. You must express all work algebraically using NESA Stage 6 kinematic equations and vector resolution triangles. Ensure you state directional vectors and explicit positive/negative conventions (e.g., taking upwards as positive $y$).

Worked Examples (Step-by-Step)

Worked Example 1: Direct Component Resolution (Easy)

[Easy]

A football is kicked off the ground with an initial velocity of $22.0\text{ m/s}$ at an angle of $35.0^\circ$ above the horizontal. Calculate the horizontal ($u_x$) and vertical ($u_y$) components of the initial velocity.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify given variables

$$u = 22.0\text{ m/s}, \quad \theta = 35.0^\circ$$

Step 2: Apply horizontal resolution formula

$$u_x = u \cos\theta = 22.0 \times \cos(35.0^\circ) = 22.0 \times 0.81915 = 18.02\text{ m/s}$$

Step 3: Apply vertical resolution formula

$$u_y = u \sin\theta = 22.0 \times \sin(35.0^\circ) = 22.0 \times 0.57358 = 12.62\text{ m/s}$$

Final Answer:
Horizontal initial velocity component $u_x = 18.0\text{ m/s}$ (rightwards).
Vertical initial velocity component $u_y = 12.6\text{ m/s}$ (upwards) [both to 3 sig. figs.].

Worked Example 2: Reconstructing Resultant Velocity Vector (Medium)

[Medium]

At a specific point during its flight, a projectile has a horizontal velocity component of $v_x = 18.5\text{ m/s}$ (rightward) and a vertical velocity component of $v_y = -12.4\text{ m/s}$ (downward). Calculate the magnitude and direction of the resultant velocity vector $\mathbf{v}$.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate magnitude using Pythagorean theorem

$$v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2} = \sqrt{(18.5)^2 + (-12.4)^2} = \sqrt{342.25 + 153.76} = \sqrt{496.01} = 22.27\text{ m/s}$$

Step 2: Calculate direction angle using inverse tangent

$$\theta = \tan^{-1}\left( \frac{|v_y|}{v_x} \right) = \tan^{-1}\left( \frac{12.4}{18.5} \right) = \tan^{-1}(0.67027) = 33.83^\circ$$

Final Answer:
The resultant velocity is $22.3\text{ m/s}$ at an angle of $33.8^\circ$ below the horizontal (to 3 sig. figs.).

Worked Example 3: Inclined Launcher & Coordinate Transformation (Hard)

[Hard]

A cannon mounted on a hill inclined at $15.0^\circ$ to the horizontal fires a shell at an elevation angle of $30.0^\circ$ relative to the inclined hill surface with an initial speed of $85.0\text{ m/s}$. Determine the true horizontal ($u_x$) and vertical ($u_y$) components of the initial velocity relative to a standard flat Cartesian coordinate frame.

Solution:

Step 1: Find total launch angle relative to true horizontal ($\theta_{total}$)

$$\theta_{total} = \theta_{hill} + \theta_{rel} = 15.0^\circ + 30.0^\circ = 45.0^\circ$$

Step 2: Resolve into Cartesian components

$$u_x = u \cos(\theta_{total}) = 85.0 \times \cos(45.0^\circ) = 85.0 \times 0.70711 = 60.10\text{ m/s}$$

$$u_y = u \sin(\theta_{total}) = 85.0 \times \sin(45.0^\circ) = 85.0 \times 0.70711 = 60.10\text{ m/s}$$

Final Answer:
True horizontal component $u_x = 60.1\text{ m/s}$.
True vertical component $u_y = 60.1\text{ m/s}$ (both upwards/rightwards to 3 sig. figs.).

Worked Example 4: Trigonometric Identity Proof of Speed Conservation (Extension)

[Extension]

Prove analytically using trigonometric component decomposition that the total speed $u$ of a projectile launched at speed $u$ and angle $\theta$ is identically equal to $\sqrt{u_x^2 + u_y^2}$ for all launch angles $\theta \in [0^\circ, 90^\circ]$.

Analytical Proof:

Substitute $u_x = u \cos\theta$ and $u_y = u \sin\theta$ into the right-hand expression:

$$\sqrt{u_x^2 + u_y^2} = \sqrt{(u \cos\theta)^2 + (u \sin\theta)^2}$$ $$\sqrt{u_x^2 + u_y^2} = \sqrt{u^2 \cos^2\theta + u^2 \sin^2\theta}$$

Factor out the common scalar $u^2$:

$$\sqrt{u_x^2 + u_y^2} = \sqrt{u^2 (\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta)}$$

Apply the fundamental Pythagorean trigonometric identity $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$:

$$\sqrt{u_x^2 + u_y^2} = \sqrt{u^2 (1)} = \sqrt{u^2} = u$$

Q.E.D. The magnitude of the initial speed vector is perfectly conserved through component decomposition across all valid angles.

Question 1: Archery Vector Decomposition (Easy)

[2 Marks]

An archer fires an arrow with an initial velocity of $48.0\text{ m/s}$ at an angle of $24.0^\circ$ above the horizontal. Calculate the initial vertical component of velocity ($u_y$).

Hint: The vertical component lies opposite the launch angle in the right-angled vector triangle. Use $u_y = u \sin\theta$.

Worked Solution:

$$u_y = u \sin\theta = 48.0 \times \sin(24.0^\circ)$$

$$u_y = 48.0 \times 0.40674 = 19.52\text{ m/s}$$

$$u_y = 19.5\text{ m/s}\text{ (upwards, 3 sig. figs.)}$$

Marking Criteria:

Question 2: Tennis Ball Cliff Launch (Medium)

[3 Marks]

A tennis player strikes a ball off the edge of a cliff at a speed of $16.5\text{ m/s}$ at an angle of $12.0^\circ$ below the horizontal. Calculate the initial horizontal ($u_x$) and vertical ($u_y$) components of velocity, taking upwards as the positive $y$-direction.

Hint: Since the launch is below the horizontal, the vertical direction is negative relative to an upward Cartesian frame ($u_y = -u\sin\theta$).

Worked Solution:

Horizontal component ($u_x$):

$$u_x = u \cos\theta = 16.5 \times \cos(12.0^\circ) = 16.5 \times 0.97815 = 16.14\text{ m/s}$$

Vertical component ($u_y$):

$$u_y = -u \sin\theta = -16.5 \times \sin(12.0^\circ) = -16.5 \times 0.20791 = -3.43\text{ m/s}$$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates horizontal component $u_x = 16.1\text{ m/s}$.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly calculates magnitude of vertical component ($3.43\text{ m/s}$).
  • 3 Marks: Correctly applies negative sign to $u_y = -3.43\text{ m/s}$ to indicate downward initial vector orientation relative to Cartesian frame.

Question 3: Resultant Impact Vector Determination (Hard)

[4 Marks]

A rescue package lands on the ground with a horizontal velocity component of $v_x = 25.0\text{ m/s}$ and a vertical velocity component of $v_y = -38.5\text{ m/s}$. Determine the final resultant speed $v$ and direction angle $\theta$ relative to the ground surface at impact.

Hint: Combine $v_x$ and $v_y$ using $v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$. Then find $\theta = \tan^{-1}(|v_y| / v_x)$.

Worked Solution:

1. Resultant Speed ($v$):

$$v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2} = \sqrt{(25.0)^2 + (-38.5)^2} = \sqrt{625 + 1482.25} = \sqrt{2107.25} = 45.90\text{ m/s}$$

2. Impact Angle ($\theta$):

$$\theta = \tan^{-1}\left( \frac{|v_y|}{v_x} \right) = \tan^{-1}\left( \frac{38.5}{25.0} \right) = \tan^{-1}(1.54) = 57.0^\circ$$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly uses Pythagorean theorem formula setup.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates correct resultant speed of $45.9\text{ m/s}$.
  • 3 Marks: Applies inverse tangent formula correctly.
  • 4 Marks: States final direction clearly ($57.0^\circ$ below horizontal / to ground) with appropriate SI units and 3 sig. figs.

Question 4: Aircraft Cargo Relative Vector Analysis (Extension)

[5 Marks]

A cargo aircraft climbs at a constant speed of $110\text{ m/s}$ at an angle of $20.0^\circ$ above the horizontal. A crate is released from the aircraft payload bay. At the instant of release, calculate:

  1. The initial horizontal component of velocity of the crate ($u_x$).
  2. The initial vertical component of velocity of the crate ($u_y$).
  3. Explain why the crate initially moves upwards despite being dropped under the action of gravity.

Hint: Remember Newton's First Law of Motion! An object released from a moving vehicle inherits the exact velocity vector of that vehicle at the moment of release.

Worked Solution:

Part (a): Horizontal Component ($u_x$)

$$u_x = u \cos\theta = 110 \times \cos(20.0^\circ) = 110 \times 0.93969 = 103.37\text{ m/s} \approx 103\text{ m/s}$$

Part (b): Vertical Component ($u_y$)

$$u_y = u \sin\theta = 110 \times \sin(20.0^\circ) = 110 \times 0.34202 = 37.62\text{ m/s} \approx 37.6\text{ m/s}$$

Part (c): Physical Explanation

According to Newton's First Law of Motion (Inertia), an object in motion continues in its state of motion unless acted upon by an external net force. At the instant of release, the crate possesses the exact velocity vector of the climbing aircraft ($u_y = +37.6\text{ m/s}$). Gravity produces a downward acceleration ($a_y = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$), which gradually decelerates the upward velocity to zero over time, but cannot instantaneously eliminate the initial upward momentum.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Calculates $u_x = 103\text{ m/s}$ correctly.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates $u_y = 37.6\text{ m/s}$ correctly.
  • 3 Marks: Identifies that the crate inherits the aircraft's initial velocity vector upon release.
  • 4 Marks: Links initial upward velocity to Newton's First Law / Inertia.
  • 5 Marks: Fully explains that downward gravitational force provides acceleration that decelerates vertical speed over time rather than causing instantaneous downward motion.
