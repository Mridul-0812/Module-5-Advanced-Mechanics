Welcome to Advanced Mechanics

Year 12 HSC Physics - Module 5

The Physics of Motion in Two Dimensions

Welcome to Module 5! Advanced Mechanics expands standard 1D linear Newtonian dynamics into two-dimensional motion, rotational systems, and cosmic gravitational fields. Rather than relying on simple linear formulas, this module equips you with trigonometric vector resolution, radial force modeling, and universal potential energy concepts.

This course covers all three core NESA Inquiry Questions: Projectile Motion, Circular Motion & Torque, and Motion in Gravitational Fields. Mastery of these fundamentals is essential for your Year 12 HSC Physics examination.

Note for Mathematics Extension 1 Students

If you take HSC Mathematics Extension 1 (3U), you may already be familiar with projectile motion derived via vector calculus and second derivatives ($\ddot{x} = 0, \ddot{y} = -g$).

Crucial NESA Physics Rules to keep in mind:

  • No Calculus Required in HSC Physics: NESA HSC Physics strictly assesses algebraic and trigonometric solutions ($s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$, $u_x = u\cos\theta$, $u_y = u\sin\theta$). Calculus-based derivations are not required and will gain no additional marks over algebraic methods.
  • Explicit Vector Direction & Units: Physics questions heavily penalise missing directional vectors, improper significant figures (typically 3 s.f. based on $g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$), or missing SI units ($\text{m/s}$, $\text{m/s}^2$, $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$, $\text{J}$).
  • Working Scientifically: You will be assessed on experimental assumptions (e.g., zero air resistance, point-mass approximations) and numerical finite-difference modeling ($\Delta t$ spreadsheet calculations) alongside theoretical problem solving.

Practise Question: Projectile Component Analysis (Demo)

[3 Marks]

A projectile is launched from ground level with an initial speed of $20.0\text{ m/s}$ at an angle of $30.0^\circ$ above the horizontal. Assuming $g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$ and ignoring air resistance, calculate the vertical component of the initial velocity ($u_y$) and the maximum height reached ($s_{y,\text{max}}$).

Hint: Resolve the initial vertical velocity using $u_y = u \sin\theta$. At maximum height, the vertical velocity becomes zero ($v_y = 0\text{ m/s}$). Apply the kinematic equation $v_y^2 = u_y^2 + 2a_y s_y$ with $a_y = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Calculate initial vertical velocity component ($u_y$)

$$u_y = u \sin\theta = 20.0 \sin(30.0^\circ) = 10.0\text{ m/s}$$

Step 2: Calculate maximum height ($s_{y,\text{max}}$)

$$v_y^2 = u_y^2 + 2a_y s_y$$

$$0^2 = (10.0)^2 + 2(-9.80)s_{y,\text{max}}$$

$$19.6 s_{y,\text{max}} = 100$$

$$s_{y,\text{max}} = 5.10\text{ m}\text{ (to 3 sig. figs.)}$$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates the initial vertical velocity $u_y = 10.0\text{ m/s}$.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly substitutes $v_y = 0\text{ m/s}$ and $a_y = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$ into a valid kinematic equation.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains the correct maximum height of $5.10\text{ m}$ with correct SI units and significant figures.
