08. Banked Curves, Conical Pendulums & Vector Resolution

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 2: Why do objects move in circles?

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Rigorous Core Mechanics

Inquiry Question 2: Why do objects move in circles?

Under the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus, students are required to analyse two-dimensional vector components for objects executing uniform circular motion in horizontal circles. Specifically, students must:

  • Analyse the forces acting on an object undergoing uniform circular motion in horizontal circles, including:
    • Frictionless banked tracks: Tilted surfaces where a horizontal component of the Normal reaction force supplies the necessary centripetal force ($N \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r}$).
    • Conical pendulums: Suspended masses sweeping out horizontal circles where a horizontal component of string tension supplies the centripetal force ($T \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r}$).
    • Banked aircraft: Aircraft executing level circular turns where an aerodynamic lift vector component maintains vertical equilibrium while another supplies centripetal force ($L \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r}$).
  • Derive and apply the ideal design speed equation for banked curves and conical pendulums: $$\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r} \implies v_{\text{design}} = \sqrt{g r \tan\theta}$$
  • Band 6 Distinction Extension: Analyse complex 2D systems combining banking angle $\theta$ and surface static friction coefficient $\mu_s$ to solve for the maximum safe speed ($v_{\max}$) before outward skidding and the minimum safe speed ($v_{\min}$) before inward slipping.

Crucial NESA Exam Warning: Choice of Coordinate Axes

When solving standard inclined plane problems (like a block sliding down a ramp), students rotate their coordinate axes parallel and perpendicular to the slope. DO NOT DO THIS FOR HORIZONTAL CIRCULAR MOTION!

In horizontal circular motion (banked tracks, conical pendulums, banking jets), the circle lies strictly in the horizontal plane. Therefore, centripetal acceleration $\vec{a}_c$ points purely horizontally toward the centre of curvature ($a_x = \frac{v^2}{r}$) and vertical acceleration is zero ($a_y = 0$). Standard horizontal ($x$) and vertical ($y$) Cartesian axes must be used so that acceleration is isolated to a single axis.

Deep Dive Part A: Frictionless Banked Curves & Geometry of Vector Triangles

1. Physical Rationale & The Need for Banking

On a flat, horizontal road, static tire friction ($f_s$) is the sole force supplying the inward centripetal force ($F_c = \frac{m v^2}{r}$). However, static friction is strictly limited ($f_{s,\max} = \mu_s m g$) and degrades dangerously on wet, icy, or worn roads. Civil engineers tilt (or bank) high-speed roadways, velodromes, and racetracks at an incline angle $\theta$ toward the turn centre so that the Normal reaction force from the road surface provides the required centripetal acceleration naturally without relying on tire friction.

Figure 1: Free-body diagram and vector decomposition for a vehicle on a frictionless banked curve. The Normal force $N$ is resolved into vertical ($N \cos\theta$) and horizontal ($N \sin\theta$) components.

Step-by-Step Proof: Why $N_y = N \cos\theta$ and $N_x = N \sin\theta$

Students often wonder why $N_y$ uses $\cos\theta$ and $N_x$ uses $\sin\theta$, when standard incline problems use $g \sin\theta$ for horizontal motion. Here is the rigorous geometric derivation using complementary angles:

  1. Incline Geometry: The road surface is inclined at angle $\theta$ relative to the horizontal floor.
  2. Perpendicular Normal Force: By definition, the Normal reaction force vector $\vec{N}$ acts at right angles ($90^\circ$) to the road surface.
  3. Angle with Vertical: Since the incline is at angle $\theta$ to the horizontal, its angle with the vertical axis is $(90^\circ - \theta)$. Because the Normal force is at $90^\circ$ to the incline, the angle $\vec{N}$ makes with the vertical $y$-axis is equal to $\theta$!
  4. Applying Right-Triangle Trigonometry (SOH-CAH-TOA):
    • Vertical Component ($N_y$): This side is adjacent to angle $\theta$. Thus, $\cos\theta = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{N_y}{N} \implies \mathbf{N_y = N \cos\theta}$.
    • Horizontal Component ($N_x$): This side is opposite to angle $\theta$. Thus, $\sin\theta = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{N_x}{N} \implies \mathbf{N_x = N \sin\theta}$.

Derivation of Ideal Design Speed ($v_{\text{design}}$)

Consider a vehicle of mass $m$ rounding a track of radius $r$ banked at angle $\theta$. Two real physical forces act on the vehicle: Gravity ($m g$) vertically downward, and Normal Force ($N$) perpendicular to the track.

Step 1: Vertical Force Equilibrium ($\Sigma F_y = 0$)

The vehicle does not accelerate vertically ($a_y = 0$). Therefore, the upward vertical component of the Normal force must exactly balance gravity:

$$N \cos\theta - m g = 0 \implies \mathbf{N \cos\theta = m g} \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$ $$\implies N = \frac{m g}{\cos\theta}$$

Step 2: Horizontal Centripetal Acceleration ($\Sigma F_x = m a_c$)

The horizontal component of the Normal force points directly toward the centre of curvature, supplying 100% of the required centripetal force:

$$\mathbf{N \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r}} \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

Step 3: Dividing Equation 2 by Equation 1

To eliminate the unknown Normal force $N$ and mass $m$, divide Eq. 2 by Eq. 1:

$$\frac{N \sin\theta}{N \cos\theta} = \frac{\frac{m v^2}{r}}{m g}$$

Cancelling $N$ and $m$ on both sides, and noting that $\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \tan\theta$:

Banked Curve & Conical Pendulum Ideal Formula

$$\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r} \implies v_{\text{design}} = \sqrt{g r \tan\theta}$$

Core Physics Insights:

  • Mass Independence: Vehicle mass $m$ cancels out completely! A $500\text{ kg}$ formula car and a $20000\text{ kg}$ semi-trailer require the exact same ideal speed $v_{\text{design}}$ to round a banked corner without relying on tire friction!
  • No-Friction Ideal Speed: At $v = v_{\text{design}}$, there is zero lateral friction demand ($f_s = 0$). If $v > v_{\text{design}}$, the vehicle tends to slide up the slope; if $v < v_{\text{design}}$, gravity tends to pull it down the slope.

Deep Dive Part B: Conical Pendulums & Aircraft Banking Dynamics

1. The Conical Pendulum System

A conical pendulum consists of a mass bob $m$ suspended by a string of length $L$. Unlike a simple pendulum that oscillates back and forth in a vertical plane, the bob sweeps out a horizontal circle of radius $r$, while the string traces out the surface of a cone at angle $\theta$ relative to the vertical line.

Figure 2: Conical pendulum system showing string tension decomposition $T_y = T \cos\theta$ balancing gravity and $T_x = T \sin\theta$ supplying centripetal acceleration.

Derivation of Orbital Period ($T_{\text{period}}$) for Conical Pendulum

Resolving String Tension $T$ into Cartesian right-triangle components:

  • Vertical Equilibrium: $T \cos\theta = m g \implies T = \frac{m g}{\cos\theta}$
  • Horizontal Centripetal Acceleration: $T \sin\theta = F_c = m \omega^2 r$

Substituting $T = \frac{m g}{\cos\theta}$ into the horizontal centripetal equation:

$$\left(\frac{m g}{\cos\theta}\right) \sin\theta = m \omega^2 r$$ $$m g \tan\theta = m \omega^2 r \implies g \tan\theta = \omega^2 r$$

From string geometry, radius $r = L \sin\theta$. Substituting $r$ into the equation:

$$g \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \omega^2 (L \sin\theta)$$

Cancelling $\sin\theta$ on both sides yields the angular velocity $\omega$:

$$\frac{g}{\cos\theta} = \omega^2 L \implies \omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L \cos\theta}}$$

Recalling that period $T_{\text{period}} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}$:

Conical Pendulum Orbital Period Equation

$$T_{\text{period}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L \cos\theta}{g}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{h}{g}}$$

Where $h = L \cos\theta$ is the vertical height of the cone suspended below the ceiling.

2. Aerodynamic Aircraft Banking & Apparent G-Force (Load Factor)

When an airplane executes a horizontal circular turn, wings generate an aerodynamic Lift force ($\vec{L}$) directed perpendicular to the wing surface. To turn, the pilot banks the aircraft at an angle $\theta$:

Aircraft Banking Equations & Cockpit G-Force

  • Vertical Lift Component: $L \cos\theta = m g$ (supports plane weight).
  • Horizontal Lift Component: $L \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r}$ (provides turning centripetal force).
  • Load Factor ($n$ / Apparent G-Force): The pilot feels heavier because the total lift force $L$ increases with bank angle: $$n = \frac{L}{m g} = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}$$ In a steep $60^\circ$ bank, $\cos(60^\circ) = 0.5 \implies n = \frac{1}{0.5} = \mathbf{2.0\text{ g's}}$!

Deep Dive Part C: Band 6 Extension — Banked Track WITH Static Friction ($\mu_s$)

In real-world engineering, road tracks combine both a banking angle $\theta$ and surface static friction coefficient $\mu_s$. This provides a safe velocity window $[v_{\min}, v_{\max}]$ within which a vehicle can round the corner without skidding outward or slipping inward.

Vector Resolution for Maximum Safe Speed ($v_{\max}$)

Figure 3: Free-body vector resolution at maximum speed ($v_{\max}$). Because the car tends to skid outward/upward, static friction $f_s$ acts downward along the incline, contributing both to inward centripetal force ($f_s \cos\theta$) and downward vertical force ($f_s \sin\theta$).

1. Deriving Maximum Safe Speed ($v_{\max}$) — Preventing Outward Skidding

When travelling at maximum speed $v_{\max}$, the vehicle tends to skid up the track slope. Therefore, static friction $f_s = \mu_s N$ acts downward along the incline to resist outward motion.

Step-by-step vector resolution of static friction $f_s$:

  • The incline is at angle $\theta$ to horizontal. Thus, the friction vector along the slope is at angle $\theta$ below the horizontal axis.
  • Horizontal Inward Component of Friction: $f_{s,x} = f_s \cos\theta = \mu_s N \cos\theta$ (points radially inward, aiding turning).
  • Vertical Downward Component of Friction: $f_{s,y} = f_s \sin\theta = \mu_s N \sin\theta$ (points vertically downward, adding to weight).

Vertical Axis Equilibrium ($\Sigma F_y = 0$):

$$N \cos\theta - f_s \sin\theta - m g = 0$$ $$N \cos\theta - (\mu_s N) \sin\theta = m g$$ $$\mathbf{N(\cos\theta - \mu_s \sin\theta) = m g} \quad \text{--- (Equation A1)}$$

Horizontal Axis Centripetal Force ($\Sigma F_x = F_c = \frac{m v_{\max}^2}{r}$):

$$N \sin\theta + f_s \cos\theta = \frac{m v_{\max}^2}{r}$$ $$N \sin\theta + (\mu_s N) \cos\theta = \frac{m v_{\max}^2}{r}$$ $$\mathbf{N(\sin\theta + \mu_s \cos\theta) = \frac{m v_{\max}^2}{r}} \quad \text{--- (Equation A2)}$$

Dividing Equation A2 by Equation A1:

$$\frac{N(\sin\theta + \mu_s \cos\theta)}{N(\cos\theta - \mu_s \sin\theta)} = \frac{\frac{m v_{\max}^2}{r}}{m g}$$

Cancelling Normal force $N$ and mass $m$:

$$\frac{\sin\theta + \mu_s \cos\theta}{\cos\theta - \mu_s \sin\theta} = \frac{v_{\max}^2}{g r}$$

Dividing numerator and denominator on the left side by $\cos\theta$ gives the master $v_{\max}$ formula:

Maximum Speed on Banked Track with Friction

$$v_{\max} = \sqrt{g r \left( \frac{\tan\theta + \mu_s}{1 - \mu_s \tan\theta} \right)}$$

2. Deriving Minimum Safe Speed ($v_{\min}$) — Preventing Inward Slipping

When travelling too slowly ($v < v_{\text{design}}$), gravity tends to pull the vehicle down the slope. Static friction $f_s = \mu_s N$ acts upward along the incline to prevent inward slipping.

Reversing the direction of friction ($f_s \to -f_s$) in our component force equations yields:

$$\text{Vertical: } N(\cos\theta + \mu_s \sin\theta) = m g$$ $$\text{Horizontal: } N(\sin\theta - \mu_s \cos\theta) = \frac{m v_{\min}^2}{r}$$

Dividing equations and simplifying gives the master $v_{\min}$ formula:

Minimum Speed on Banked Track with Friction

$$v_{\min} = \sqrt{g r \left( \frac{\tan\theta - \mu_s}{1 + \mu_s \tan\theta} \right)}$$

Physical Interpretation of Limits & Special Cases

  • When $\tan\theta \le \mu_s$: The numerator for $v_{\min}$ becomes zero or negative. This means $v_{\min} = 0\text{ m/s}$ — static friction is strong enough that the car can stop completely on the slope without slipping down!
  • When $\mu_s \tan\theta \ge 1$: The denominator for $v_{\max}$ becomes zero or negative. This means $v_{\max} \to \infty$ — the banking is so steep and sticky that the vehicle can travel at any speed without skidding outward!

Four Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Velodrome Banked Corner Design Speed

[Core Banked Track Application]

An Olympic velodrome track corner has a radius of curvature $r = 28.0\text{ m}$ and is banked at an angle of $\theta = 32.0^\circ$.

a) Calculate the ideal design speed $v_{\text{design}}$ in $\text{m/s}$ and $\text{km/h}$ for a cyclist to negotiate the corner without needing tire friction.
b) Calculate the Normal force exerted by the track on a $75.0\text{ kg}$ cyclist and bicycle combined at this design speed.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Ideal Design Speed ($v_{\text{design}}$):

Setting horizontal Normal component equal to centripetal force ($N \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r}$) and vertical component equal to weight ($N \cos\theta = m g$):

$$\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r} \implies v_{\text{design}} = \sqrt{g r \tan\theta}$$ $$v_{\text{design}} = \sqrt{9.80 \times 28.0 \times \tan(32.0^\circ)} = \sqrt{274.4 \times 0.62487} = \sqrt{171.46} = \mathbf{13.1\text{ m/s}}$$

Converting to $\text{km/h}$:

$$v = 13.094 \times 3.6 = \mathbf{47.1\text{ km/h}}$$

Part b) Normal Reaction Force ($N$):

From vertical force equilibrium $N \cos\theta = m g$:

$$N = \frac{m g}{\cos\theta} = \frac{75.0 \times 9.80}{\cos(32.0^\circ)} = \frac{735}{0.84805} = \mathbf{867\text{ N}}$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Conical Pendulum Period & Geometry

[Conical System Kinematics]

A $0.400\text{ kg}$ brass bob is suspended from a string of length $L = 1.20\text{ m}$ and rotates in a horizontal circle as a conical pendulum. The string maintains a constant angle $\theta = 25.0^\circ$ to the vertical axis.

a) Calculate the radius $r$ of the horizontal circle.
b) Calculate the string tension force $T$.
c) Calculate the rotation period $T_{\text{period}}$ and orbital speed $v$ of the bob.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Horizontal Radius ($r$):

Using string geometry right triangle where string length $L$ is hypotenuse and radius $r$ is opposite to angle $\theta$:

$$r = L \sin\theta = 1.20 \times \sin(25.0^\circ) = 1.20 \times 0.42262 = \mathbf{0.507\text{ m}}$$

Part b) String Tension ($T$):

From vertical force equilibrium $T \cos\theta = m g$:

$$T = \frac{m g}{\cos\theta} = \frac{0.400 \times 9.80}{\cos(25.0^\circ)} = \frac{3.92}{0.90631} = \mathbf{4.32\text{ N}}$$

Part c) Period ($T_{\text{period}}$) and Tangential Speed ($v$):

$$T_{\text{period}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L \cos\theta}{g}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{1.20 \times \cos(25.0^\circ)}{9.80}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{1.08757}{9.80}} = \mathbf{2.09\text{ s}}$$ $$v = \sqrt{g r \tan\theta} = \sqrt{9.80 \times 0.50714 \times \tan(25.0^\circ)} = \sqrt{4.970 \times 0.46631} = \mathbf{1.52\text{ m/s}}$$

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Jet Fighter Banked Turn & Pilot Load Factor

[Aerodynamic Banking & Physiology]

A high-performance jet fighter flying at a constant speed of $v = 220\text{ m/s}$ ($792\text{ km/h}$) executes a level horizontal turn banked at an angle of $\theta = 65.0^\circ$.

a) Calculate the turning radius $r$ of the aircraft trajectory.
b) Calculate the aerodynamic Lift force $L$ generated by the wings on an $8500\text{ kg}$ aircraft.
c) Calculate the load factor ($n = L / m g$) experienced by the pilot inside the cockpit.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Turning Radius ($r$):

$$\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r} \implies r = \frac{v^2}{g \tan\theta}$$ $$r = \frac{(220)^2}{9.80 \times \tan(65.0^\circ)} = \frac{48400}{9.80 \times 2.1445} = \frac{48400}{21.016} = \mathbf{2303\text{ m}} \approx \mathbf{2.30\text{ km}}$$

Part b) Aerodynamic Lift Force ($L$):

$$L \cos\theta = m g \implies L = \frac{m g}{\cos(65.0^\circ)} = \frac{8500 \times 9.80}{0.42262} = \frac{83300}{0.42262} = \mathbf{1.97 \times 10^5\text{ N}} \quad (197\text{ kN})$$

Part c) Pilot Load Factor ($n$):

$$n = \frac{L}{m g} = \frac{1}{\cos(65.0^\circ)} = \frac{1}{0.42262} = \mathbf{2.37\text{ g's}}$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension - Band 6 Separator): Banked Track WITH Static Friction ($\mu_s$)

[Complex Vector Friction Derivation]

A highway curve of radius $r = 50.0\text{ m}$ is banked at an angle $\theta = 20.0^\circ$. The static coefficient of friction between tire rubber and asphalt is $\mu_s = 0.300$.

a) Derive the general formula for the maximum speed ($v_{\max}$) before the car skids UP the track.
b) Derive the general formula for the minimum speed ($v_{\min}$) before the car slips DOWN the track.
c) Calculate $v_{\max}$ and $v_{\min}$ for this specific roadway in $\text{km/h}$.

Full Band 6 Algebraic Derivation:

Part a) Maximum Speed ($v_{\max}$) Derivation:

At $v_{\max}$, outward centrifugal tendency causes static friction $f_s = \mu_s N$ to point downward along the incline.

$$\text{Vertical: } N \cos\theta - f_s \sin\theta = m g \implies N(\cos\theta - \mu_s \sin\theta) = m g \quad \text{--- (Eq. 1)}$$ $$\text{Radial: } N \sin\theta + f_s \cos\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r} \implies N(\sin\theta + \mu_s \cos\theta) = \frac{m v^2}{r} \quad \text{--- (Eq. 2)}$$

Dividing Eq. 2 by Eq. 1 and simplifying yields:

$$v_{\max} = \sqrt{g r \left( \frac{\tan\theta + \mu_s}{1 - \mu_s \tan\theta} \right)}$$

Part b) Minimum Speed ($v_{\min}$) Derivation:

At $v_{\min}$, inward slipping tendency causes static friction $f_s = \mu_s N$ to point upward along the incline. Reversing the friction sign yields:

$$v_{\min} = \sqrt{g r \left( \frac{\tan\theta - \mu_s}{1 + \mu_s \tan\theta} \right)}$$

Part c) Numerical Calculation:

For $\theta = 20.0^\circ$ ($\tan(20^\circ) = 0.36397$), $r = 50.0\text{ m}$, $\mu_s = 0.300$:

$$v_{\max} = \sqrt{9.80 \times 50.0 \times \left( \frac{0.36397 + 0.300}{1 - (0.300 \times 0.36397)} \right)} = \sqrt{490 \times \left( \frac{0.66397}{0.89081} \right)} = \mathbf{19.11\text{ m/s}} \approx \mathbf{68.8\text{ km/h}}$$ $$v_{\min} = \sqrt{490 \times \left( \frac{0.36397 - 0.300}{1 + (0.300 \times 0.36397)} \right)} = \sqrt{490 \times \left( \frac{0.06397}{1.10919} \right)} = \mathbf{5.32\text{ m/s}} \approx \mathbf{19.1\text{ km/h}}$$

Practise Question 1 (Easy): Conical Swing Ride String Angle

[3 Marks]

A fairground swing ride acts as a conical pendulum. A rider suspended from a $6.00\text{ m}$ chain rotates in a horizontal circle at a linear speed of $v = 5.20\text{ m/s}$.

Calculate the angle $\theta$ the chain makes with the vertical axis ($g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$).

Hint: Use $\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r}$ with radius $r = L \sin\theta$. Thus $\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{v^2}{g L \sin\theta} \implies \frac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{v^2}{g L}$. Set $x = \cos\theta$ and solve the quadratic $1 - x^2 = k x$.

From conical pendulum relations: $T \cos\theta = m g$ and $T \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r}$.

Dividing equations gives $\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r}$. Substituting horizontal radius $r = L \sin\theta$:

$$\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{v^2}{g L \sin\theta} \implies \frac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{v^2}{g L}$$ $$\frac{1 - \cos^2\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{(5.20)^2}{9.80 \times 6.00} = \frac{27.04}{58.80} = 0.45986$$

Forming a quadratic equation in terms of $x = \cos\theta$:

$$x^2 + 0.45986 x - 1 = 0 \implies x = \frac{-0.45986 + \sqrt{(0.45986)^2 + 4}}{2} = 0.7967$$ $$\cos\theta = 0.7967 \implies \theta = \cos^{-1}(0.7967) = \mathbf{37.2^\circ}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Resolving conical forces gives $\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r}$. Substituting $r = L \sin\theta$ yields $\frac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{v^2}{g L} = \frac{5.20^2}{9.80 \times 6.00} = 0.460$. Setting $x = \cos\theta$ yields quadratic $x^2 + 0.460x - 1 = 0$, giving $x = 0.797$. Therefore $\theta = \cos^{-1}(0.797) = 37.2^\circ$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly identifies $r = L \sin\theta$ and sets up trigonometric relation.
  • 2 Marks: Formulates quadratic equation in terms of $\cos\theta$.
  • 3 Marks: Solves correctly for angle $\theta = 37.2^\circ$.

[4 Marks]

Engineers design a circular racetrack turn of radius $r = 120\text{ m}$ for race cars travelling at $110\text{ km/h}$ ($30.56\text{ m/s}$). Calculate the ideal banking angle $\theta$ required so that cars experience zero lateral friction demand.

Step 2: Apply banked curve formula:

$$\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r} = \frac{(30.556)^2}{9.80 \times 120} = \frac{933.64}{1176} = 0.79391$$ $$\theta = \tan^{-1}(0.79391) = \mathbf{38.4^\circ}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Converting speed gives $v = 110 / 3.6 = 30.56\text{ m/s}$. For zero friction demand, the horizontal normal force component supplies centripetal force: $\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r} = \frac{30.56^2}{9.80 \times 120} = 0.794$. Taking the inverse tangent yields $\theta = 38.4^\circ$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Converts speed from $\text{km/h}$ to $\text{m/s}$ ($30.56\text{ m/s}$).
  • 2 Marks: States the correct banked curve formula $\tan\theta = v^2 / g r$.

Practise Question 3 (Hard - Distinction Separator): Banked Track Friction Breakdown

[5 Marks]

A car negotiates a banked curve ($r = 60.0\text{ m}$, $\theta = 15.0^\circ$). The static coefficient of friction between tires and road is $\mu_s = 0.250$. Determine the maximum speed $v_{\max}$ in $\text{km/h}$ at which the car can round the corner without skidding up the embankment.

Hint: When skidding up, static friction acts down the track: $v_{\max} = \sqrt{g r \left( \frac{\tan\theta + \mu_s}{1 - \mu_s \tan\theta} \right)}$. Calculate $v$ in $\text{m/s}$ then convert to $\text{km/h}$.

Step 1: Calculate trigonometric values:

$$\tan(15.0^\circ) = 0.26795$$

Step 2: Apply banked track friction formula:

$$v_{\max} = \sqrt{g r \left( \frac{\tan\theta + \mu_s}{1 - \mu_s \tan\theta} \right)}$$ $$v_{\max} = \sqrt{9.80 \times 60.0 \times \left( \frac{0.26795 + 0.250}{1 - (0.250 \times 0.26795)} \right)}$$ $$v_{\max} = \sqrt{588 \times \left( \frac{0.51795}{0.93301} \right)} = \sqrt{588 \times 0.55514} = \mathbf{18.07\text{ m/s}}$$

Step 3: Convert to $\text{km/h}$:

$$v_{\max} = 18.067 \times 3.6 = \mathbf{65.0\text{ km/h}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"At maximum speed, friction acts down the incline to prevent outward skidding. Resolving radial and vertical forces yields $v_{\max} = \sqrt{g r \left(\frac{\tan\theta + \mu_s}{1 - \mu_s \tan\theta}\right)}$. Substituting $r = 60\text{ m}$, $\theta = 15^\circ$, $\mu_s = 0.25$ gives $v_{\max} = \sqrt{588 \times \frac{0.518}{0.933}} = 18.07\text{ m/s} = 65.0\text{ km/h}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies that friction acts down the slope for maximum speed.
  • 2 Marks: States or derives the correct banked friction equation.
  • 3 Marks: Correctly calculates intermediate values ($\tan 15^\circ = 0.268$).
  • 4 Marks: Obtains velocity in $\text{m/s}$ ($18.07\text{ m/s}$).
  • 5 Marks: Converts accurately to $\text{km/h}$ ($65.0\text{ km/h}$).

Practise Question 4 (Extension - Band 6 Separator): Jet Fighter Banking & Structural G-Limit

[6 Marks]

A $12000\text{ kg}$ fighter jet flying at $v = 300\text{ m/s}$ ($1080\text{ km/h}$) executes a level horizontal turn. The airframe has a strict structural load factor limit of $n_{\max} = 6.00\text{ g's}$ ($L \le 6.00 m g$).

a) Calculate the maximum allowable banking angle $\theta_{\max}$ before exceeding structural safety limits.
b) Calculate the minimum turning radius $r_{\min}$ achieved at this maximum bank angle.
c) Calculate the magnitude of the total aerodynamic Lift force $L$ on the wings during this maneuver.

Hint: Load factor $n = \frac{1}{\cos\theta} = 6.00 \implies \cos\theta = \frac{1}{6.00}$. Then use $\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r}$ to find $r_{\min}$.

a) Maximum Banking Angle ($\theta_{\max}$):

$$n = \frac{L}{m g} = \frac{1}{\cos\theta} = 6.00$$ $$\cos\theta_{\max} = \frac{1}{6.00} = 0.16667 \implies \theta_{\max} = \cos^{-1}(0.16667) = \mathbf{80.4^\circ}$$

b) Minimum Turning Radius ($r_{\min}$):

For $\theta = 80.405^\circ$, $\tan(80.405^\circ) = 5.9161$:

$$r_{\min} = \frac{v^2}{g \tan\theta_{\max}} = \frac{(300)^2}{9.80 \times 5.9161} = \frac{90000}{57.978} = \mathbf{1552\text{ m}} \approx \mathbf{1.55\text{ km}}$$

c) Aerodynamic Lift Force ($L$):

$$L = n \times m g = 6.00 \times 12000 \times 9.80 = \mathbf{7.06 \times 10^5\text{ N}} \quad (706\text{ kN})$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"The load factor is $n = \frac{1}{\cos\theta} = 6.00$, yielding maximum bank angle $\theta_{\max} = \cos^{-1}(1/6) = 80.4^\circ$. Turning radius is $r_{\min} = \frac{v^2}{g \tan\theta} = \frac{300^2}{9.80 \times \tan(80.4^\circ)} = 1.55\text{ km}$. Total lift force is $L = 6.00 m g = 6.00 \times 12000 \times 9.80 = 706\text{ kN}$."
