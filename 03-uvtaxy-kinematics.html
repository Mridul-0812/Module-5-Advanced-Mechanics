03. UVTAXY Kinematics Grid & Trajectory Functions

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 1

NESA Syllabus Alignment & Inquiry Question

Inquiry Question 1: How can models that are used to explain projectile motion be used to analyse and make predictions?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus, students must master the following specific outcomes:

  • Analyse the motion of projectiles by resolving initial velocity vectors into orthogonal horizontal ($u_x$) and vertical ($u_y$) components.
  • Derive, express, and apply time-dependent parametric functions for horizontal velocity ($v_x$), vertical velocity ($v_y$), horizontal displacement ($s_x$), and vertical displacement ($s_y$).
  • Eliminate the scalar time variable $t$ to construct the explicit spatial trajectory equation $y(x)$ representing the inverted parabolic path.
  • Construct and interpret graphical representations of motion ($a_x-t$, $a_y-t$, $v_x-t$, $v_y-t$, $s_x-t$, $s_y-t$, and $s_y-s_x$).
  • Apply uniform acceleration kinematic equations independently using systematic problem-solving frameworks (UVTAXY) to make quantitative predictions for flight time, maximum height, horizontal range, and final impact velocity vectors.

What is Projectile Motion? Fundamental Physics Principles

1. Physical Definition & Universal Characteristics

A projectile is defined as any object launched into two-dimensional space that, once projected, continues in motion by its own inertia and is influenced solely by the force of gravity. Crucially, a projectile experiences no self-propulsion (such as a rocket motor) during its flight path, nor does it experience external contact forces after leaving its launcher.

Every ideal projectile trajectory exhibits three key physical characteristics:

  • Uniform Constant Horizontal Velocity: Because no horizontal forces act on the object ($\Sigma F_x = 0$), Newton's First Law of Motion dictates that the horizontal velocity vector component remains absolutely constant throughout the entire flight ($v_x = u_x$).
  • Uniform Vertical Acceleration: Earth's gravitational force exerts a constant downward pull ($\Sigma F_y = m a_y = -mg$). This causes a uniform vertical acceleration downward at rate $a_y = -g = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$ near Earth's surface.
  • Symmetric Parabolic Trajectory: For ground-to-ground launches, the combination of uniform horizontal displacement and uniformly accelerated vertical displacement traces an symmetrical inverted parabolic curve in space.

2. Standard Physics Modelling Assumptions

To mathematically model projectile dynamics at the Year 12 HSC Physics level, NESA mandates four foundational idealisations:

  1. Zero Aerodynamic Resistance: Air resistance, drag forces, rotational spin effects (Magnus effect), and atmospheric lift forces are assumed completely absent.
  2. Uniform Gravitational Acceleration: The acceleration due to gravity is treated as constant in both magnitude and direction ($g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$ downward) over the entire flight domain.
  3. Point-Mass Particle Model: The projectile is treated as an infinitely small point mass without physical dimensions, geometry, or internal rotational motion.
  4. Flat Earth Geometry: The Earth's surface is assumed flat, and atmospheric curvature alongside Coriolis effects are ignored over typical terrestrial ranges.

Deriving the Time-Dependent Parametric Kinematic Equations

Rather than relying on memorised rules, a beginner must understand how the four fundamental $SUVAT$ equations from the NESA HSC Data Sheet derive time-dependent functions $v_x(t)$, $v_y(t)$, $s_x(t)$, and $s_y(t)$ for any launch speed $u$ and launch angle $\theta$.

1. The Horizontal Motion Equations ($x$-axis)

Because there is zero horizontal acceleration ($a_x = 0\text{ m/s}^2$):

A. Horizontal Velocity Function $v_x(t)$:

Applying $v = u + at$ to the horizontal axis:

$$v_x(t) = u_x + a_x t = u_x + (0)t \implies \mathbf{v_x(t) = u \cos\theta}$$

Physical Meaning: Horizontal velocity is constant and completely independent of time $t$.

B. Horizontal Displacement Function $s_x(t)$:

Applying $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$ to the horizontal axis:

$$s_x(t) = u_x t + \frac{1}{2}a_x t^2 = u_x t + \frac{1}{2}(0)t^2 \implies \mathbf{s_x(t) = (u \cos\theta)t}$$

Physical Meaning: Horizontal position increases linearly over time.

2. The Vertical Motion Equations ($y$-axis)

Because gravity causes constant downward acceleration ($a_y = -g = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$):

A. Vertical Velocity Function $v_y(t)$:

Applying $v = u + at$ to the vertical axis:

$$v_y(t) = u_y + a_y t \implies \mathbf{v_y(t) = u \sin\theta - gt}$$

Physical Meaning: Vertical velocity decreases linearly at a rate of $9.80\text{ m/s}$ every second, reaching $0\text{ m/s}$ at apex peak before turning negative during descent.

B. Vertical Displacement Function $s_y(t)$:

Applying $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$ to the vertical axis:

$$s_y(t) = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2 \implies \mathbf{s_y(t) = (u \sin\theta)t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2}$$

Physical Meaning: Vertical displacement is a quadratic function of time, producing an inverted parabolic height profile.

C. Vertical Velocity-Displacement Relation $v_y(s_y)$:

Applying $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ to the vertical axis:

$$\mathbf{v_y^2 = (u \sin\theta)^2 - 2g s_y}$$

Physical Meaning: Allows direct calculation of vertical velocity at any height $s_y$ without needing time $t$.

3. Deriving the Parabolic Trajectory Equation $y(x)$

To find the spatial path of the projectile (the equation relating vertical height $s_y$ directly to horizontal displacement $s_x$ without time), we eliminate $t$:

Step 1: Rearrange the horizontal displacement equation for $t$:

$$s_x = (u \cos\theta)t \implies t = \frac{s_x}{u \cos\theta}$$

Step 2: Substitute this expression for $t$ into the vertical displacement function:

$$s_y = (u \sin\theta)\left(\frac{s_x}{u \cos\theta}\right) - \frac{1}{2}g\left(\frac{s_x}{u \cos\theta}\right)^2$$

Step 3: Simplify using trigonometric identity $\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \tan\theta$:

$$\mathbf{s_y(s_x) = s_x \tan\theta - \frac{g}{2 u^2 \cos^2\theta} s_x^2}$$

Notice the mathematical form of this equation! Letting $A = \tan\theta$ and $B = \frac{g}{2 u^2 \cos^2\theta}$, the relation becomes:

$$s_y = A s_x - B s_x^2$$

This is the standard equation of a concave-downward inverted parabola passing through the origin $(0,0)$, mathematically proving that ideal projectiles follow parabolic paths!

The UVTAXY Grid Framework: Systematic Exam Problem Solving

The UVTAXY Table is a structured layout that isolates horizontal and vertical variables. By keeping $x$ and $y$ parameters strictly separated, you eliminate confusion and prevent catastrophic formula mixing in exam questions.

Variable Symbol Physical Meaning Horizontal Axis ($x$) Vertical Axis ($y$) Key Examination Rules
U Initial Launch Velocity $u_x = u \cos\theta$ $u_y = u \sin\theta$ Resolve using trigonometry first
V Final / Instantaneous Velocity $v_x = u_x$ $v_y = u_y + a_y t$ $v_x$ never changes; $v_y = 0$ at apex
T Time Interval $t$ $t$ Scalar Link: $t$ connects both axes
A Acceleration Rate $a_x = 0\text{ m/s}^2$ $a_y = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$ Always populated automatically
X / Y Displacement $s_x = u_x t$ $s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2$ $s_x$ = Range; $s_y$ = Height relative to origin

Step-by-Step Problem-Solving Procedure

  1. Draw a Spatial Diagram: Sketch the launch vector $u$, angle $\theta$, origin $(0,0)$, and trajectory path.
  2. Construct & Fill the UVTAXY Grid: Write down the table and populate known constants ($a_x = 0$, $a_y = -9.80\text{ m/s}^2$). Insert question marks (?) for desired unknowns.
  3. Resolve Launch Velocity $u$: Compute $u_x = u\cos\theta$ and $u_y = u\sin\theta$.
  4. Solve for Time ($t$) via the Vertical Axis: Use $s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2$ or $v_y = u_y + a_y t$ to calculate flight time $t$.
  5. Bridge Across to the Horizontal Axis: Substitute scalar time $t$ into $s_x = u_x t$ to find horizontal range.
  6. Recombine Vector Components if Required: If asked for final impact velocity, calculate $v_{\text{impact}} = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$ and direction $\phi = \tan^{-1}\left|\frac{v_y}{v_x}\right|$.

Extension 1 Mathematics vs HSC Physics Derivation Guidelines

If you take Mathematics Extension 1 (3U), you are accustomed to integrating second derivatives ($\ddot{x}=0, \ddot{y}=-g$). In HSC Physics, calculus integration is completely unnecessary. Use the standard $SUVAT$ equations directly.

HSC Exam Strategy: Short-Answer Questions (SAQs) vs Multiple Choice Questions (MCQs)

MCQs: You are encouraged to use shortcut formulas like $R = \frac{u^2\sin(2\theta)}{g}$ or $s_{y,\text{max}} = \frac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}$ to save time.

SAQs: DO NOT use unsupplied shortcut formulas directly without derivation! They are not on the NESA Physics Formula Sheet. You must start from basic $SUVAT$ equations ($s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2$) and derive your solution line-by-line to secure full marks from NESA markers.

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Standard Ground-to-Ground Launch

[Syllabus Core Application]

A football is kicked from ground level with an initial launch speed of $35.0\text{ m/s}$ at an angle of $40.0^\circ$ above the horizontal. Assuming $g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$ and zero air resistance, construct a complete UVTAXY table and calculate:

a) The total time of flight ($t$).
b) The maximum apex height achieved ($s_{y,\text{max}}$).
c) The horizontal range ($s_x$).

Step 1: Populate the UVTAXY Table

Resolve velocity components:

$$u_x = 35.0 \cos(40.0^\circ) = 26.812\text{ m/s}$$

$$u_y = 35.0 \sin(40.0^\circ) = 22.498\text{ m/s}$$

V$26.812\text{ m/s}$$-22.498\text{ m/s}$ (at landing) / $0\text{ m/s}$ (at apex)

A$0\text{ m/s}^2$$-9.80\text{ m/s}^2$

Part a) Total Time of Flight:

$$s_y = u_y t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2 \implies 0 = 22.498 t + \frac{1}{2}(-9.80)t^2$$

$$4.90 t^2 = 22.498 t \implies t = \frac{22.498}{4.90} = \mathbf{4.59\text{ s}}\text{ (to 3 sig. figs.)}$$

Part b) Maximum Height:

At apex, $v_y = 0\text{ m/s}$:

$$v_y^2 = u_y^2 + 2a_y s_y \implies 0^2 = (22.498)^2 + 2(-9.80)s_{y,\text{max}}$$

$$19.6 s_{y,\text{max}} = 506.16 \implies s_{y,\text{max}} = \mathbf{25.8\text{ m}}\text{ (to 3 sig. figs.)}$$

Part c) Horizontal Range:

$$s_x = u_x t = 26.812 \times 4.5914 = \mathbf{123\text{ m}}\text{ (to 3 sig. figs.)}$$

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Inverse Reconstructions from Experimental Data

[Algebraic Inverse Synthesis]

An unknown ground-level launch yields a maximum apex height $s_{y,\text{max}} = 20.0\text{ m}$ and a total horizontal range $R = 110\text{ m}$. Construct a UVTAXY framework and derive the launch speed $u$ and angle $\theta$.

Step 1: Set up the UVTAXY Grid with Symbolic Parameters
Variable Horizontal Axis ($x$) Vertical Axis ($y$)
U$u_x = u\cos\theta$$u_y = u\sin\theta$
V$u_x$$0\text{ m/s}$ (at apex)

Step 2: Relate Apex Height to $u_y$:

$$v_y^2 = u_y^2 + 2a_y s_y \implies 0 = u_y^2 - 2(9.80)(20.0) \implies u_y^2 = 392 \implies u_y = 19.799\text{ m/s}$$

$$s_x = u_x t_{\text{flight}} \implies 110 = u_x (4.0406) \implies u_x = 27.224\text{ m/s}$$

$$\tan\theta = \frac{u_y}{u_x} = \frac{19.799}{27.224} = 0.72726 \implies \mathbf{\theta = 36.0^\circ}$$

$$u = \sqrt{u_x^2 + u_y^2} = \sqrt{(27.224)^2 + (19.799)^2} = \mathbf{33.7\text{ m/s}}$$

$$8.00 = 25.173 - \frac{7514.9}{u^2} \implies \frac{7514.9}{u^2} = 17.173 \implies u^2 = 437.60 \implies \mathbf{u = 20.9\text{ m/s}}$$

Because ground-to-ground launch under zero air drag is perfectly symmetric, final landing impact speed $v_{\text{impact}} = u = \mathbf{20.9\text{ m/s}}$.
