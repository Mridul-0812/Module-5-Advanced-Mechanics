07. Centripetal Acceleration ($a_c$) & Centripetal Force ($F_c$)

Module 5: Advanced Mechanics — Inquiry Question 2

NESA Syllabus Alignment & HSC Examination Scope

Inquiry Question 2: Why do objects move in circles?

According to the NESA Stage 6 Physics Syllabus, students must master the following specific outcomes regarding centripetal acceleration and centripetal force dynamics:

  • Solve quantitative problems involving objects undergoing uniform circular motion to evaluate centripetal acceleration ($a_c$) and centripetal force ($F_c$).
  • Apply Newton's Second Law ($\Sigma \vec{F} = m \vec{a}$) to uniform circular motion to establish and apply the primary centripetal relations: $$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2}$$ $$F_c = m a_c = \frac{m v^2}{r} = m \omega^2 r = \frac{4\pi^2 m r}{T^2}$$
  • Identify real physical forces (such as tension, normal force, friction, and gravitational attraction) that combine to provide the required net centripetal force across diverse contexts.
  • Evaluate inertial versus non-inertial reference frames to debunk the common "centrifugal force" fallacy using Newton's First Law of Motion (inertia).

HSC Exam Scope & Extension Derivation Note

Standard HSC Scope: On official HSC Physics examinations, NESA provides the formulas $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$ and $F_c = \frac{m v^2}{r}$ directly on the NESA Reference Sheet. Standard HSC examination questions test your ability to apply these equations, draw free-body diagrams, decompose vector components (e.g. banked tracks, conical pendulums), and perform multi-step quantitative calculations.

High-Achiever / Band 6 Scope: While standard HSC exam papers do not ask students to reproduce formal vector proofs of $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$, mastering the geometric similar-triangles proof below is invaluable for Band 6 candidates. It establishes deep vector calculus reasoning, proving why acceleration points strictly inward toward the centre of curvature.

Deep Dive Part A: Vector Kinematics & Geometric Proof

1. The Physical Nature of Centripetal Acceleration

In everyday language, acceleration implies "speeding up" or "slowing down". However, in vector physics, acceleration is defined as the time rate of change of the velocity vector:

$$\vec{a} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta \vec{v}}{\Delta t}$$

Because velocity $\vec{v}$ is a vector containing both magnitude (speed $v$) and direction, motion can accelerate in two fundamental ways:

Tangential vs Centripetal Acceleration Components

  • Tangential Acceleration ($a_t$): Directed parallel/antiparallel to velocity ($\vec{a}_t \parallel \vec{v}$). It changes the **scalar magnitude** (speed $v$). In *uniform* circular motion, scalar speed is constant, so $a_t = 0$.
  • Centripetal Acceleration ($a_c$): Directed strictly perpendicular to velocity ($\vec{a}_c \perp \vec{v}$). It changes the **direction** of velocity without altering speed. In uniform circular motion, $a_c$ acts continuously toward the circle's centre.

Figure 1: Position triangle $\Delta O P_1 P_2$ and velocity vector triangle. Because tangential velocity is always perpendicular to radius, these isosceles triangles are geometrically similar!

2. Similar-Triangles Derivation of $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$ (Extension Enrichment)

To mathematically derive centripetal acceleration, consider an object moving along a circle of radius $r$ at constant speed $v$. Over a short time interval $\Delta t$, the particle moves from position $\vec{r}_1$ to $\vec{r}_2$, sweeping out angle $\Delta \theta$. Its velocity changes from $\vec{v}_1$ to $\vec{v}_2$.

Step-by-Step Geometric Proof

Step 1: Establishing Triangle Similarity:

The position triangle has equal side lengths $|\vec{r}_1| = |\vec{r}_2| = r$ and base chord length $|\Delta \vec{r}|$.

The velocity triangle has equal side lengths $|\vec{v}_1| = |\vec{v}_2| = v$ and base length $|\Delta \vec{v}|$.

Because $\vec{v}_1 \perp \vec{r}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2 \perp \vec{r}_2$, the subtended vertex angle of both isosceles triangles is identically $\Delta \theta$. By Side-Angle-Side similarity:

$$\Delta \text{Position Triangle} \sim \Delta \text{Velocity Triangle}$$

Step 2: Equating Ratios of Corresponding Sides:

$$\frac{|\Delta \vec{v}|}{v} = \frac{|\Delta \vec{r}|}{r}$$

Step 3: Introducing Arc Length & Calculus Limit ($\Delta t \to 0$):

For an infinitesimal time step $\Delta t$, the straight displacement chord $|\Delta \vec{r}|$ approaches arc length $\Delta s = v \Delta t$. Substituting $|\Delta \vec{r}| \approx v \Delta t$:

$$\frac{|\Delta \vec{v}|}{v} = \frac{v \Delta t}{r}$$

Rearranging to isolate $\frac{|\Delta \vec{v}|}{\Delta t}$:

$$\frac{|\Delta \vec{v}|}{\Delta t} = \frac{v^2}{r}$$

Taking the limit as $\Delta t \to 0$ yields the instantaneous magnitude of centripetal acceleration:

$$a_c = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{|\Delta \vec{v}|}{\Delta t} = \mathbf{\frac{v^2}{r}}$$

Step 4: Proving Direction:

In the velocity triangle, as $\Delta \theta \to 0$, the interior base angle approaches $90^\circ$. A vector at $90^\circ$ to the tangential velocity points radially inward toward the centre of curvature!

3. Rotational Family of Centripetal Acceleration Formulas

Combining $v = \omega r$ and $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}$ provides the complete set of centripetal acceleration equations:

Complete Centripetal Acceleration Equations

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2} = 4\pi^2 f^2 r$$

Deep Dive Part B: Centripetal Force ($F_c$) & Real Physical Force Providers

1. Applying Newton's Second Law

Newton's Second Law ($\Sigma \vec{F} = m \vec{a}$) dictates that any accelerating body requires a non-zero net external force. Multiplying centripetal acceleration $a_c$ by object mass $m$ gives the net radial centripetal force $F_c$:

Complete Centripetal Force Equations

$$F_c = m a_c = \frac{m v^2}{r} = m \omega^2 r = \frac{4\pi^2 m r}{T^2}$$

CRITICAL HSC RULE: Centripetal Force is NOT an Independent Force!

In NESA examinations, students lose marks when drawing Free-Body Diagrams (FBDs) by adding an extra arrow labelled "$F_c$".

Never draw $F_c$ as a separate force on an FBD! Centripetal force is not an autonomous force category like gravity or friction. It is the label given to the net vector resultant of real physical forces acting inward along the radius:

$$\Sigma \vec{F}_{\text{radial}} = F_c = \frac{m v^2}{r}$$

2. Real Physical Providers of Centripetal Force

Different real physical interactions supply the necessary net radial force depending on the scenario:

Physical Context Real Physical Force Provider(s) Radial Force Balance Equation
Car Turning on Flat Road Static friction ($f_s$) between tires and road. $$f_s = \frac{m v^2}{r} \le \mu_s m g \implies v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}$$
Mass Whirled on String Inward tension ($T$) in string. $$T = \frac{m v^2}{r} = m \omega^2 r$$
Satellite Orbiting Earth Newtonian Universal Gravitational attraction ($F_g$). $$\frac{G M m}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r} \implies v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$$
Car Traversing Banked Curve Horizontal component of Normal force ($N \sin\theta$). $$N \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r} \quad \text{and} \quad N \cos\theta = m g$$
Vertical Coaster Loop (Bottom) Upward Normal force ($N$) minus downward Weight ($m g$). $$N - m g = \frac{m v^2}{r} \implies N = m\left(g + \frac{v^2}{r}\right)$$

3. Inertial Reference Frames & The "Centrifugal Force" Myth

When a turning car swerves sharply left, passengers feel pushed rightward against the car door. Laypeople describe this as "centrifugal force". In physics, this is a **fictitious force** arising from observing motion in an accelerating reference frame.

Inertia vs Centripetal Force Breakdown

1. Inertial Frame Analysis (Ground Observer): By Newton's First Law, your body possesses linear inertia and tries to keep moving in a straight line at constant velocity. As the car turns left underneath you, the right door turns into your straight path and pushes **inward** against you. That inward Normal force is the real centripetal force making you turn!

2. Non-Inertial Frame (Inside Car): Inside the accelerating car, an occupant perceives an artificial outward push. **Centrifugal force does not exist in inertial frames of reference!**

Four Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 (Easy): Flat Road Cornering & Static Friction Limit

[Core Friction Application]

A $1200\text{ kg}$ sedan traverses a flat, unbanked circular curve of radius $r = 45.0\text{ m}$. The coefficient of static friction between tire rubber and dry asphalt is $\mu_s = 0.650$. Calculate:

a) The maximum static friction force $f_{s,\text{max}}$ available.
b) The maximum safe cornering speed $v_{\text{max}}$ in $\text{m/s}$ and $\text{km/h}$ before skidding occurs.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Maximum Static Friction Force ($f_{s,\text{max}}$):

On a flat horizontal road, normal reaction force equals weight ($N = m g$). Static friction supplies centripetal force:

$$f_{s,\text{max}} = \mu_s N = \mu_s m g$$ $$f_{s,\text{max}} = 0.650 \times 1200 \times 9.80 = \mathbf{7644\text{ N}}$$

Part b) Maximum Safe Cornering Speed ($v_{\text{max}}$):

Equating static friction to centripetal demand:

$$\frac{m v_{\text{max}}^2}{r} = f_{s,\text{max}} \implies \frac{m v_{\text{max}}^2}{r} = \mu_s m g$$

Dividing both sides by mass $m$ shows speed is mass-independent:

$$v_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}$$ $$v_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{0.650 \times 9.80 \times 45.0} = \sqrt{286.65} = \mathbf{16.9\text{ m/s}}$$

Converting to $\text{km/h}$:

$$v_{\text{max}} = 16.93 \times 3.6 = \mathbf{61.0\text{ km/h}}$$

Worked Example 2 (Medium): Whirling Stone Vertical String Tension

[Vertical Non-Uniform Dynamics]

A $0.500\text{ kg}$ stone tied to a string of length $r = 0.850\text{ m}$ is whirled in a vertical circle. At its lowest point (bottom), the stone's speed is $v = 8.90\text{ m/s}$. Calculate:

a) The required centripetal acceleration $a_c$ at the bottom.
b) The string tension force $T_{\text{bottom}}$ at the lowest point.
c) The minimum critical speed $v_{\text{top,min}}$ at the highest point needed to keep the string taut ($T_{\text{top}} \ge 0$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part a) Centripetal Acceleration ($a_c$):

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(8.90)^2}{0.850} = \frac{79.21}{0.850} = \mathbf{93.2\text{ m/s}^2}\quad \text{(directed radially inward)}$$

Part b) String Tension at Bottom ($T_{\text{bottom}}$):

At the bottom, upward tension $T$ opposes downward weight $m g$:

$$\Sigma F_{\text{radial}} = T_{\text{bottom}} - m g = \frac{m v^2}{r}$$ $$T_{\text{bottom}} = m \left( g + a_c \right) = 0.500 \times (9.80 + 93.188) = \mathbf{51.5\text{ N}}$$

Part c) Minimum Critical Speed at Apex ($v_{\text{top,min}}$):

At the top, both tension and weight point downward toward centre:

$$T_{\text{top}} + m g = \frac{m v^2}{r}$$

Setting boundary condition $T_{\text{top}} = 0$ for tension breakdown threshold:

$$m g = \frac{m v_{\text{top,min}}^2}{r} \implies v_{\text{top,min}} = \sqrt{g r} = \sqrt{9.80 \times 0.850} = \mathbf{2.89\text{ m/s}}$$

Worked Example 3 (Hard): Conical Pendulum Vector Decomposition

[2D Vector Equilibrium]

A $1.20\text{ kg}$ pendulum bob is suspended from a ceiling by a string of length $L = 1.50\text{ m}$. The bob sweeps out a horizontal circle of radius $r = 0.750\text{ m}$ (a conical pendulum). Calculate:

a) The angle $\theta$ the string makes with the vertical.
b) The tension $T_{\text{string}}$ in the string.
c) The linear tangential speed $v$ of the bob.

Step-by-Step Vector Solution:

Part a) String Angle $\theta$:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{r}{L} = \frac{0.750}{1.50} = 0.500 \implies \theta = \sin^{-1}(0.500) = \mathbf{30.0^\circ}$$

Part b) String Tension ($T_{\text{string}}$):

Vertical equilibrium: $T \cos\theta = m g$:

$$T_{\text{string}} = \frac{m g}{\cos(30.0^\circ)} = \frac{1.20 \times 9.80}{0.8660} = \mathbf{13.6\text{ N}}$$

Part c) Linear Speed $v$:

Horizontal component provides centripetal force ($T \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r}$):

$$\frac{T \sin\theta}{T \cos\theta} = \frac{\frac{m v^2}{r}}{m g} \implies \tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{g r}$$ $$v = \sqrt{g r \tan\theta} = \sqrt{9.80 \times 0.750 \times \tan(30.0^\circ)} = \mathbf{2.06\text{ m/s}}$$

Worked Example 4 (Extension): Space Station Artificial Gravity Design

[Rotational Engineering Analysis]

An engineer designs a rotating torus space station of outer floor radius $R = 150\text{ m}$ to generate artificial Earth-equivalent gravity ($a_c = 1.00g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$) for astronauts walking on the outer floor.

a) Calculate the required rotation period $T$ and angular velocity $\omega$.
b) Calculate the linear tangential speed $v_{\text{rim}}$ of the outer floor.
c) Calculate the centripetal acceleration experienced by the head of a $1.80\text{ m}$ tall astronaut standing upright on the floor, and evaluate the percentage difference in artificial weight across their body.

Step-by-Step Engineering Calculations:

Part a) Angular Velocity $\omega$ & Period $T$:

$$a_c = \omega^2 R \implies 9.80 = \omega^2 \times 150 \implies \omega = \mathbf{0.2556\text{ rad/s}}$$ $$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{0.2556} = \mathbf{24.6\text{ s}}$$

Part b) Outer Floor Tangential Speed ($v_{\text{rim}}$):

$$v_{\text{rim}} = \omega R = 0.2556 \times 150 = \mathbf{38.3\text{ m/s}}$$

Part c) Acceleration at Head & Gradient Evaluation:

Astronaut's feet are at $r_{\text{feet}} = 150\text{ m}$, head is at $r_{\text{head}} = 150 - 1.80 = 148.2\text{ m}$.

$$a_{\text{head}} = \omega^2 r_{\text{head}} = (0.2556)^2 \times 148.2 = \mathbf{9.682\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Percentage difference across height:

$$\text{Percentage Diff} = \frac{a_{\text{feet}} - a_{\text{head}}}{a_{\text{feet}}} \times 100\% = \frac{9.80 - 9.682}{9.80} \times 100\% = \mathbf{1.20\%}$$

Practise Question 1 (Easy): Fairground Rotor Ride Wall Friction

[3 Marks]

A fairground Rotor ride consists of a vertical hollow cylinder (radius $r = 3.20\text{ m}$) spinning rapidly about its central axis. Riders stand against the inner wall. When spinning at angular velocity $\omega$, the floor drops away, leaving riders pinned to the wall by static friction ($\mu_s = 0.400$). Calculate the minimum angular velocity $\omega_{\text{min}}$ in $\text{rad/s}$ required to prevent riders from sliding down the wall ($g = 9.80\text{ m/s}^2$).

Hint: Normal force provides centripetal force ($N = m \omega^2 r$). Static friction prevents falling ($f_s = m g \le \mu_s N$). Equate $m g = \mu_s m \omega^2 r$ and solve for $\omega$.

Vertical balance: $f_s = m g$.

Horizontal Normal force: $N = F_c = m \omega^2 r$.

Static friction threshold: $f_s \le \mu_s N \implies m g \le \mu_s (m \omega^2 r)$.

$$g = \mu_s \omega^2 r \implies \omega_{\text{min}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\mu_s r}} = \sqrt{\frac{9.80}{0.400 \times 3.20}} = \mathbf{2.77\text{ rad/s}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"For vertical balance, weight $m g$ must be supported by static friction $f_s = \mu_s N$. Inward normal force supplies centripetal force $N = m\omega^2 r$. Equating friction to weight gives $m g = \mu_s m \omega^2 r$. Cancelling mass $m$ and rearranging yields $\omega_{\text{min}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\mu_s r}} = \sqrt{\frac{9.80}{0.400 \times 3.20}} = 2.77\text{ rad/s}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Equates vertical friction force to weight ($f_s = m g$) and identifies $N = m \omega^2 r$.
  • 2 Marks: Formulates algebraic equality $g = \mu_s \omega^2 r$.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains final minimum angular velocity $2.77\text{ rad/s}$ with correct SI units.

Practise Question 2 (Medium): Roller Coaster Loop Normal Reaction Force

[4 Marks]

A $650\text{ kg}$ roller coaster car enters a circular vertical loop-the-loop of radius $R = 14.0\text{ m}$. At the lowest point (bottom) of the loop, the car's speed is $v_{\text{bottom}} = 24.0\text{ m/s}$.

a) Construct a free-body force equation at the bottom of the loop.
b) Calculate the normal reaction force $N_{\text{bottom}}$ exerted by the track on the car at the lowest point.
c) Express this normal force as an apparent $g$-force load ($N / m g$).

Hint: Normal force acts upward toward the centre and weight acts downward away from centre: $N - m g = \frac{m v^2}{R} \implies N = \frac{m v^2}{R} + m g$.

a) Free-Body Force Equation:

$$\Sigma F_{\text{radial}} = N_{\text{bottom}} - m g = \frac{m v^2}{R}$$

b) Normal Force Calculation ($N_{\text{bottom}}$):

$$N_{\text{bottom}} = \frac{650 \times (24.0)^2}{14.0} + (650 \times 9.80) = 26742.8 + 6370.0 = \mathbf{33113\text{ N}} \approx \mathbf{33.1\text{ kN}}$$

c) Apparent $g$-force Load:

$$\text{Apparent Load} = \frac{33113}{6370} = \mathbf{5.20\text{ g's}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"At the bottom of the loop, normal force acts upward toward centre while weight acts downward. Net radial force is $N - m g = \frac{m v^2}{R}$. Solving gives $N = \frac{650 \times 24.0^2}{14.0} + (650 \times 9.80) = 33.1\text{ kN}$. Dividing by weight $m g = 6370\text{ N}$ yields an apparent g-force load of $5.20\text{ g's}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Formulates correct force equation $N - m g = m v^2 / R$.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates centripetal force component $F_c = 26.7\text{ kN}$.
  • 3 Marks: Obtains total normal force $33.1\text{ kN}$.
  • 4 Marks: Calculates correct apparent $g$-force load ($5.20\text{ g's}$).

Practise Question 3 (Hard): String Breaking Strength & Tangential Fly-Off

[5 Marks]

A $0.400\text{ kg}$ sphere is whirled in a horizontal circle on a smooth tabletop using a string of length $r = 0.750\text{ m}$. The string has a maximum breaking strength $T_{\text{max}} = 85.0\text{ N}$.

a) Calculate the maximum allowable angular velocity $\omega_{\text{max}}$ in $\text{rad/s}$ before string failure occurs.
b) Calculate the linear tangential speed $v_{\text{max}}$ at the instant of snapping.
c) Describe the path taken by the sphere immediately after the string snaps, citing Newton's First Law.

Hint: Set $T_{\text{max}} = m \omega_{\text{max}}^2 r$. After snapping, zero net horizontal force acts on the sphere, so it travels in a straight line tangent to the circle.

a) Maximum Angular Velocity ($\omega_{\text{max}}$):

$$T_{\text{max}} = m \omega_{\text{max}}^2 r \implies 85.0 = 0.400 \times \omega_{\text{max}}^2 \times 0.750$$ $$\omega_{\text{max}}^2 = \frac{85.0}{0.300} = 283.33 \implies \omega_{\text{max}} = \mathbf{16.8\text{ rad/s}}$$

b) Maximum Linear Speed ($v_{\text{max}}$):

$$v_{\text{max}} = \omega_{\text{max}} r = 16.833 \times 0.750 = \mathbf{12.6\text{ m/s}}$$

c) Trajectory After Snapping:

The moment the string breaks, centripetal tension vanishes ($\Sigma F_x = 0$). By Newton's First Law, the sphere moves in a straight line at constant velocity $v = 12.6\text{ m/s}$ along a line strictly **tangent** to the circle at the break point.

Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Equating breaking tension to centripetal force $T_{\text{max}} = m\omega^2 r$ gives $85.0 = 0.400 \times 0.750 \times \omega^2$, yielding $\omega_{\text{max}} = 16.8\text{ rad/s}$. The maximum linear speed is $v = \omega r = 12.6\text{ m/s}$. When the string snaps, centripetal force drops to zero ($\Sigma F = 0$). By Newton's First Law, the sphere continues in a straight line at constant velocity $12.6\text{ m/s}$ tangent to the circle at the break location."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Equates breaking tension to $m \omega^2 r$.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates maximum angular velocity $\omega_{\text{max}} = 16.8\text{ rad/s}$.
  • 3 Marks: Calculates maximum linear speed $v_{\text{max}} = 12.6\text{ m/s}$.
  • 4 Marks: States straight-line tangential trajectory post-snap.
  • 5 Marks: Explicitly links post-snap motion to Newton's First Law ($\Sigma F = 0 \implies \text{constant velocity}$).

Practise Question 4 (Extension): Satellite Orbital Mechanics & Mass Independence

[6 Marks]

A weather satellite of mass $m = 1200\text{ kg}$ orbits Earth in a circular path at altitude $h = 700\text{ km}$ above Earth's surface ($R_E = 6371\text{ km}$, $M_E = 5.972 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$, $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$).

a) Calculate total orbital radius $r$ in metres.
b) Calculate centripetal acceleration $a_c$ of the satellite.
c) Prove algebraically that orbital acceleration $a_c$ is independent of satellite mass $m$.
d) Calculate orbital linear speed $v$ and period $T$ of the satellite.

Hint: Equate gravitational force $F_g = \frac{G M_E m}{r^2}$ to centripetal force $F_c = m a_c$. Mass $m$ cancels to leave $a_c = \frac{G M_E}{r^2}$.

a) Total Orbital Radius ($r$):

$$r = R_E + h = 6371000 + 700000 = \mathbf{7.071 \times 10^6\text{ m}}$$

b) Centripetal Acceleration ($a_c$):

$$a_c = \frac{G M_E}{r^2} = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \times (5.972 \times 10^{24})}{(7.071 \times 10^6)^2} = \mathbf{7.97\text{ m/s}^2}$$

c) Algebraic Mass Independence Proof:

$$F_g = F_c \implies \frac{G M_E m}{r^2} = m a_c \implies a_c = \frac{G M_E}{r^2}$$

Because satellite mass $m$ cancels, $a_c$ depends purely on central mass $M_E$ and radius $r$.

d) Orbital Speed $v$ & Period $T$:

$$v = \sqrt{a_c r} = \sqrt{7.9717 \times 7.071 \times 10^6} = \mathbf{7508\text{ m/s}} \approx \mathbf{7.51\text{ km/s}}$$ $$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi \times 7.071 \times 10^6}{7508} = \mathbf{5917\text{ s}} \approx \mathbf{98.6\text{ minutes}}$$
Exemplar Full-Mark Written Response:

"Total orbital radius is $r = 6371 + 700 = 7071\text{ km} = 7.071 \times 10^6\text{ m}$. Equating gravitational force to centripetal force gives $\frac{G M_E m}{r^2} = m a_c$. Cancelling satellite mass $m$ yields $a_c = \frac{G M_E}{r^2} = 7.97\text{ m/s}^2$, algebraically proving independence of satellite mass. The orbital speed is $v = \sqrt{a_c r} = 7.51\text{ km/s}$, corresponding to an orbital period $T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = 5920\text{ s} = 98.6\text{ minutes}$."

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Computes total orbital radius $r = 7.071 \times 10^6\text{ m}$.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates centripetal acceleration $a_c = 7.97\text{ m/s}^2$.
  • 3 Marks: Algebraically proves mass independence by cancelling $m$.
  • 4 Marks: Calculates orbital velocity $v = 7.51\text{ km/s}$.
  • 5 Marks: Calculates orbital period $T = 5920\text{ s}$.
  • 6 Marks: Expresses all quantities with correct SI units and 3 significant figures.
